## 1. Progressive name-on-the-board

**Verdict: unsupported.** Not "proven harmful" — the direct evidence is close to nonexistent, which is itself the finding. But every adjacent line of evidence points against the two things that define it: that it is **public**, and that it **accumulates**.

### Where it comes from

Name-on-the-board with escalating checkmarks is the signature move of **Assertive Discipline** (Lee Canter, late 1970s onward). It was enormously widely adopted and it was audited twice by the same team.

Render, Padilla & Krank published two pieces in 1989. The *Educational Leadership* version summarized the procedures and findings of **12 studies** and found "no indication that the program is any more effective than any other approach to reducing discipline problems in schools," concluding that the effectiveness claims made by Canter and other advocates "are not supported by the limited research that has been conducted in the program's 12 years of existence." The fuller *Teachers College Record* review reached the same place on the research and went further on the design: the program is "philosophically questionable and unsupported by well-designed research," and is "based primarily on teachers' needs, characterized by authoritarian values and techniques." [T3 — critical reviews; I retrieved the ERIC records and search-level summaries, not the full articles.]

Canter's published reply is titled **"Response to Render, Padilla, and Krank: But Practitioners Say It Works!"** and argues that the decision to implement a program should rest on many factors in addition to research. That is a fair position for a practitioner to hold. It is not a research defense, and the title concedes the point.

Emmer & Aussiker (1990) reviewed four widely used discipline packages including Assertive Discipline and landed in the same place from a different angle: **teachers liked the program and their own behavior was influenced by it, but "effects on student behavior are unclear."** [T3; retrieved via search summary of the review chapter, published in Moles (Ed.), *Student Discipline Strategies*, SUNY Press.]

That pattern — the adults find it satisfying, the student effect can't be demonstrated — recurs across this whole literature. It's the same shape as the behavior-specific praise finding in the main brief, where over 96% of studies measured whether we could get teachers to praise more rather than whether students changed.

### Clip charts and color systems

Essentially no peer-reviewed efficacy literature exists. I searched specifically and what came back was teacher-marketplace products, practitioner blogs, and graduate theses. The one empirical item I could locate is a master's manuscript (Poole, Abilene Christian University) on **student perceptions**, reporting that a majority of students disliked the clip chart **whether it was public or hidden**. [T4 — unpublished thesis. That is a signal about acceptability, not an effect on behavior, and it should not be cited as evidence.]

So: if someone tells you clip charts are research-based, ask for the study. There isn't one.

### The two findings that actually bear on it

**Public delivery is the part with real evidence against it.** O'Leary, Kaufman, Kass & Drabman (1970) ran a four-month reversal design across five classes with two high-disruption children each. At baseline almost all teacher reprimands were loud enough for many other children to hear. Switching to soft, private reprimands **reduced** disruptive behavior; switching back to loud produced "a consequent increase in disruptive behavior"; switching to soft again reduced it. [T2 — reversal design, small n, but a demonstrated functional relationship.]

Name-on-the-board is, by construction, the maximally public version of a reprimand. It is audible to nobody and visible to everybody, and it stays visible.

**And 11-to-13-year-olds are the worst possible audience for it.** Somerville, Jones, Ruberry, Dyke, Glover & Casey (2013), *Psychological Science*, "The Medial Prefrontal Cortex and the Emergence of Self-Conscious Emotion in Adolescence": participants viewed cues indicating a camera was off, warming up, or projecting their image to a peer, while behavioral, autonomic, and fMRI data were collected. **Merely believing a peer was watching was sufficient to induce self-conscious emotion that rose in magnitude from childhood to adolescence and partially subsided into adulthood, and autonomic arousal was uniquely heightened in adolescents.** [T2 — lab study, not a classroom study. Retrieved via the journal listing and press summaries, not the full article.]

You are putting a persistent visual marker of failure in front of the age group whose physiology is most reactive to being observed by peers. That is a mechanism argument, not a trial, and I'm labeling it as such — but it is the right mechanism to worry about.

### The "progressive" part specifically

I found **no evidence** that a graduated consequence hierarchy outperforms a flat consequence. Not negative evidence — absent evidence. Nobody appears to have tested whether check-one-check-two-check-three beats a single consistent response.

### One inference, labeled as mine

A running public tally is a device for making **incident count** salient — to you, and to the room. The Okonofua & Eberhardt result in the main brief is that bias did not appear at incident one; it appeared at incident two, mediated by whether the teacher perceived "a pattern." A board that displays accumulating marks is an instrument for manufacturing exactly that perception, and for broadcasting it. That connection is my inference from two separate findings, not something anyone has tested.

### What I'd do instead

Keep the tracking, drop the display. A tally on your clipboard or in the control panel preserves whatever consistency benefit the system gives you — and consistency is the variable that keeps mattering — while removing the public audience and the visible pattern-cue. If you want a student to know they are on their second redirect, that is a sentence delivered quietly at their desk, which is the version with the reversal-design evidence behind it.

## 2. Positive family contact

**Verdict: family contact works, and it has genuinely good RCT evidence including 6th graders. But "positive" is not the active ingredient, and one trial found the improvement-focused framing outperformed the positive framing.** This is the answer I'd least expected going in.

### The trial that includes your grade level

**Kraft, M. A., & Dougherty, S. M. (2013). "The Effect of Teacher-Family Communication on Student Engagement: Evidence from a Randomized Field Experiment." *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness*, 6(3), 199–222.** [T1 — randomized field experiment. Retrieved via search summaries and the Harvard/Annenberg listings; I did not read the full PDF, so treat the percentages as abstract-level.]

Design: **6th and 9th grade students** in a mandatory summer school program, randomly assigned to receive a **daily phone call home plus a text or written message**. Teachers used a conversation protocol that started with academic progress and classroom behavior, then flagged upcoming homework and tests.

Found: teacher-family communication **increased the odds a student completed homework by 42%**, **decreased instances in which teachers had to redirect students' attention by 25%**, and increased class participation (reported as roughly 15%). The authors name three mechanisms: stronger teacher-student relationships, expanded parental involvement, and increased student motivation.

That redirection number is the one to notice — it is the same outcome you asked about, measured directly, in a sample that includes 6th graders.

### The trial with the uncomfortable twist

**Kraft, M. A., & Rogers, T. (2015). "The underutilized potential of teacher-to-parent communication: Evidence from a field experiment." *Economics of Education Review*.** [T1. Retrieved via search summaries and the Annenberg/Harvard listings.]

Design: **weekly one-sentence individualized messages** from teachers to parents of high school students in a credit recovery program. Three arms — messages with **positive** information about performance, messages about **how the student could improve**, and a **control** with no messages.

Found: messages **decreased the percentage of students who failed to earn course credit from 15.8% to 9.3% — a 41% reduction**, driven primarily by preventing dropout rather than by reducing failure or dismissal rates. And the part that matters for your question: the intervention shaped the content of parent-child conversations, with **messages emphasizing what students could improve, versus what students were doing well, producing the largest effects.**

So the honest answer: frequent, specific, individualized contact is the lever. Positive framing was not harmful and it was not the strongest. If you have one sentence a week per kid, the evidence says spend it on the one thing to work on, not on the compliment.

Two caveats before you overcorrect. Both studies ran in summer or credit-recovery settings with short horizons and high-stakes outcomes, which is not your February. And the relationship channel is real — it was one of Kraft & Dougherty's three named mechanisms, and in the main brief teacher-student relationship quality correlates .39 with engagement and the association is *larger* in low-SES and secondary samples. A first contact that is purely positive is defensible as relationship investment even though the improvement-framed message is what moved work completion.

### The gap in the evidence, stated plainly

**I could not find a controlled study that isolates *positive* contact from contact generally.** Kraft & Rogers is the closest thing, and it compared positive framing to improvement framing within an intervention where both arms were contacting parents weekly. The specific practice "make positive phone calls home" as its own intervention does not have its own trial in what I retrieved. Anyone who tells you it does is probably citing Kraft & Dougherty, which was daily calls covering academics and behavior — not a positivity intervention.

### The design lesson on teacher time

Kraft & Dougherty used **daily phone calls** in a summer program. That is not runnable across five sections in a school year and you should not try. Kraft & Rogers used **one sentence, once a week**, and got a 41% reduction in credit failure. The transferable design is the second one: extremely short, individualized, high-frequency, and specific enough that a parent can act on it that evening.

For a "checked out" kid specifically, the attendance-nudge literature is adjacent and worth knowing — Rogers & Feller found that mailings correcting parents' beliefs about their child's absences reduced absenteeism. [I retrieved the citation listing only, not the design or magnitude — treat as **unverified** until pulled.]

## 3. Your send-out pattern

Three things, in order: what the literature says about the pattern you're describing, what your recollection can and can't tell you, and the finding that will keep you from wasting the next six months.

### The pattern you described is the best-documented disparity in the field, and it is documented specifically for Black girls

This is the less-covered half of the discipline literature — most of the public conversation is about Black boys — and the numbers for girls are stark.

**U.S. Government Accountability Office (September 2024). *K-12 Education: Nationally, Black Girls Receive More Frequent and More Severe Discipline in School than Other Girls.* GAO-24-106787.** [T1 — analysis of Department of Education civil rights data, school year 2017–18, plus a national infraction-level dataset and regression analyses. I retrieved the report's Highlights and table of contents directly.]

Verbatim findings:

- **"In school year 2017–18, Black girls comprised 15 percent of all girls in public schools but received almost half of suspensions and expulsions."**
- **"Black girls received harsher punishments than White girls even when the infractions that prompted disciplinary action were similar. For example, Black girls had higher rates of exclusionary discipline compared to White girls for similar behaviors such as defiance, disrespect, and disruption."**
- **"The data also show that in every state in the U.S., Black girls are disciplined at higher rates."** When they also had a disability, the gap grew larger.
- On mechanism, GAO's review of empirical studies identified **adultification** — "a form of racial and gender bias in which adults view Black girls as older and more promiscuous than their same-age peers" — and **colorism**, bias against those with darker skin. Stakeholders noted that "many teachers encourage girls to uphold a quiet, docile form of femininity and 'to act like ladies.'"
- And the student-side data: Black girls reported feeling less safe than other girls, **more frequently disagreed that teachers at their schools treat students with respect**, and more frequently felt that school rules were unfair.

Note which infraction categories carry it: **defiance, disrespect, disruption.** Those are the subjective categories — the same ones that Girvan et al. found explain the vast majority of variance in total disproportionality across 1.15 million students. This is not a separate finding from the main brief; it is the same finding with gender added.

**Epstein, R., Blake, J. J., & González, T. (2017). *Girlhood Interrupted: The Erasure of Black Girls' Childhood.* Georgetown Law Center on Poverty and Inequality.** [T2 — survey experiment. I retrieved the report PDF; the design details below come from the report and its summaries.] The adultification study, and the first data of its kind: **adults view Black girls as less innocent and more adult-like than their white peers, especially in the age range of 5–14.** Adults judged Black girls as young as 5 to need less protection and nurturing than white peers. Your students sit in the middle of that band.

**Morris, E. W. (2007). "'Ladies' or 'Loudies'? Perceptions and Experiences of Black Girls in Classrooms." *Youth & Society*, 38(4), 490–515.** [T3 — classroom ethnography. Retrieved via the university listing and coverage of the article, not the full text.] This is the one I'd read first, because the setting is almost exactly yours: **a Texas middle school that was roughly 45% African-American.** Morris found the Black girls performed well academically but educators persistently questioned their manners and behavior — **disciplining them for assertive behavior interpreted as loud and overbearing, while the same descriptor was not applied to other groups of girls in the school.** Teachers encouraged a "docile form of femininity, emblematized in the prescription to act like 'ladies,'" while viewing these girls' existing femininity as "coarse and overly assertive," leading one teacher to call them "loudies."

**Annamma, S. A., Anyon, Y., Joseph, N. M., Farrar, J., Greer, E., Downing, B., & Simmons, J. (2019). "Black Girls and School Discipline: The Complexities of Being Overrepresented and Understudied." *Urban Education*, 54(2), 211–242.** [T2/T3 — mixed methods on Denver district administrative data. I retrieved the ERIC record and abstract only.] Examined racial group differences in **office referral reasons**, ran multivariate analysis to test whether patterns held after accounting for other identity markers, and applied Critical Discourse Analysis specifically to **whether office referrals for Black girls were for subjective or objective behaviors.** The design is aimed exactly at the question you're asking about your own log.

**Blake, J. J., Jackson, L., Ruffin, N., Salter, P., Li, H., Banks, C., & Solari Williams, K. (2022). "Black Girls and School Discipline: The Role of Teacher's Race, Pubertal Development, and Discipline Philosophy on Discipline Decisions." *Journal of Emotional and Behavioral Disorders*.** [T2 — experimental design with **515 practicing educators**. Retrieved via the journal listing and ERIC record; I did not read the article.] Finding: **teacher's racial/ethnic background and discipline philosophy were the most salient predictors of discipline severity.** Which is to say the variance sat in the adults, not in the described student behavior.

**So: your read is well supported.** The literature's account of this pattern is not "these students behave worse." It is that adults systematically read assertiveness in Black girls as defiance, and that the reading happens in the categories where judgment has the most room — which is precisely where you'd expect a well-intentioned teacher to be affected without noticing.

### What your recollection can and can't tell you

Two things, and they cut in both directions.

Recollection is a biased sample of itself. The send-outs you remember are the ones that were memorable — emotionally charged, conflictual, or recent — and there is no reason to think that sample is representative of your actual distribution. Your recollection could be overstating the pattern. It could also be understating it, because the routine ones don't stick.

And a single teacher's annual send-out count is a small number. If you sent out fifteen students last year, the difference between "disproportionate" and "chance" is a handful of cases. I looked for methodological guidance on interpreting individual-teacher referral counts and **could not find any** — this appears to be a genuine gap, which means nobody can hand you a threshold.

The conclusion is not "so don't worry about it." It's **count it instead of recalling it.** The count tells you magnitude and location; the recollection only told you where to look. Both are useful and they are not the same thing.

### The finding that will save you six months

Here is the study you need before you build anything:

**McIntosh, K., Smolkowski, K., Gion, C. M., Witherspoon, L., Bastable, E., & Girvan, E. J. (2020). "Awareness Is Not Enough: A Double-Blind Randomized Controlled Trial of the Effects of Providing Discipline Disproportionality Data Reports to School Administrators." *Educational Researcher*, 49(7), 533–537.** [T1 — double-blind RCT. Abstract retrieved verbatim.]

Design: **35 schools**, half randomly assigned to receive **monthly disciplinary equity reports**, half to receive monthly **general** discipline reports. Outcomes: report viewing, disciplinary equity, and whether equity made it into school improvement plan goals.

Result, verbatim: **"Schools receiving the equity reports had significantly increased rates of viewing equity reports but no meaningful change in disciplinary equity or equity goal setting."**

They looked at the data. Nothing changed.

That is the most important sentence in this addendum. **A referral log is necessary and it is not sufficient.** Seeing your own numbers is the diagnostic step, not the intervention. If you build the log and stop there, the best available trial predicts you will have a well-maintained spreadsheet and the same distribution.

What did move it, in the same research group's later work, was an actual intervention rather than data alone: an equity-focused PBIS approach that produced decreases in racial disparities and in office referral rates for Black students while control schools showed minimal change. [Reported as McIntosh et al., 2021, *School Psychology*, 36(6), 433–444. **I retrieved this only via search summary and an ERIC-hosted brief, not the article — treat the design and magnitude as unverified until pulled.**]

And the two teacher-level levers already in the main brief remain the best-evidenced things available to one person: the coaching RCT that **eliminated the within-classroom referral gap**, mediated by increased skill at engaging students in high-level analysis and inquiry; and the empathic-mindset replication that narrowed the Black and Hispanic disparity by **45%**, entirely through in-school suspensions.

### The audit, concretely

Design it so it does two jobs: measure the distribution, and change the decision. The second is the one the "Awareness" trial says you'll otherwise skip.

**Log every send-out — and every near-miss.** The near-misses are where your decision actually lives. A referral you almost wrote and didn't is the most informative row in the table, and it will never show up in official data.

**Fields, six of them, ten seconds to fill:**

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| Date and class period | Disproportionality varied substantially **by time of day** in the 1,666-school analysis. Your vulnerable window is probably specific. |
| Student | So you can see the repeat margin, which is where the national gap widens most |
| Race and gender | Both, together. The interaction is the thing — this is the whole point |
| Infraction, in your own words | So you can code it later without contaminating it now |
| **Subjective or objective** | Subjective referrals carry the vast majority of the disproportionality variance |
| First incident today, or repeat | Bias appeared at incident two, not incident one |
| Sent out, or near-miss | The decision, not just the outcome |

**Then compare against your roster, per section.** Not against the school. Your fourth period is a different population from your first, and a school-level comparison will hide or invent a pattern.

**Read the subjective column separately.** If your objective referrals are proportionate and your subjective ones aren't, that is the specific, actionable version of the finding — and it's the one every large dataset predicts.

**And pair it with a decision rule, from day one.** The written send-out threshold from the main brief, plus the incident-two question — *am I responding to this incident, or to a story I've started telling about this kid?* The log tells you whether the rule is working. The rule is what does the work.

**Timeline:** don't conclude from three weeks, and don't wait for a year. A quarter of near-miss-inclusive data across five sections is enough to see whether there's a pattern worth acting on, and short enough that you can still act on it this year.

- **Render, Padilla & Krank (1989), both papers** — retrieved ERIC records and search-level summaries; not the full articles. The "12 studies" count and the quoted conclusions come from those summaries. The claim I have heard that the underlying evidence base was largely unpublished dissertations and vendor-supplied data is **not verified** and does not appear above.
- **Emmer & Aussiker (1990)** — search summary only.
- **Somerville et al. (2013)** — journal listing and press summaries; not the article.
- **Kraft & Dougherty (2013) and Kraft & Rogers (2015)** — search summaries and institutional listings. The percentages (42%, 25%, 15%, 15.8% to 9.3%, 41%) are abstract-level and consistent across multiple independent summaries, but I did not read either PDF. Pull them before you quote numbers in a formal setting.
- **Morris (2007), Annamma et al. (2019), Blake et al. (2022), Girlhood Interrupted design details** — abstracts, ERIC records, and coverage; not full texts.
- **McIntosh et al. (2021) equity-focused PBIS RCT** — search summary only. This is the one positive-intervention citation in the addendum and it is the least verified. Get it before you rely on it.
- **Rogers & Feller attendance mailings** — citation listing only, no design or magnitude.
- **Fully verified verbatim:** the GAO-24-106787 Highlights, and the "Awareness Is Not Enough" abstract.
- **No efficacy trial exists** — as far as I can retrieve — for a teacher-level referral self-audit. The protocol above is built from the disproportionality-measurement literature plus the one trial showing that data alone doesn't move it. It is a reasonable design, not a proven one, and I'd rather you know that than not.
