# Behavior Redirection — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Compiled:** 2026-07-29
**Scope:** What to do and say in the moment a student goes off-task, and which behavior frameworks hold up. Covers micro-moves (correction form, praise ratios, precorrection, active supervision, OTR, choice, behavioral momentum), whole-class prevention systems, restorative practices, exclusionary discipline and its tradeoffs, bias at the referral decision, and function-based thinking. Deliberately left out: severe/dangerous behavior requiring a crisis plan, special education procedural requirements, and anything that needs a co-teacher or an administrator's budget.

**Evidence at a glance:** ~22 Tier 1 · ~20 Tier 2 · ~15 Tier 3/4 sources retrieved. The evidence is **strong** on prevention architecture (group contingencies, CW-FIT, assigned seating, response cards, function-based intervention) and on the *costs* of exclusion. It is **surprisingly thin** on the exact micro-moves teachers are trained in — most of the famous numbers (5:1, 3–5 OTRs/min, planned ignoring, high-p sequences, choice-as-de-escalation) are weaker than their reputations. It is **contested and politically loaded** on restorative practices, where every RCT that randomized program adoption came back null or negative in middle grades while the largest positive study is observational.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*One page. Read before third period.*

### The short version

The best-evidenced thing you can do about behavior is not a redirection technique. It's a two-year coaching RCT in 86 secondary classrooms that **eliminated the racial referral gap inside intervention classrooms** — and the mediator was teachers getting better at *engaging students in high-level analysis and inquiry*. Not a script. The work itself. That should reorder your priorities: your OTR rate, your instructional match, and your seating chart do more for behavior than anything you say after the fact.

For what you say after the fact, the defensible core is small and boring: **walk over, say it quietly, say it in one or two sentences, name the replacement behavior, and praise the moment they comply.** Every part of that has evidence. The famous stuff layered on top of it mostly doesn't.

### The redirection ladder — what to actually do, in order

| Move | What it is | Warrant |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Precorrect** | Name the expectation *before* the moment you know is coming ("this next part is partner work — voices at a two") | Active supervision + precorrection: 7/7 studies met CEC quality indicators; one demonstration was literally in a 6th grade gen ed classroom |
| **1. Proximity + scan** | Keep moving; the correction is the walk | Degree of active supervision — not supervisor-to-student ratio — accounted for the most variance in problem behavior |
| **2. Quiet, brief, specific** | Get close. 1–2 sentences. Name the behavior and the replacement. Leave. | O'Leary et al. (1970) reversal design: switching from loud to soft reprimands *reduced* disruption; switching back *increased* it; switching again reduced it |
| **3. Directive + praise on compliance** | Clear one-step direction, then praise the second they do it | Matheson & Shriver: compliance rose with effective commands and rose **more** when praise was added contingent on compliance |
| **4. Private conversation, not an office referral** | Ask why. Solve the thing. | Okonofua et al.: the empathic-mindset condition was less likely to involve an administrator, and suspension rates dropped |
| **5. Referral** | Last, and logged | See "when to send a kid out" below |

### Where the leverage actually is

1. **Response cards / whiteboards instead of hand-raising.** In five middle school students with EBD *during math instruction*, response cards produced a between-case effect size of **1.3** on engagement. Under formal CEC criteria, response cards are the one form of "increasing opportunities to respond" that earned a rating — not OTR in general.
2. **Assigned seats.** Disruption during independent seatwork ran **more than three times higher** when 5th graders chose their own seats versus the teacher choosing. Rows for independent work; clusters only for collaborative tasks, and the disruptive students benefit most from rows.
3. **Raise your praise-to-reprimand ratio — continuously, not to a target.** In **28 middle school classrooms**, as the ratio rose, on-task behavior improved class-wide, and on-task behavior *and grades* improved for students at risk for EBD.
4. **CW-FIT.** WWC rates student behavior outcomes **positive with "strong evidence," ES 1.38**. Tested in grades 6–9. Teacher and implementation materials are **free** at cwfit.ku.edu.

### The four things to stop

- **Chasing 5:1.** No study found a threshold. The relationship is a straight line; the review that examined it concluded there is "a lack of empirical support for any specific ratio" and argued against using one.
- **Offering choice to a kid who's already off-task.** The 2026 meta-analysis (34 studies, 170 cases) found instructional choice significantly increased *desired* behavior (LRR = .186) but did **not** significantly reduce challenging behavior. Choice is a front-end engagement move.
- **Planned ignoring on its own.** It has never been validated standalone. It's function-dependent and fails for peer-attention and escape-maintained behavior, which is most of 6th grade.
- **Individual token economies.** A systematic review of 24 studies concluded the evidence "does not provide sufficient evidence to be deemed best-practice" under WWC criteria. Group contingencies *do* clear that bar. If you want a points system, make it a group contingency.

### The bias checkpoint — one thought, at one moment

Racial disparity in discipline originates **at the classroom referral**, and it lives almost entirely in **subjective categories** — disrespect, excessive noise, loitering, "attitude" — not in the documentable ones. Across 1,154,686 students, disproportionality in subjective referrals explained the vast majority of variance in total disproportionality. And the mechanism is specific: in randomized experiments with real teachers, there was **no race difference after the first infraction**. The difference appeared at the **second**, mediated by whether the teacher perceived "a pattern."

So the checkpoint is one question, at incident #2: *am I responding to this incident, or to a story I've started telling about this kid?*

### When to send a kid out

Honestly: rarely, and consistently. The evidence cuts both ways and you should know both halves.

- A suspension reduces the suspended student's math and reading achievement (~0.05 SD), and peer spillover benefits are "very small."
- Attending a middle school one SD stricter raises dropout 15%, arrest 17%, incarceration 20% — with the largest effects for male and minority students.
- **But:** principals more likely to remove students genuinely *do* reduce reported minor misconduct. And one additional disruptive classmate causally lowers peers' achievement and their earnings at 24–28 by 3–4%. Absorbing disruption is not a neutral choice either.
- **The finding that resolves it:** in Philadelphia, schools that *fully* complied with a suspension ban saw no peer harm. Schools that *partially* complied saw peer math fall 0.06 SD and unexcused absences rise 8%. **Half-following a discipline policy is worse for bystanders than either following it or not having it.** Pick a threshold you will actually hold every single time.

### Watch out for

- **Restorative practices in middle grades is the weakest link in the whole framework.** The 44-school RCT found suspension reductions concentrated in *elementary* — none in middle grades — plus a significant negative math estimate (grades 3–8, ES −0.068) and students rating their teachers' classroom management significantly *worse* (ES −0.21) while teachers rated climate better. Two other middle-school RCTs found nothing. Use restorative *conversations* with individual kids; don't treat the framework as a substitute for an enforced floor.
- **Three frameworks you'll be sold that have essentially no school evidence:** trauma-informed practice (a full Campbell systematic review found **zero studies** meeting inclusion criteria), Collaborative & Proactive Solutions in classrooms (two real RCTs, both clinical and parent-delivered; the school evidence is two uncontrolled pilots with *teacher stress* as the headline outcome), and de-escalation training (the definitive review states no model has demonstrated effectiveness in a sufficiently rigorous evaluation).
- **The Colvin & Sugai escalation cycle** — calm, trigger, agitation, acceleration, peak, de-escalation, recovery — is a practitioner heuristic with no validation study I could find. Useful mental map; not evidence.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. The frame: prevention is the intervention

The strongest classroom-level result in this entire brief is not a behavior technique.

Gregory, Hafen, Ruzek, Mikami, Allen & Pianta (2016) randomized **86 secondary classrooms** (44 treatment / 42 control, 1,195 students) to My Teaching Partner–Secondary, a two-year video-coaching program [T1]. Outcome: whether a student received at least one disciplinary referral from that teacher. In control classrooms the racial referral gap persisted (student race predicted referral, b = .04, p < .01). In intervention classrooms **there was no significant Black/non-Black referral disparity** (race × intervention b = −.06, 95% CI [−.107, −.032], p < .01). It replicated Year 1 findings and **maintained the year after coaching was withdrawn**.

The mediator is what makes this brief-defining: Black students had a low probability of referral specifically with teachers who **increased their skill at engaging students in high-level analysis and inquiry**. Not with teachers who got better at redirection.

That finding is corroborated from an unexpected direction. Leflot, van Lier, Onghena & Colpin (2010) followed **570 Belgian children from 2nd to 3rd grade** through a Good Behavior Game intervention with observational mediation analysis [T2]. The active ingredient they identified was **the reduction in teachers' negative remarks** — that predicted increased on-task behavior and decreased talking-out, and those classroom changes in turn *mediated* the intervention's effect on hyperactive and oppositional behavior development. The game apparatus wasn't the mechanism. The teacher talking less negatively was.

And from a third direction: Zoromski, Evans, Owens, Holdaway & Royo Romero (2021) observed middle school classrooms and found wide variability in strategy use, "low rates of appropriate responses to disruptive behavior," and — critically — that **appropriate responses to disruptive behavior had the strongest association with total rates of disruptive and on-task behavior** [T3, correlational]. Stronger than praise rate. Stronger than OTR rate.

Read together: the *quality of the instructional demand* and the *quality of your response to violations* dominate, and most teachers are weak on the second.

**Where instructional match fits — and where it's contested.** The classic claim is that off-task behavior spikes when work is at frustration level. Treptow, Burns & McComas (2007) found time on task highest at the **instructional** level (93–97% known words), lower at frustration *and* lower at independent [T3, n=3]. Center, Deitz & Kaufman (1982) found that in 15 children with behavior disorders, a math-level mismatch increased inappropriate behavior **both with and without a reinforcement contingency on accuracy** — you cannot incentivize your way out of a bad match [T3]. Umbreit, Lane & Dejud (2004) found the mirror image: a 4th grader's disruption occurred *after* he finished, and assigning **harder** work fixed it [T3, n=1].

But the study closest to your room complicates it. Simonsen, Little & Fairbanks (2010) ran a multi-element structural analysis with **three middle school boys (two 6th grade, one 8th) in honors math classes**, randomizing easy/hard × high/low teacher attention daily [T2/T3]. **Attention, not difficulty, drove off-task behavior**: 10.00% vs 35.67% off-task intervals for one student, 3.06% vs 14.86% for another, 6.33% vs 26.83% for the third. Difficulty showed no consistent relationship. The authors flag a confound — "high attention" also meant proximity and possible direct assistance — which arguably strengthens the practical read.

**The defensible synthesis:** mismatch in *either* direction generates behavior, and a strong independent effect of teacher proximity/attention during seatwork sits on top of it. In a mixed-readiness room, the move that serves both is circulating constantly during independent work.

### 2. The in-the-moment moves, ranked by warrant

**Private, brief, specific correction — the best-evidenced micro-move.** O'Leary, Kaufman, Kass & Drabman (1970) used a reversal design across five classes, two high-disruption children each, over four months [T2]. Baseline (almost all reprimands loud enough for the class to hear) → soft reprimands → back to loud → soft again. "With the institution of the soft reprimands, the frequency of disruptive behavior declined in most of the children… the teachers were asked to return to the loud reprimand and a **consequent increase in disruptive behavior** was observed. Finally… again disruptive behavior declined." That's a demonstrated functional relationship, not a correlation.

Form matters too. Simonsen, Fairbanks, Briesch, Myers & Sugai's (2008) synthesis reports that corrections which were **brief (1–2 words)** beat longer ones (2+ phrases); **quiet or discreet** beat loud; **consistent** delivery beat inconsistent; and academic corrections that were "direct, immediate, and ended with the student emitting the correct response" were most effective [T3 for the synthesis; the primaries — Abramowitz, O'Leary & Futtersak 1988; Acker & O'Leary 1988; Barbetta et al. 1994 — were not independently retrieved]. Note their vocabulary: in this literature an "explicit reprimand" is not harsh — it means *names the behavior and states exactly what to do instead, concisely.*

**Directive plus praise on compliance.** Matheson & Shriver (2005) worked with three elementary students selected for compliance rates far below peers [T2, single-case]. "Students' rates of compliance increased with increased use of effective commands. **When verbal praise was added contingent on compliance, students' rates of compliance increased even more.** In addition, academic engagement was shown to increase as student compliance increased." The praise-after-compliance step is the part most teachers skip, and it's the part that amplified the effect. *(The underlying alpha/beta command distinction — direct, one-step, non-question vs. vague, multi-part, phrased as a question — comes largely from parent-training research and I could not retrieve primary sources; treat it as [unverified].)*

**Active supervision and precorrection.** Allen, Common, Germer, Lane, Buckman, Oakes & Menzies (2020) reviewed 7 single-case treatment-outcome studies aggregating 15 cases and **1,686 participants; all 7 met the ≥80% weighted CEC quality criterion**, and they rated active supervision a **potentially evidence-based practice** [T2]. Colvin, Sugai, Good & Lee (1997) found "the degree of active supervision — and not the supervisor to student ratio — accounted for the most variance in problem behavior," with a significant inverse relationship between supervisor-to-student interactions and problem-behavior instances [T2, as characterized in the retrieved Simonsen synthesis]. And **De Pry & Sugai (2002)** — the title alone is worth the citation: *"The effect of active supervision and pre-correction on minor behavioral incidents in a **sixth grade general education classroom**"* — found active supervision produced a classroom-wide decrease in minor behavioral incidents [T2, single-case; design details not retrieved].

**Praise-to-reprimand ratio.** The middle school study is the one to use. Caldarella, Larsen, Williams & Wills (2023) analyzed **28 U.S. middle school classrooms** within a CW-FIT trial, controlling for the intervention [T2]. "Multivariate regressions revealed a statistically significant **linear** relationship… As PRRs increased, on-task behavior of the entire class improved, on-task behavior **and grades** of students at risk for EBD increased, and disruptive behavior of students at risk for EBD decreased." The elementary companion (Caldarella et al. 2020; **19 schools, 151 classrooms, three states**) found the same linear relationship and stated explicitly: "**Although no PRR threshold (e.g. 3:1, 4:1) was found where behaviour dramatically improved**, a positive linear relationship was evident."

**Behavior-specific praise — real but thinner than advertised.** Royer, Lane, Dunlap & Ennis (2019) found **only 6 articles** met inclusion for teacher-delivered BSP with student outcomes in K–12 traditional school settings; all 6 met the 80% criterion; they concluded BSP is a **"potentially EBP"** [T2]. Ennis, Royer, Lane & Dunlap (2020) mapped the full 50-year base — 57 studies, 1,947 students — and found "**more than 96% of studies featur[ed] BSP as an outcome measure**" [T2]. That is, nearly the whole literature studies whether we can get teachers to praise more, not whether kids then change. Relatedly, Zoder-Martell et al.'s meta-analysis of 28 single-case studies reports **Tau-U = 0.85** — and that is the effect of training on **teacher** praise rates, not on student behavior. Do not let that number get recycled.

The genuinely useful finding in this cluster: Ennis, Royer, Lane & Dunlap (2020, coaching review) concluded that **coaching educators to increase BSP meets CEC guidelines to be considered an evidence-based practice** [T2]. Self-monitoring your own praise rate has better warrant than most scripts.

**Opportunities to respond — and the number that isn't real.** MacSuga-Gage & Simonsen (2015) reviewed 15 class-wide OTR studies and stated plainly: "**none of the 15 studies focused on differential effects of specific TD-OTR rates**" [T2]. The famous "3–5 per minute" is a *descriptive back-calculation from studies that worked* — Sutherland et al.'s teachers ran ~3.50/min in intervention against a **baseline of 1.68/min**; Haydon's ran ~4.50–5.00. The real actionable finding is that gap, not a threshold. (Descriptive baselines for students with EBD run **0.02–0.16 OTRs per minute**.)

Fitzgerald Leahy, Miller & Schardt (2019) quantitatively synthesized 12 studies / 78 students and found increased OTRs associated with improved behavioral outcomes across response modalities and grade levels, with modest differences by study quality [T1-adjacent]. Van Camp, Wehby, Martin, Wright & Sutherland (2020) meta-analyzed OTR and report increased OTRs are associated with improved academic outcomes and reduced disruptive behavior [T1; pooled effect size paywalled, not retrieved].

Common et al. (2020) is the nuance that should drive your practice: applying CEC criteria to 21 studies, they rated **response cards specifically** — not OTR generally — as a potentially evidence-based practice [T2]. Haydon et al. (2010) compared individual vs. choral vs. **mixed** responding and found the mixed condition produced the lowest off-task percentage, the lowest disruptive rate, and the highest active student responding [T2, n=6].

And the study built for your exact room: **Didion, Toste & Wehby (2020)** ran an A-B-A-B with **five middle school students with EBD during mathematics instruction**, replacing hand-raising with response cards [T2]. "Visual analyses reveal that response cards increased the engagement of all participants," converting to a **between-case standardized mean difference of 1.3**. They note students with EBD "have shown a marked decline particularly in mathematics performance as they move from the elementary into secondary grades."

### 3. Where the popular advice is weaker than it sounds

**The 5:1 ratio is not a validated threshold.** Sabey, Charlton & Charlton (2019) put it in writing: "Although this rule has good face validity, **there is a lack of empirical support for any specific ratio**. The authors support the idea of maintaining a high positive to negative ratio but **argue against the use of any specific ratio**" [T3]. Caldarella's two large studies found no threshold. The study most often cited *for* 5:1 — Cook et al. (2017) — had **N = 6 teachers** [T2 at best]. What it does demonstrate is worth stealing: teachers wore a device prompting on a **VI 5-minute schedule** to deliver praise, plus a self-monitoring chart, and their students showed fewer disruptive behaviors and higher academic engaged time. The mechanism is the prompt and the tally, not the number.

**Instructional choice does not de-escalate.** Royer, Lane, Cantwell & Messenger (2017) reviewed 26 experimental studies and — the same group that rated praise, precorrection, and active supervision as potentially evidence-based — classified instructional choice as **"insufficient evidence"** under CEC criteria [T2]. Wilkinson, Hancock, Majeika, Wehby & Simonsen (2026) meta-analyzed 34 studies, 89 students, 170 cases, K–12 [T1]: choice "significantly increased desired classroom behaviors (**LRR = .186, 95% CI [.042, .330]**), but **did not significantly decrease challenging behaviors** (LRR = −.468, 95% CI [−1.1, .163])." Moderator worth knowing: effects were **larger for secondary students**. So choice earns a place at the front of a task (which problem set, which representation, which order), not as your move after a kid is already off.

**High-probability request sequences are not the low-intensity tool they're sold as.** Common, Bross, Oakes, Cantwell, Lane & Germer (2019) reviewed 22 studies (2 meeting all quality indicators, 16 meeting the 80% criterion) and rated HPRS **potentially evidence-based** [T2]. Then Maag (2020) took the literature apart [T3, and open access]. He tabulates the actual high-p requests used across the reviewed studies — *"Touch your hair," "Clap your hands," "Stomp your feet," "Give me a hug," "Move your eyebrows"* — and comments: "A cursory examination of these high-p requests could lead one to believe the game of 'Simon Says' was being used." He notes the one classroom-relevant version ("get out a sheet of paper," "take out your pencil," "write your name," "begin independent seatwork") is "the exception rather than the rule," concludes "a disconnect exists between research and practice," and points out that the recommended implementation includes a token economy — "a purportedly low-intensity technique… has embedded in it an extremely high-intensity reinforcement delivery system."

His constructive distinction is the usable one: **high-p *task* sequences** (3–4 problems the student can already do at the top of the set, same instruction) are trivially implementable and are the classroom-valid form of behavioral momentum. Do that; skip the request sequences.

**Planned ignoring was never validated alone.** Madsen, Becker & Thomas (1968), the founding study, concluded: "(a) **Rules alone exerted little effect** on classroom behavior, (b) Ignoring Inappropriate Behavior and showing Approval for Appropriate Behavior (**in combination**) were very effective… and (c) **showing Approval for Appropriate Behaviors is probably the key**" [T2, two teachers, three focal children]. Simonsen et al. list the practice under the name "planned ignoring **plus** contingent praise and/or instruction of classroom rules" — the qualifier is in the practice name — and note its effectiveness "is directly related to the degree to which teacher attention is a positive reinforcer maintaining undesired behavior." For peer-attention-maintained or escape-maintained behavior, ignoring does nothing or makes it worse.

**Token economies do not clear the bar; group contingencies do.** Maggin, Chafouleas, Goddard & Johnson (2011) applied WWC single-subject standards plus meta-analysis across four effect-size types to 24 token-economy studies: "**Results indicated that the extant research on token economies does not provide sufficient evidence to be deemed best-practice based on the WWC criteria**" [T1]. The same lead author's group-contingency review (Maggin, Johnson, Chafouleas, Ruberto & Berggren, 2012) found the opposite — the literature "demonstrated sufficient rigor, evidence, and replication to label the intervention as **evidence-based**" — with the honest caveat that reporting was too thin to say "for whom and under what conditions" [T1]. The 2017 update (Maggin, Pustejovsky & Johnson) corroborated it and found group contingencies effective "**particularly for addressing challenging behavior in general education classrooms**" [T1].

Little, Akin-Little & O'Neill (2015) meta-analyzed 50 single-subject group-contingency studies: overall ES **3.41**; dependent 3.75 (n=11), independent 3.27 (n=8), interdependent 2.88 (n=35) [T1]. **Carry this caveat:** these are single-case effect-size metrics and are *not* comparable to Cohen's d benchmarks. And the apparent superiority of dependent contingencies rests on 11 studies.

**On rewards and intrinsic motivation, the two famous meta-analyses agree more than the debate suggests.** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), 128 studies [T1]: "engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (**d = −0.40, −0.36, and −0.28**)… **Positive feedback enhanced both free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31).** Tangible rewards tended to be **more detrimental for children than college students**." Cameron & Pierce (1994), 96 studies [T1]: "reward does not decrease intrinsic motivation… **verbal praise produces an increase**… **The only negative effect appears when expected tangible rewards are given to individuals simply for doing a task.** Under this condition, there is a **minimal** negative effect."

They agree on direction and mechanism and disagree on magnitude. The practice that is safe on both readings: **praise and specific positive feedback, freely; expected tangible rewards for mere participation, sparingly.**

**Greeting at the door** has an RCT with middle school teachers reporting improvements in academic engaged time and reduced disruption — and **WWC reviewed that study and rated it "Does Not Meet Evidence Standards"** [T3]. The circulating "20 percentage points" and "12 minutes per hour" figures do not appear in the retrieved abstract. Do it because it's free and humane; don't cite a number.

### 4. Restorative practices — what the RCTs actually found

This is the section a skeptical evaluator will press hardest on, so it's the one to get exactly right.

**The flagship RCT.** Augustine, Engberg, Grimm, Lee, Wang, Christianson & Joseph (2018) randomized **44 Pittsburgh public schools** (22/22, matched pairs, randomized in front of district staff) to the IIRP whole-school restorative model over two years [T1].

*What improved:* Days of instruction lost to suspension fell 0.103 days against a 0.63 baseline — a **16% reduction, ES −0.060, p < .05**. Teacher-rated classroom-conduct management rose **ES 0.308, p < .05**. Reductions concentrated among African American students, low-income students, and female students, narrowing suspension gaps.

*What did not:* **No suspension reduction in middle grades. None for male students, none for students with IEPs, none in arrests.** Suspensions for violence or weapons: not significant.

*What got worse:* **Students rated their teachers' classroom management significantly lower** — 4.1 points off a 51.9 NCE baseline, **ES 0.209, p < .01** — along with four of six other Tripod subscales and In-Class Peer Support. RAND notes the pre-existing downward trend but concludes "the relative downward trend became **more severe** rather than less severe during the PERC initiative."

*Achievement, precisely:* Combined PSSA math + reading, grades 3–8: ES −0.065, **p < .10 only** — the report itself calls this not significant at conventional levels. **Math alone, grades 3–8: ES −0.068, p < .05.** Subgroups on the combined measure: **African American −0.150 (p < .01); grade 7–8 students −0.105 (p < .05)**; students with IEPs −0.088 (p<.10); grades 1–5 +0.020 (ns).

Two corrections worth carrying, because both are usually garbled. First, the negative African American estimate is **not a student-race effect** — RAND's supplementary analysis found the effect "happened at the school level… PERC had a negative impact on achievement for **both African American and white students at schools that were predominantly attended by African American students**," and they attribute it to implementation rather than student attributes. Second, RAND flags a puzzle that breaks the simple causal story: the suspension gain was in *elementary* and the achievement loss in *middle* grades, "suggesting that reduced suspensions are not the reason that PERC reduced achievement."

**Two more middle-school RCTs, both null.** Acosta, Chinman, Ebener, Malone, Phillips & Wilks (2019): two-year cluster RCT, **2,771 students in 13 Maine middle schools** — "**The intervention did not yield significant changes in the treatment schools**" [T1]. But student self-reported *experience* with restorative practices significantly predicted better climate, connectedness, peer attachment, and social skills. That second result is correlational within the trial. Huang, Gregory & Ward-Seidel (2023): cluster RCT, **18 K–12 schools, 5,878 students** — "**no differences in the likelihood of suspension**," no variation by race, gender, or disability, but reductions for students who had *previously* been suspended [T1].

**The largest positive study is observational.** Darling-Hammond (2023, Learning Policy Institute) combined practice data from **485 middle schools** with outcomes for roughly **2 million middle school students**, tracking *student exposure* rather than program adoption [T3]. Increasing exposure across the 5th-to-6th transition improved ELA and math and reduced suspension probability, with stronger effects for Black and Latino/a students. It also found exposure was **inequitably distributed** — lower in schools serving more Black and low-income students, and lower for those students within schools. The report's own argument is that prior RCTs evaluated *programs* that failed to shift practice. That's a coherent argument. It is also the case that exposure is not randomly assigned.

**And the equity result doesn't replicate in one direction.** RAND found Black–white suspension gaps narrowed. Davison, Penner & Penner (2022), a difference-in-differences study across 2008–2017, found "students in RJ schools experienced a **profound decline in their suspension rates during the first 5 years**… However… **disciplinary outcomes for Black students were largely unchanged**… **racial disproportionality widened**" [T2]. LAUSD's interrupted time series (Hashim, Strunk & Dhaliwal, 2018) found large suspension declines while "suspension gaps between black and non-black students, and between special education and non-SPED students, **still persist**" [T2].

#### Where the research disagrees, and what it hinges on

**Programs vs. practices.** Every study that randomizes *adoption* returns null or negative in middle grades. Every study that measures *exposure* returns benefits. Advocates read the gap as implementation failure; skeptics read it as selection. No retrieved study randomizes exposure, so nothing adjudicates it — but Acosta's within-trial dose finding leans toward the advocates on the mechanism while leaving the selection problem intact.

**Whose report you believe.** Pittsburgh is the sharpest case: teachers rated climate significantly *better* (ES +0.31) while students rated classroom management significantly *worse* (ES −0.21), same schools, same years, same randomization. Any summary citing one and not the other isn't summarizing the study. Worth noting: the IES/REL Northwest (2025) practitioner handout — the only quasi-official federal product I could find — cites Augustine et al. for its positive findings and mentions **none** of the negative ones.

**My call.** Use restorative *conversations* — the one-on-one, ask-why, repair-the-thing move — as your step-4 response, because that's the component that the Okonofua work and Acosta's dose analysis both point at, and because it's the part a single teacher controls. Do **not** treat "restorative" as a substitute for an explicit, enforced behavioral floor in a 6th grade math class; the middle-grades RCT evidence is null-to-negative and the math estimate in the best trial was significantly negative.

**What would flip me:** a middle-grades RCT that randomizes *teacher-level* restorative conversation practice (not whole-school adoption) and reports math achievement. That study does not exist in what I could retrieve.

### 5. Exclusion — the genuine tradeoff

Present both halves or the argument doesn't hold.

**The costs.** Lacoe & Steinberg (2019), Philadelphia panel data with student fixed effects *and* an IV leveraging a district policy change [T2]: "**A suspension decreases math and reading achievement for suspended students**," robust to IV; their working paper gives the magnitude as approximately **0.05 SD** in both subjects. Peer spillover from suspensions for serious misconduct was "**very small**" and did not change peer absences.

Bacher-Hicks, Billings & Deming (2019) exploited a sudden 2002 boundary change in Charlotte-Mecklenburg that reassigned about half of students, comparing neighbors who ended up on opposite sides of a new line [T1-grade quasi-experiment]. Assignment to a middle school **one SD stricter**: dropout +1.7 pp (**+15%**), 4-year college −2.4 pp (**−11%**), ever arrested +3.2 pp (**+17%**), ever incarcerated +2.5 pp (**+20%**), adult arrests +25%, incarceration spells +29%. Largest for males and minority students. The authors also report "**some limited evidence of positive effects on the academic achievement of white male students**" and frame the whole thing explicitly as a tradeoff.

Perry & Morris (2014), Kentucky School Discipline Study, **16,897 students in 17 schools, grades 6–10, 38,062 observations**, student- and school-fixed-effects growth curves with placebo regressions [T1]: below-mean school suspension levels had almost no effect on non-suspended students; above the mean, and especially >1 SD above, non-suspended students' achievement declined. In **low-violence schools**, predicted reading fell from about the **54th percentile at mean suspension to the 28th at +2 SD**. The bystander damage is worst in the schools that are otherwise calm.

**The other half.** Sorensen, Bushway & Gifford (2022) linked disciplinary, education, and criminal-justice records across North Carolina middle schools 2008–2016, exploiting variation in principals' removal propensity [T1-grade]: principals more likely to remove students **do reduce reported rates of minor student misconduct**. And the bill: more juvenile justice complaints, lower graduation rates for all students in their schools, and additional attendance and test-score declines for students who committed minor infractions under a harsh principal. Principals showing racial bias widened White–Black educational gaps.

Carrell & Hoekstra (2010) and Carrell, Hoekstra & Kuka (2016) identify disruption's cost from population variation in the share of classmates from domestic-violence-involved families [T2]: "Children from troubled families **significantly decrease the reading and math test scores of their peers and increase misbehavior in the classroom.**" Long-run: exposure to **one additional disruptive student in a class of 25** throughout elementary school reduces grade 9–10 math and reading by **0.02 SD** and earnings at 24–28 by **3 to 4 percent**; disruptive peers "explain around 5 or 6 percent of the rich-poor earnings gap in adulthood." Note carefully: this identifies peer *composition*, not disciplinary *response* — it establishes that disruption is costly, not that removal fixes it.

Angrist, Pathak & Walters (2013), using admissions lotteries in Massachusetts charters [T2 for achievement, T3 for the practice link]: "**Schools that emphasize discipline and comportment raise achievement in math and ELA by 0.29σ and 0.11σ more than schools that do not.**" And the same paper, same design, on discipline outcomes: "urban charter attendance is estimated to **increase suspensions by 0.7 days in middle school** and more than a full day in high school." The authors caution these practices "are typically implemented as a package rather than in isolation." Golann & Torres's review argues the opposite reading — little evidence connecting no-excuses *disciplinary* methods to test performance, and some evidence they undermine nonacademic outcomes [T4, retrieved via search summary only].

**The finding that resolves it for one teacher.** Steinberg & Lacoe (2017/2019) studied Philadelphia's ban on out-of-school suspension for two classroom-disorder infractions, partitioning schools into **full compliers** and **partial compliers** [T2]. In full-compliance schools, "peer achievement and attendance were unaffected post-reform." In partial-compliance schools, **peer math achievement fell about 0.06 SD** — "large enough to reduce the likelihood that these students would achieve academic proficiency in math" — and **peer absences rose 0.45 days (3%), driven by a 0.78-day (8%) rise in unexcused absences**. (Baseline confound the authors are careful about: full compliers were already 0.17 SD above district peers in math, partial compliers 0.03 SD below.)

Hinze-Pifer & Sartain (2018) point the same way from Chicago, where the share of high schoolers suspended fell from 24% to 16% over four years [T2/T3]: reducing suspensions for **severe** infractions was associated with slightly higher test scores and better attendance; "**students at the highest estimated risk of suspension felt safer**"; "students at the **lowest** estimated risk saw the **largest test score gains**"; and "we do not find evidence that, overall, students' academic achievement was harmed."

**The synthesis:** the variable that determines whether reduced exclusion helps or hurts bystanders is **consistency of implementation**, not the philosophy. Pick a threshold you will hold every time and hold it.

### 6. Bias at the referral decision

**Where it lives.** Skiba, Michael, Nardo & Peterson (2002) analyzed all **11,001 middle schoolers in 19 schools** in a large urban Midwestern district [T2]. Controlling for number of office referrals substantially reduced the race difference in suspensions — "disproportionality in school suspension for African-American students can be accounted for in large measure by prior disproportionate referral of African-American students to the office." The disparity starts at the classroom door.

The discriminant analysis found **no evidence** Black students were referred for more serious or a greater variety of offenses — a *different pattern* instead. White students were referred more for **smoking, leaving without permission, vandalism, obscene language** (documentable). Black students were referred more for **disrespect, excessive noise, threat, loitering** (interpretive). Overall Wilks's Λ = .981, χ²(8) = 86.22, p < .001 — significant, and the authors are candid that it accounts for a small share of variance.

Girvan, Gion, McIntosh & Smolkowski (2017) tested this at scale: **1,154,686 students in 1,824 schools** [T2]. "Disproportionality in subjective ODRs explained the vast majority of variance in total disproportionality." Smolkowski, Girvan, McIntosh, Nese & Horner (2016), 1,666 schools and 483,686 ODRs, found disproportionality elevated for subjectively defined behaviors, **in classrooms** rather than other settings, for incidents rated more severe, and **varying substantially by time of day** [T2].

And the grade band matters: Skiba, Horner, Chung, Rausch, May & Tobin (2011), 364 schools, found African American students **2.19× (elementary) to 3.78× (middle)** as likely to be referred, and more likely than White peers to receive expulsion or out-of-school suspension **for the same or similar behavior** [T2]. Middle school is where the multiplier peaks.

**The mechanism, and the moment it happens.** Okonofua & Eberhardt (2015) ran two randomized experiments with practicing teachers responding to real office-referral records, race manipulated by name only [T2]. Study 1 (n=53): **no race effect after the first infraction** on feeling troubled (t < 0.01, p = .999) or discipline severity (p = .686). After the **second**: troubled M = 5.53 vs 4.44, p = .007, d = 0.9; severity M = 5.13 vs 3.93, p = .003, d = 0.89. Within-student escalation from first to second infraction happened for the Black student (d = 1.9, 2.10) and **not** for the White student. Study 2 (n=191, better powered) replicated with smaller effects (d ≈ 0.3–0.5) and identified the mediator: **pattern perception** (3.78 vs 3.45, p = .008) independently mediated both discipline severity and imagined future suspension. Their check against national OCR data: Black students were 16.7% of enrollment, 33.3% of students suspended once, and **44.3% of students suspended multiple times** — the disparity widens exactly at the repeat margin the lab result predicts.

Effect sizes shrank markedly from the small study to the larger one, the classic small-sample inflation pattern; trust the Study 2 numbers. And these are judgments about paper records, not real referrals.

**The intervention — and its honest attenuation.** Okonofua, Paunesku & Walton (2016, PNAS) ran a placebo-controlled randomized field experiment with **31 middle school math teachers across 5 California schools and 1,682 students** [T1]. One 45-minute empathic-mindset module plus a 25-minute booster. Year-long official suspension records: **4.6% treatment vs 9.8% control, OR = 0.42, p = .001**. Previously suspended students: **51.2% → 29.4%**. *(The paper's own abstract prints 9.6%/4.8% while Results print 9.8%/4.6% — a small internal inconsistency.)*

Okonofua, Goyer, Lindsay, Haugabrook & Walton (2022, *Science Advances*) ran the preregistered replication: **66 randomized middle school math teachers, 5,822 students**, far more diverse (57.8% White, 16.5% Black, 14.7% Hispanic) [T1]. **The preregistered main effect did not reach significance in the clean randomized sample** — 2.4 pp, b = 0.024, 95% CI (−0.007 to 0.055), **P = 0.130 two-tailed**. The effect was **entirely on in-school suspension** (9.4% vs 14.2%, P = 0.001); out-of-school suspension showed nothing (P = 0.553). What did replicate, and was preregistered: **for Black and Hispanic students, suspension fell 20.8% vs 26.5% (P = 0.005)**, with no effect for other students, narrowing the disparity from 10.6 pp to 5.9 pp — a **45% reduction** (interaction P = 0.011). A teacher-level analysis the following year found nothing (P = 0.754).

That is a real attenuation from "cuts suspension rates in half," and any use of this literature should say so. The defensible claim is narrower and still valuable: an empathic framing changes whether *you* escalate a lower-stakes exclusion for a student of color.

**The one practice with better evidence than any of this:** Gregory et al. (2016), above — instructional coaching that eliminated the within-classroom referral gap, mediated by high-level analysis and inquiry.

**What's not established:** the "vulnerable decision point / neutralizing routine" recommendation — a pre-committed if–then delay before acting on a subjective call — is well-motivated by Smolkowski et al.'s data, but **I could not retrieve any experimental test showing it reduces referrals or disproportionality**. Treat it as theory-motivated. Same for the finding that a small share of teachers issue most referrals and roughly double district racial gaps (Liu, Penner & Gao, 2023) — [unverified, retrieval blocked].

### 7. Prevention architecture one teacher can run

**CW-FIT is the best-evidenced package, and it's free.** The WWC (2023) intervention report identified 30 studies, 8 meeting standards (2 group designs, 6 single-case), 759 students PreK–grade 10, 67% FRPL [T1]. **Student behavior: positive effects, "strong evidence," summary ES 1.38, improvement index +42, p < .01.** Component studies: Wills et al. (2016) cluster RCT, 313 students, 17 schools — disruptive behavior 7.01% vs 15.72% (ES 1.00), on-task 85.20% vs 65.85% (ES 1.42). Wills et al. (2018) RCT, 324 students, 21 schools — on-task 80.00% vs 58.80% (ES 1.38).

Directly in your band: **Caldarella, Wills, Anderson & Williams (2019)**, ABAB withdrawal with **234 students in grades 6–8** across nine middle level classes including 23 screened at risk for EBD, **rated by WWC as "Meets Evidence Standards without Reservations"** [T2] — significant improvement in on-task behavior at group and individual level, increased praise-to-reprimand ratios, decreased target-student disruptions.

Two honest caveats. Teacher-practice outcomes were rated only "promising evidence," and **reprimand reductions were not statistically significant in either RCT** (ES 0.06, p=0.69; ES 0.11, p=0.49) — CW-FIT reliably raises your praise, not reliably lowers your correcting. And a companion middle school study (Wills et al., 2019) was rated by WWC as **"Does Not Meet Evidence Standards"** — cite Caldarella 2019 instead. Materials at cwfit.ku.edu; coach training packages start at $6,000, which you don't need.

**Assigned seating.** Bicard, Ervin, Bicard & Baylot-Casey (2012) crossed teacher-selected vs. student-selected seats with group vs. individual arrangements for fifth graders during independent seatwork [T2/T3]: "Disruptive behavior during group seating occurred at **twice the rate** when students chose their seats than when the teacher chose. During individual seating, disruptive behavior occurred **more than three times as often** when the students chose their seats." Wannarka & Ruhl (2008) synthesized 8 studies [T2]: "students display **higher levels of appropriate behaviour during individual tasks when they are seated in rows, with disruptive students benefiting the most**." Let the task dictate the arrangement.

**Transitions.** The Timely Transitions Game — explicit timing, publicly posted feedback, and an interdependent group contingency with **randomly selected** transitions and criteria so students can't predict which one is scored — was developed and evaluated in **a sixth-grade classroom** (Campbell & Skinner, 2004) [T3, A-B design only]. Yarrough et al. (2004) added experimental control in grade 2. The mechanism is directly portable; the evidence is weak.

**Pacing.** Carnine (1976) compared slow-rate (delay between student response and next task) vs. fast-rate presentation in an ABABAB, holding praise constant via a fixed 90-second prompt to prevent confounding [T3]: fast-rate was accompanied by lower off-task behavior for both subjects, plus more correct answering and participation. Clean design — and it is **two first-graders in reading**. "Brisk pacing" for 6th grade math is an extrapolation, not a finding.

**The Good Behavior Game, honestly.** Humphrey et al. (2018) ran the definitive modern trial for EEF: **77 English primary schools, 3,084 Year 3 pupils, two years**, EEF security rating "high," 0% school-level attrition [T1]. Result: reading **g = 0.03, 95% CI −0.08 to 0.16, p = 0.30**, zero months' progress; "no significant impact on pupils' reading, concentration problems, disruptive behaviour, or pro-social behaviour"; no impact on teacher stress, self-efficacy, or retention. **One-quarter of intervention schools stopped before the trial ended.** Teachers' reasons are instructive: setup time wasn't worth it, the game didn't fit all content, and the rule forbidding teacher interaction during the game blocked them from helping students with additional needs. The report's leading explanation for the null: "teachers in both trial arms reported frequent use of several core aspects of the GBG at baseline as part of their existing approaches," so "programme differentiation may have been too limited."

That is the most useful null result in this brief. A manualized game bolted onto a classroom that already has rules, monitoring, teams, and rewards adds nothing. Improve the baseline instead.

### 8. Function-based thinking, Tier 2, and the frameworks that aren't there yet

**FBA-based intervention is the best-evidenced version of "understand the cause."** Goh & Bambara (2012) meta-analyzed 83 studies and 145 participants in school settings [T1, single-case]: **overall median PND 88%**; reducing problem behavior 80%, increasing appropriate skills 90%, maintenance 100%. By setting: **general education classrooms 93%** (no significant differences across settings). By grade: elementary 88%, **middle school 76%**, high school 99% (no significant differences). Students with **no diagnosed disability: 90%**. The one process feature that predicted significantly larger effects was **team decision-making during intervention planning**.

Gage, Lewis & Stichter (2012) ran an HLM meta-analysis of 69 FBA studies, 146 subjects, 206 outcome graphs [T1]: **FBA-based interventions reduced problem behavior by an average of 70.5%**. The WWC (2016) intervention report rates FBA-based interventions **"potentially positive effects"** on school engagement (15 studies, 32 cases, 74% of single-case experiments positive, 0% negative) and on problem behavior (8 studies, 21 cases, 68% positive, 0% negative) [T1]. "Potentially positive," not "positive," because the base is entirely single-case — that's the ceiling of this literature.

**Check-In Check-Out — know which number applies to you.** Drevon, Hixson, Wyse & Rigney (2019) meta-analyzed 59 dependent variables across 32 studies and reported **overall g = 1.22 [1.00, 1.44]** with "considerable heterogeneity that went largely unexplained" [T1]. But Park & Blair (2020), restricting to **group designs only** (6 studies, 146 students), found **overall g = 0.42**, with grade level a significant moderator: **elementary g = 0.70 vs middle school g = 0.27** (Q = 3.95, p < .05) [T1]. Plan around 0.27.

**Three frameworks that are oversold.**

*Trauma-informed schools.* Maynard, Farina, Dell & Kelly (2019) ran a full Campbell systematic review — multi-database search, conference proceedings, reference harvesting, author emails, dual independent screening [T1]. Their finding, verbatim: "*Although we conducted a comprehensive search to find studies testing trauma-informed approaches in schools, **no studies met the inclusion criteria**.*" And: "the evidence in this area is completely lacking." It is a values framework and a staff-training market. Say so.

*Collaborative & Proactive Solutions.* Two genuine RCTs exist, both clinical and parent-delivered. Greene et al. (2004): 50 clinic-referred children with ODD randomized to CPS vs. parent training; on the primary symptom measure **Time × Group interactions were non-significant** — CPS did not beat parent training; the between-group wins were on a global clinical impression rating [T2]. Murrihy et al. (2022/23), the largest trial, **160 youth with ODD in Australian community clinics** with independent diagnostic interviews and equivalence testing: **both treatments equivalent, no between-group differences** [T2]. The school evidence is two uncontrolled studies from one consulting group — Schaubman, Stetson & Plog (2011), a self-described pilot whose headline outcome was **teacher self-reported stress**, and Stetson & Plog (2016), pre-post with no control [both T3]. CPS is a defensible clinical alternative to parent training. It is not a demonstrated classroom-management method.

*De-escalation training.* Price et al. (2024), the EDITION systematic review and feasibility trial [T1 for the review component]: "All National Health Service staff are trained in de-escalation but **there is little to no evidence supporting training's effectiveness**," and their earlier review concluded "**no model of training has demonstrated effectiveness in a sufficiently rigorous evaluation**." That's healthcare. I found **no controlled trial of de-escalation training for teachers**. The Colvin & Sugai seven-phase acting-out cycle is a book-based practitioner model; the only school evaluation I located is a dissertation with 30 volunteer teachers, no student outcomes, no control group [T4]. The *premise* — that lower-intensity precursor behaviors reliably precede severe behavior and share its function — has support from the applied-behavior-analysis precursor literature [citations located, data [unverified — retrieval blocked]].

**Relationship repair after conflict.** Duong et al. (2019) randomized 20 teachers and 190 middle school students to Establish–Maintain–Restore [T2]: relationships g = .61 [0.21, 1.02]; academically engaged time g = .81 [0.01, 1.63]; disruptive behavior g = 1.07 [0.01, 2.16]. Note both behavior CIs bottom out essentially at zero; the authors' own wording is "potential promise." The scale-up — Gaias, Cook et al. (2022), a cluster RCT with **94 teachers, 417 students, 6 high schools** — found **non-significant main effects**, targeted benefits for students starting low and for several minoritized groups, and **unexpected negative effects for high-performing and advantaged students** [T2]. That is the honest current state.

**Spillover.** How you correct one student teaches the room. The origin is Kounin & Gump's (1958) "ripple effect" [T3/T4 — I verified the citation but could not retrieve the article; the familiar claims about task-focused vs. harsh desists come from secondary summaries and are **[unverified]**]. The modern, quantified version is Perry & Morris above: heavy exclusionary discipline damages non-suspended students' achievement, and damages it most in otherwise-calm schools.

### 9. Open questions and weak spots

- **Nobody has experimentally tested the wording of a redirection.** I searched for it directly. There is no retrieved study manipulating private vs. public, question vs. command, or naming-the-behavior vs. naming-the-student and measuring student compliance or escalation. O'Leary (1970) tests volume; Matheson & Shriver tests command clarity plus praise; everything else in the "what to say" space is craft.
- **The micro-move literature is almost entirely single-case.** PND, Tau-U, and LRR are not comparable to Cohen's d, and I have flagged this wherever a number appears. An ES of 3.41 for group contingencies does not mean what an ES of 0.34 would mean in a group design.
- **Middle grades are systematically under-served.** Restorative RCTs found nothing there. CICO drops from 0.70 to 0.27. FBA drops from 88% to 76% PND. The best micro-move demonstrations are elementary. The exceptions — Caldarella's CW-FIT and PRR work, Didion's response-card study, De Pry & Sugai — are the ones I've weighted most heavily for that reason.
- **RAND's own two documents frame the same result differently.** The research brief describes days lost to suspension declining 36% in treatment vs. 18% in control; the full report gives the primary Year 2 estimate as a 0.103-day reduction on a 0.63 baseline, ES −0.060. I've used the full-report figures throughout.
- **Effect sizes shrink on replication in this literature, repeatedly.** Okonofua's suspension halving became a non-significant 2.4 pp. Two Strikes went from d ≈ 0.9 to d ≈ 0.3–0.5. Discount headline numbers accordingly.
- **Items I could not retrieve and would want before a formal defense:** Ennis et al. (2017) precorrection review findings (Project MUSE paywall — the citation is verified, the numbers are not); Roseth-style effect sizes for several reviews behind APA/SAGE paywalls; Liu, Penner & Gao (2023) on frequent referrers; Markowitz et al. (2023) on referral language; Leflot et al. (2013); Lannie & Martens (2004); the Kounin & Gump (1958) primary.
- **Marked [unverified] in this brief:** the alpha/beta command distinction; the "4–6 / 8–12 OTRs per minute" figures; the Positive Greetings "20 percentage point" figure; the Cook EMR percentage figures; the Colvin & Scott escalation-cycle validation; Golann & Torres's review content; precursor-behavior primaries. None carries a recommendation here.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

Everything here is a routine or a surface, not a lesson state.

**Warm-up window (the highest-value five minutes).** Precorrection lives here. Before the warm-up you name the one expectation that matters for today's structure — "this is silent, then partner check" — and you name it *before* anyone breaks it. This is the CEC-rated move, and De Pry & Sugai demonstrated it in a 6th grade general ed room. It costs eight seconds.

**Every independent-work block: circulate, don't sit.** Active supervision is 7-for-7 on quality indicators, and the honors-math structural analysis found teacher attention mattered more than task difficulty for 6th graders. The correction *is* the walk — you should never be delivering one from the front of the room.

**Response cards, not hands.** Replace hand-raising with whiteboards for the current-question portion of the warm-up and for any check-for-understanding. This is the CEC-rated form of OTR, and the one study of it in middle school math produced an effect size of 1.3. Mixed choral-and-individual beat either pure format.

**Seating chart, assigned, rows for independent work.** Disruption during individual seatwork ran **3× higher** when students chose their seats. Clusters only when the task is genuinely collaborative.

**A praise counter on the control panel.** Not a target — a tally. The mechanism that worked in the one study people cite for 5:1 was a **vibrating prompt on a 5-minute variable interval plus a self-monitoring chart**, and the best-evidenced praise finding in the whole literature is that self-monitoring reliably changes teacher praise rates. A silent interval timer and a click counter is the whole intervention. The direction is what matters; there is no threshold to hit.

**A referral log with two columns: subjective vs. objective.** Disproportionality lives almost entirely in the subjective categories, in classrooms, and it varies by time of day. Log the reason and the period. Once a month, look at who's in the subjective column. *(Honest caveat: nobody has run a trial showing that logging this changes teacher behavior. It's a well-motivated diagnostic, not a proven intervention.)*

**A written referral threshold, and the discipline to hold it.** Write down the two or three things that go to the office and hold that line every single time. The Philadelphia comparison is the argument: full compliers saw no peer harm; partial compliers saw peer math fall 0.06 SD and unexcused absences rise 8%. Half-following a policy is the worst option available.

**A scripted step 4.** When you're about to write a referral for something subjective, the alternative move with evidence is the private conversation — ask why, solve the thing. And the one thought at that moment: *incident #1 or incident #2?* The bias in the randomized experiments did not appear on the first infraction. It appeared on the second, through pattern perception.

**What does not belong in the OS:** an individual token economy, a bolt-on behavior game, a de-escalation flowchart, a trauma-informed poster, or a choice menu offered to a kid who's already off-task. Each of those is either unsupported or has failed in a trial larger than anything you'd run.
