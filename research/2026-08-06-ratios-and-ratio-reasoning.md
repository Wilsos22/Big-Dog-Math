# Ratios and Ratio Reasoning — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Standard(s):** 6.RP.A.1 (understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language) · 6.RP.A.3 (use ratio and rate reasoning to solve problems using tables, tape diagrams, double number lines, equations) · 6.RP.A.3a (tables of equivalent ratios, missing values, plot pairs, compare ratios)
**Compiled:** 2026-08-06
**Module map note:** In the CCSD Grade 6 Acc Teacher Clarity Guides, this is Module 2 Topic 1 — "Ratios." Percents and unit rates are *both* inside Topic 2 ("Percents, Unit Rates and Conversions"), and Topic 3 is The Statistical Process (6.SP), not percents. Your mental map of "T1/T2/T3 = ratios/percents/unit rates" does not match the district guide. This matters for pacing: T2 carries six learning progressions covering two conceptually distinct topics.

**Scope:** Covers the concept of ratio, equivalent ratios, ratio tables, double number lines, tape diagrams, comparing ratios, and the additive-vs-multiplicative reasoning problem you named. Deliberately left out: unit rate as a topic (its own brief), percent (its own brief), unit conversion, and the coordinate-graph work in CCSD progression 3.3–3.4, which is thin in the research and is genuinely grade 7's job in the CCSS Progressions.

**Evidence at a glance:** 11 Tier 1 · 8 Tier 2 · 6 Tier 3 sources retrieved this session. *(Tier definitions: **T1** = meta-analysis, RCT, or a WWC recommendation rated Strong or Moderate. **T2** = peer-reviewed study with a smaller sample or weaker design, well-designed qualitative or design-based research, or a WWC recommendation rated Minimal. **T3** = practitioner and professional consensus — NCTM, curriculum design documents, district guidance. **T4** = anecdotal; never carries an effectiveness claim.)* The evidence is **strong** on how to *deliver* ratio instruction (schema-based instruction has multi-hundred-student cluster RCTs on exactly this content) and on the *size and shape of the misconception* (large cross-sectional datasets, two continents, converging within a percentage point). It is **weak to absent** on which representation to use — there is no meta-analysis of proportional reasoning instruction, and the one WWC recommendation that addresses ratio directly is rated *Minimal Evidence*. Representation choice in this brief rests on measured student-performance asymmetries and on expert design documents, not on efficacy trials, and is labeled that way throughout.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*Read this first. About ten minutes — the first two sections alone will get you through a day.*

### The short version

A sixth grader's default move is to add. Measured on 755 students across grades 4–10, sixth graders used an **additive method on 57% of proportional problems and 58.5% of additive problems** — they are not choosing a model, they have one move, and it fires regardless of the situation (Fernández et al., 2010). The single most important thing you can do is make that move *fail visibly and early*, and the cheapest lever for that is **number choice**: on proportional problems with an integer scale factor, sixth graders reasoned proportionally 22.5% of the time; change the numbers so the ratio is non-integer and it collapses to **5.5%**. A worksheet built on doubling and halving will show you a class that looks competent and leaves the misconception fully intact.

The thing most likely to go wrong is subtler than teaching the wrong strategy. It is **teaching "ratios are multiplicative, not additive" as a rule.** In a small controlled teaching experiment (18 experimental students, 17 control) that successfully taught students to stop over-applying proportionality, the experimental group's accuracy on genuinely proportional problems *fell from 83.3% to 58.3%* — a significant drop (Van Dooren et al., 2004). Small sample, so treat it as a warning rather than a measurement. But it is a warning worth heeding, because the developmental data from a much larger sample says the same thing from the other direction: the additive error does not get fixed as students age, it **flips** into over-proportionalizing. You cannot trade one over-generalization for another. What has to be taught is the **decision** — is this situation proportional? — not the rule.

### Where to spend the time

- **Deciding whether a situation is proportional at all.** This is the sub-skill with the highest documented error rate and the only one whose absence guarantees the other two errors. Two independent datasets on two continents put the additive-on-proportional error at **57.0%** (Spain, grade 6) and **56.5%** (Australia, Year 5–6).
- **Iterating and partitioning a composed unit.** "One batch is 3 cups grape to 2 cups peach — here are two batches, here is half a batch." This is what makes a ratio a *thing* rather than two numbers, and it is the operation the whole topic sits on. It is also the accessible entry for students two years behind, because it is arithmetic they already own.
- **Reading a ratio table by rows/columns as multiples, not differences.** The CCSS Progressions names this explicitly as the table's failure mode, and the CCSD clarity guide's own success criterion 3.2.b ("determine equivalent ratios in a table by adding or subtracting two equal ratios") is one careless reading away from licensing exactly the error you are trying to kill. See "Watch out for."

**What gets compressed to pay for it:** the three-notations lesson (colon, "to", fraction bar — CCSD 1.3.a–c) collapses into vocabulary embedded in every other lesson rather than a day of its own; **and 1.3.a, writing a part-to-part ratio as a fraction, should be dropped from grade 6 entirely** — see "Watch out for." The coordinate-graph work (CCSD 3.3–3.4) gets one day as a *checking and classifying* tool, not as a solving tool; the Progressions puts its real work at 7.RP.2a, and 6th graders misread these graphs at measurable rates.

### The sequence

1. **Composed unit, physically iterated.** Build a batch, double it, halve it, triple it. *Makes possible:* holding two quantities together as one object, which is the prerequisite for every representation that follows.
2. **Comparing two ratios qualitatively before any computation.** "Which is stronger?" *Makes possible:* the relative-vs-absolute distinction — the most common error in the retrieved data (60% made an absolute comparison, Hilton et al., 2012) is upstream of additive-vs-multiplicative, because a student comparing one column has not arrived at the ratio yet.
3. **Tape diagram for same-unit ratios.** *Makes possible:* the part-part-whole structure and "5 parts → 85, so 1 part → 17."
4. **Double number line for different-unit ratios.** *Makes possible:* seeing infinitely many equivalent pairs on a continuous scale, and the unit rate sitting paired with 1.
5. **Ratio table as the bridge from build-up to multiplication.** *Makes possible:* scaling past what a diagram can hold, and the explicit multiplication/division that repeated addition cannot reach.
6. **Non-integer ratios and non-proportional situations, interleaved.** *Makes possible:* the decision. This is not an extension; it is the assessment of everything above.

### The three misconceptions to plan for

| The wrong answer you will actually see | The task that surfaces it early |
|---|---|
| "3 kg sugar for 6 kg strawberries, so **15 kg** sugar for 18 kg strawberries" (added 12 to both) | Fernández's Peter-and-Tom box task, run in both versions: 40→160 boxes (integer) and 40→100 boxes (non-integer), same day |
| "3:5 and 5:7 are the same, both differ by 2" | The Progressions' paint task — Abby 1 red : 3 yellow, Zack 3 red : 5 yellow. Ask three ways: same red, same yellow, same total |
| "They run equally fast but Ellen started later; Ellen has run 12 laps so Kim has run **24**" (multiplied when it was additive) | The *same* Peter-and-Tom stem with one clause changed to "equally fast but Tom started earlier" — same numbers, different model |

### The progression at a glance

The evidence does **not** support CRA as the spine for this topic — see Part 2 §6 for the full argument, but the short version is that the 2025 CRA meta-analysis covers 30 single-case studies and 116 students and contains **zero** ratio, proportion, or percent studies. What has the evidence is **schema-based instruction**, and what has expert-design (not experimental) support is a representational fade. Combined:

| Stage | Students do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| **Composed unit** | Build, iterate, and partition a physical or discrete batch. Cups, cubes, drawn dots, paper strips. | The student can *predict* the result of doubling before doing it, and can halve a batch without being told the parts stay in ratio. |
| **Diagram** (tape for same units, double number line for different units) | Draw the structure before computing. Mark what is known and what is asked. | The student draws unprompted on a new problem — and, critically, chooses the right diagram for the unit situation. |
| **Table** | Scale up and down with explicit ×/÷ recorded between rows. | The student reaches for a table when the numbers get big enough that the diagram is impractical, and reads rows as multiples out loud. |
| **Decision** (runs throughout, not at the end) | Sort problems by type before solving. Estimate a ballpark answer before computing. | The student can say why a non-proportional problem is not proportional, *without* losing accuracy on proportional ones. |

### If they're two years behind

The prerequisites that actually block this topic are **multiplicative comparison** ("this is 3 times as long as that," a grade 4 idea), **equivalent fractions as multiply-both-parts** (grade 4–5), and **multi-digit division fluency** (6.NS.B.2, a *required grade 6 fluency* the student is behind on and which unit rate and percent both demand).

Diagnostics, usable as your two warm-up review problems: (1) "A rope is 25 inches. Another is 10 inches. How many times as long is the first?" — an answer of *15* is a multiplicative-comparison gap, not an arithmetic slip. (2) "Fill in: 3/4 = ?/12." — an answer of *11* is the additive misconception already living in fractions, and it will reappear inside every ratio table you build.

**Just-in-time, not remediate-first.** The gap that must actually be closed before this unit is multiplicative comparison — it is conceptual and cannot be scaffolded around. The division fluency gap should be *scaffolded around*, not closed: hand out the calculator and the multiplication chart consistently, including on the check. That is a cognitive-load argument, not a lowered expectation — if the construct you are assessing is proportional reasoning, then arithmetic is a confound, and Ellington's (2003) meta-analysis of 54 studies found calculator use "did not hinder the development of mathematical skills" in any case, with the caveat that the benefit showed up when calculators were part of *both* instruction and testing.

### Watch out for

- **The CCSD guide's own success criterion 3.2.b** — "determine equivalent ratios in a table by adding or subtracting two equal ratios" — is mathematically *correct* (3:2 plus 6:4 gives 9:6) and pedagogically loaded. Students read "add" in a ratio table as "add the same number to both." If you teach it, teach it as *adding one whole batch*, never as adding a number, and never in the same week you are trying to kill the additive error.
- **Writing a part-to-part ratio in fraction notation** (CCSD 1.3.a). A retrieved study found students reading "1:2" as one-half and partitioning 10 people accordingly. The Progressions distinguishes ratios from fractions and the WWC panel names this as a common misconception in its own words: "even though ratios are often expressed as fractions, ratios and fractions are not always the same thing." Part-to-*whole* in fraction form is fine. Part-to-part is not worth the damage in grade 6.
- **Cross-multiplication does not belong in this topic.** The CCSS Progressions states flatly that "the Standards do not require this method in Grade 6." It is not a small preference — see the percents brief, where the argument is made in full, because that is where you are currently using it.
- **The graph as a solving tool.** 10% of Year 5–6 students misread a distance–time graph outright and a further 47.2% read it accurately and then chose an irrelevant answer. Use it to *classify* (straight line through the origin = proportional) and to *check*, not to solve.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. What the research supports

**The delivery model with the best evidence for this exact content is schema-based instruction, and it is not close.** [T1] Jitendra and colleagues ran a prospective cluster randomized trial with 82 teachers and classrooms, 1,999 seventh-grade students, 58 middle schools and 50 public school districts (the paper says "a state in the upper Midwest"). Both conditions taught ratio, proportion, and percent daily for six weeks — content and time were held constant, only delivery differed. Treatment teachers received two days (16 hours) of PD. The treatment effect on proportional problem solving was **0.46 SD** at posttest ("approximately 68% of treatment classrooms scored above the mean of control classrooms," in the paper's own words) and **0.32 SD** at a nine-week delayed posttest (Jitendra et al., 2015, *Journal of Educational Psychology*).

Two honest limits on that finding, both of which the paper states. First, **treatment was not a significant predictor on the GMADE**, the norm-referenced standardized measure — the effect appears on a proportional-problem-solving measure aligned to the intervention and does not transfer to a general test. A companion analysis found the same null on the end-of-year state math test (Jitendra et al., 2016, *Learning and Instruction*) [T1]. Do not promise state-test movement. Second, the intervention did not close demographic gaps: Black students scored lower on the posttest (γ30 = −1.06, p = .002) and the treatment did not moderate that.

The four components, quoted from the manuscript: "(a) activation of the mathematical structure of problems, (b) representation of information in the problem using diagrams, (c) development of procedural flexibility through selection and use of appropriate solution strategy based on the numbers in the problem, and (d) problem solving and metacognitive (e.g., monitoring) strategy instruction." The student-facing routine is six steps: recognize the problem type, represent the critical information with a diagram, connect to a previously solved problem, **estimate the answer**, select a strategy, check reasonableness.

That routine is independently ratified at the highest evidence level available. The WWC practice guide *Improving Mathematical Problem Solving in Grades 4 Through 8* rates **"assist students in monitoring and reflecting on the problem-solving process" as Strong** and **"teach students how to use visual representations" as Strong** [T1] (Woodward et al., 2012). The RtI guide rates **"instruction on solving word problems based on common underlying structures" as Strong** [T1] (Gersten et al., 2009). Ratio work in grade 6 is overwhelmingly word problems, so these apply directly.

**The critical caveat is dose, and it is a real constraint on your module plan.** The same research group's earlier, shorter versions of this intervention — "about 450–500 min (9–10 sessions of 50 min)" — produced *non-significant* results for students with mathematics difficulties. The authors attribute the difference to duration. Independently, the rational-number intervention meta-analysis found interventions of **20+ hours** produced significantly larger effects than those of 0–9 hours [T1] (Krowka et al., 2025; Schumacher et al., 2023, 28 studies, grades 3–9, all meeting WWC standards, overall g = 0.68). Ten lessons of ratio work is, on the retrieved evidence, underpowered for the students you are most worried about.

**Two instructional components survived the controls, and both are cheap.** In the published meta-regression controlling for the other instructional components plus group size, duration, and the nature of the control condition, **two** practices remained significant: **"teaching and use of accurate mathematical language" (b = 0.50, p = .001)** and **the use of number lines (b = 0.47, 95% CI [0.13, 0.82], p = .01)**, both surviving multiple-comparison correction [T1]. For a ratio unit, "accurate mathematical language" means insisting on *for every*, *per*, *the ratio of ___ to ___*, *3 parts to 2 parts* — and never letting "out of" stand in for a part-to-part ratio. The number-line finding is direct support for the double number line as a core representation, which matters because the representational evidence is otherwise thin.

*(Provenance note worth having straight if anyone checks: the pre-publication report of this meta-analysis — Schumacher et al., 2023, ERIC ED628292 — reported the number-line moderator as non-significant with controls (b = 0.43, p = .12) and called it "somewhat surprising." The finding changed in peer review. Cite the 2025 article, not the report.)*

**Two warnings from the same meta-analysis about your specific conditions.** Effects were significantly larger for grades 3–6 than grades 7–9, and larger when delivered by research personnel than by school personnel. Both gradients point down for a sixth-grade teacher running this himself. Plan against the pessimistic numbers.

**Ratio work is major work of the grade, three years running.** [T3] Student Achievement Partners designates 6.RP.A a **Major Cluster** for grade 6, recommends "at least 65% and up to approximately 85% of class time" on major work, and places 6.RP.A in the progression toward middle school algebra alongside 6.NS and 6.EE. Grade 7's major work is again ratios and proportional relationships; grade 8's is linear functions, which sits directly on top. A student who leaves sixth grade without ratio reasoning has been set up to be behind for three consecutive years. That is a design and policy argument, not an evidence claim, and should be labeled as such — but it is the sharpest emphasis argument available, because the predictive-evidence argument is missing (see §8).

### 2. Emphasis — where the leverage is

**a. The decide-whether-it's-proportional skill, on misconception density.** This is the strongest leverage argument in the brief because the error rate is enormous, converges across independent studies, and is documented to *persist*. Fernández et al. (2010) [T2], 755 Spanish students grades 4–10: additive errors on proportional problems ran **50.7% / 53.2% / 57.0%** for grades 4/5/6, peaked around grade 7, and were still at **21.2% in tenth grade**. Hilton et al. (2012) [T3], 140 Australian Year 5–6 students on a two-tier diagnostic, found **56.5%** using additive strategies in a direct-proportion situation — landing within half a percentage point of the Spanish grade-6 figure from a different instrument on a different continent.

And it runs both directions. In the same Australian data, **21.4%** applied a multiplicative strategy to an additive situation and **78.6%** failed to recognize the effect of changing two dimensions on area. Fernández's summary of the developmental arc is the sentence to remember: primary students "mostly use additive methods to additive word problems, but also (incorrectly) to proportional problems"; secondary students "switch and mostly use proportional methods to proportional problems, but also (incorrectly) to additive ones." **The error does not get fixed. It flips.** Which means an emphasis on "ratios are multiplicative" is an emphasis on manufacturing the second error.

**b. Composed-unit iteration and partitioning, on prerequisite fan-out.** [T2] The NCTM research brief on ratio and proportion names four components of conceptual understanding: attending to two quantities simultaneously; forming a multiplicative comparison; **forming a composed unit and iterating and partitioning it**; and creating a family of equivalent ratios. Every representation in the standard — table, tape diagram, double number line, graph — is a way of displaying an iterated composed unit. Nothing downstream works without it, and it is the one part of the topic a student two years behind can enter on day one using arithmetic they already have.

**c. Relative-vs-absolute comparison, on being upstream of everything.** The highest single error rate in the Hilton data was **60.0%** "making absolute comparison instead of relative comparison" — a student looking at one quantity, not two. This is not a variant of the additive error; it is *before* it. A student who has not formed the ratio at all cannot be additive or multiplicative about it. The instructional consequence is that every representation you choose must force both quantities into view simultaneously, which is an argument for the two-row table and the double number line over any single computed number.

**What gets less time, and why it is defensible.** The three-notation lesson is vocabulary, not concept, and the retrieved evidence gives one of the three notations a negative value (part-to-part in fraction form — §4). Fold it in. The coordinate-graph work is placed at 7.RP.2a in the Progressions and is measurably misread by students in this age band; one day as a classification tool is proportionate. Unit conversion (CCSD progression 4, in Topic 2) is an application of the same machinery, not new machinery, and can ride on ratio tables once they exist. Compressing these three buys roughly the three to four days that the dose findings say the decision work needs.

### 3. Sequence and why

**1. Composed unit, physically iterated and partitioned.** *Purpose:* build the object. Until "3 grape to 2 peach" is a single thing that can be doubled and halved, there is nothing for a diagram to represent. Retrieved support: the NCTM brief's third component; the Progressions' distinction between a ratio as a *composed unit / batch* (repeatable, subdividable) and as a *fixed number of parts*.

**2. Qualitative comparison before computation.** *Purpose:* force both quantities into view. "Which mix is more grapey?" has an answer a student can defend without arithmetic, which means the 60%-absolute-comparison error surfaces on day one instead of hiding behind a computation.

**3. Tape diagram, same-unit ratios only.** *Purpose:* make part-part-whole structure visible and make the total countable in parts. The Progressions is explicit: "Tape diagrams are best used when the two quantities have the same units." *Expires:* it has no zero and no continuous scale, so it cannot show that infinitely many equivalent pairs exist, and non-integer part counts sit badly in it.

**4. Double number line, different-unit ratios only.** *Purpose:* the continuous scale — "many, even infinitely many, pairs in the same ratio, including those with rational number entries. As in tables, unit rates appear paired with 1." *Expires:* on same-unit quantities, where the two lines would use different lengths to represent the same amount. **And it has a failure mode most curricula do not mention — see §4.**

**5. Ratio table.** *Purpose:* the bridge from build-up to multiplication, and the escape from what a diagram can physically hold. The WWC PD transcript names the job precisely: the table helps students "see that multiplication leads to the same solution as the buildup strategy." *Expires:* it silently accommodates additive reasoning, which is exactly why it needs the row-reading discipline.

**6. Non-integer ratios and non-proportional situations, interleaved throughout — not appended.**

#### Where the research disagrees

**Delay the algorithm, or teach it as one option?** The Progressions, the NCTM research brief, and the WWC panel all say delay cross-multiplication. The strongest *experimental* evidence — Jitendra's SBI — deliberately taught "a broad range of methods (e.g., equivalent fractions, unit rate, cross multiplication) for a given class of problems," with students selecting based on the numbers.

These conflict less than they appear. Read as a sequence they agree: build composed-unit iteration and table reasoning first, then *add* algorithms to a menu the student chooses from. The disagreement hinges on whether "procedural flexibility" is achievable before the concept is stable. **Recommendation for grade 6 ratio work: do not introduce cross-multiplication in this topic at all.** The Standards do not require it here, and Fernández's data is the reason — the Spanish curriculum introduces the rule of three in grades 5–6, and 57% of those sixth graders still solved proportional problems additively. The algorithm did not install the concept. **What would flip me:** evidence that early exposure to the algorithm *as one of several* improves later strategy selection. I did not find that study; Jitendra's design does not isolate it, because his students got the algorithm alongside everything else.

**Concrete first, or abstract first?** The concreteness-fading line (§6) supports fading over concrete-only and over abstract-only, but every retrieved experiment is on other content. One retrieved search summary referenced a study reporting that kindergarteners learning proportions transferred only from *abstract* materials absent explicit instruction — I could not retrieve it and am not resting anything on it, but it is flagged in §8 because if real it cuts against naive concrete-first.

### 4. Misconceptions and how to prepare

**Misconception: the ratio as a difference (additive reasoning in a proportional situation)**
- **Looks like:** "My grandmother uses 3 kg of sugar for 6 kg of strawberries. How much for 18 kg?" → **15 kg.** The student's stated reasoning, quoted from the source: 18 is 12 more than 6, so add 12 to the sugar.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** the previously-true rule is **build-up by adding**, which is a *correct* proportional strategy. Van Dooren, Vamvakoussi & Verschaffel are explicit that "the building-up approach is a totally appropriate way to solve a proportional problem" — the student is running a legitimate method with the wrong scope. This is why "stop adding" is the wrong correction: adding is half the right method.
- **Evidence:** Fernández et al. (2010) [T2], grade 6, 57.0% on proportional problems; Hilton et al. (2012) [T3], 56.5%.
- **Surface it early with:** the paired Peter-and-Tom task, both versions, same class period. Integer: "They started together but Tom loads faster. When Peter has loaded 40 boxes, Tom has loaded 160. If Peter has loaded 80, how many has Tom loaded?" Non-integer: "...When Peter has loaded 40, Tom has loaded 100. If Peter has loaded 60, how many?" The second version is where the additive answer appears, because build-up is no longer cheap.
- **When it shows up:** do not correct the arithmetic. Ask what one batch is, and whether that batch is still the same in their answer. The strawberry answer of 15 kg means one batch went from 3-per-6 to 15-per-18 — put both on a table and ask which row describes the grandmother's recipe. Confusion is step one; *what do you know* is that the recipe did not change.

**Misconception: proportional reasoning applied to an additive situation (the illusion of linearity)**
- **Looks like:** "Ellen and Kim run equally fast, but Ellen started later. When Ellen has run 4 laps, Kim has run 8. When Ellen has run 12, how many has Kim run?" → **24.** (Correct: 16.)
- **Why it makes sense to them:** the missing-value format itself is the trigger. Every problem in that shape for the last three weeks has been proportional, so the student is reading the *format*, not the situation.
- **Evidence:** Hilton et al. (2012) [T3], 21.4% applied a multiplicative strategy to an additive situation and 78.6% missed two-dimensional area scaling; Fernández et al. (2010) [T2], the proportional error on additive problems *rises* monotonically into secondary school. Van Dooren et al. (2018) [T2] attribute it directly to instruction: "Inappropriate proportional reasoning is, to a large extent, **instruction-induced**... the percentage drastically increases during formal instruction on proportional problems."
- **Surface it early with:** the same stem, one clause changed. "Equally fast but Tom started earlier" versus "started together but Tom loads faster." Identical numbers. The clause is the whole problem.
- **When it shows up:** this is the moment for "try something" — have them run the situation with small numbers. If they started together and Tom is faster, what happened at 1 box? If Tom started earlier, what happened at 1 box? The simulation settles it without you ruling.

**Misconception: absolute comparison in place of relative comparison**
- **Looks like:** comparing two washing powders (A: 1 kg, $4, 20 loads; B: 1.5 kg, $6.50, 30 loads) → **"A costs the least"** or **"B costs a bit more but you get 10 more loads."** Both look like reasoning. Neither forms a ratio.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** for years, "which is more?" has been answered by looking at one number. Nothing in the student's prior experience required holding two quantities at once.
- **Evidence:** Hilton et al. (2012) [T3] — **60.0%** made an absolute comparison, the highest single error rate in that dataset; 35.0% considered one value without a related value.
- **Surface it early with:** any two-attribute comparison where the answer flips depending on which attribute you look at. Design the numbers so the cheaper item is the worse buy.
- **When it shows up:** ask for the answer to a different question — "how much for one load?" — rather than correcting the comparison. The unit forces both quantities into the same sentence.

**Misconception: the colon read as a fraction bar**
- **Looks like:** "Can 10 people be divided into two groups with a ratio of 1:2?" → student treats 1:2 as one-half. Or: 1 + 2 = 3, "so put 10 people into groups of three."
- **Why it makes sense to them:** they have spent two years learning that a number over a number is a fraction, and the colon is being taught as an alternative notation *for the same thing*. CCSD success criterion 1.3.a ("I can write a ratio as a fraction") states the equivalence without the part-to-part exception.
- **Evidence:** Cheung, Reid O'Connor & Zunica [T3], n=15 pre-instruction, 26.7% success on that item; WWC panel [T2] names it directly — "even though ratios are often expressed as fractions, ratios and fractions are not always the same thing. Students often struggle with this common misconception."
- **Surface it early with:** "2 blue tiles and 3 red tiles. Write the ratio of blue to red as a fraction. Now: what fraction of the tiles are blue?" Both are legitimate questions with different answers. The collision is the lesson.
- **When it shows up:** do not resolve it by rule. Ask what the 5 is in each case. Part-to-whole has a whole; part-to-part does not.

### 5. Concrete activities

**Batch building (composed unit).** Students physically make a mix — two colors of centimeter cubes, two colors of paper squares, or two juices — to a stated ratio, then double it, halve it, and triple it, recording each. *Structure it embodies:* the composed unit as an object with an interior structure that survives scaling. *What it cannot show:* non-integer scale factors, and it gets expensive past about four batches, which is precisely the pressure that motivates the table. *Materials:* two colors of anything countable. **Free substitute:** two colors of paper torn into squares, or tally marks in two columns. *Design principle, not experimental finding:* NCTM's composed-unit/iterate/partition framing [T2]. **~20 minutes.**

**The paint comparison (relative vs absolute).** Abby's paint is 1 cup red to 3 yellow; Zack's is 3 red to 5 yellow. Are they the same shade? Students must answer three ways: make the red the same, make the yellow the same, make the total the same. *Structure:* that equivalence is a multiplicative property, checkable from three independent directions. *What it cannot show:* anything about non-integer ratios — pick this deliberately for a first day and abandon it after. *Materials:* none. **~15 minutes.** [T3] Progressions.

**Paired problems with the friendly numbers removed.** Same context, run twice: once with an integer scale factor, once without. *Structure:* that multiplicative reasoning is required, not optional. This is the highest-value fifteen minutes in the unit, and it costs nothing — it is a number change. *Evidence:* Fernández et al. (2010) [T2], grade-6 proportional-method use fell 22.5% → 5.5% when the ratio went non-integer, while additive errors rose 52.5% → 61.5%. *What it cannot show:* it does not teach anything; it *reveals*. Pair it with instruction. **~15 minutes.**

**The two-problem low-floor task.** Pose a ratio problem with a common factor, then re-pose the same context without one. Everyone gets into (a); (b) is where the range shows up, and you choose the order groups present in. *Retrieved example* [T3]: the New Zealand Ministry of Education Best Evidence Synthesis documents exactly this structure on a Year 8 ratio problem, with the second problem "high order due to the use of time as one variable and all the numbers not sharing a common factor." The page also names the real cost honestly: "The success of DMIC group work depends on the expertise of the teacher at every step of the process." That cost is your time, not money. **~30 minutes.**

**Problem sorting (schema-based instruction, adapted).** Students sort a stack of problem cards by *type* before solving any of them — ratio, proportional, not proportional. *Structure:* the decision, isolated from the computation. *Evidence:* this is component (a) of the SBI package with 0.46 SD behind it [T1], and it is the one differentiation move the comparison literature specifically endorses for low-prior-knowledge students (§7). *Materials:* index cards. **~15 minutes, repeatable weekly.**

### 6. The progression to abstract

**The evidence does not support CRA as the frame for this topic, and the reason is specific rather than philosophical.**

The most recent CRA meta-analysis [T1] (Ebner et al., 2025, *Learning Disabilities Research & Practice*) covers **30 studies, all single-case design, 116 students total**, with a researcher as the implementer in 22 of 30 (73%). I read its study table. The operations covered are: fractions, subtraction, multiplication, division, addition, place value, change making, rate of change, rounding, quadratic expressions. **There is no ratio, no proportion, no percent — not one row.** The nearest neighbor is two studies coded "rate of change," which is slope in linear functions at high school and college, and both were rated as not meeting WWC standards. The authors also report that of the 11 studies reporting SES, only two included low-SES students.

So importing CRA wholesale as the spine of a 6.RP unit for thirty Title 1 sixth graders is a triple extrapolation — new content, new delivery model (whole class rather than 1:1 with a researcher), new population. That does not make it wrong. It makes it undemonstrated, and it means it should not be the thing carrying the argument.

**What I considered instead, and what would have had to be true for each to win:**

- **Schema-based instruction.** Won. It has a cluster RCT of 1,999 students on exactly ratio, proportion, and percent — with a separate analysis of the 806 within it who had mathematics difficulties — delivered by regular classroom teachers in normal periods with no extra instructional time, plus a companion study showing first-year implementers get the same result as experienced ones [T1]. Note this is one trial, not three; see §8.
- **Worked examples.** Strong general evidence — g = 0.48 across 55 studies and 181 effect sizes [T1] (Barbieri et al., 2023) — and it costs paper. It is a *component*, not a spine, because it says nothing about which representation or which sequence. Adopt it inside the sequence. One moderator worth knowing: correct examples alone outperformed incorrect-only and correct-plus-incorrect, and **adding a self-explanation prompt to the worked example itself moderated the effect negatively.** Read that narrowly: it is about the prompt attached to the example, not about student explanation in general. The broader literature supports self-explanation as a learning activity — the same paper's reference list includes a meta-analysis finding exactly that — and the authors' own wording is that it "may not be a fruitful design modification," not that it is harmful. So: do not staple "explain your thinking" onto the worked example. Keep it everywhere else.
- **Comparing solution methods.** Would have won if the class had prior knowledge. It does not. Durkin, Star & Rittle-Johnson's synthesis of their own RCT program [T2] found comparison "particularly effective... among students with some prior knowledge of one of the methods," and that students who "did not attempt algebraic methods at pretest learned best from either **comparing problem types** or **studying examples sequentially**." For a room spanning two years behind, side-by-side method comparison on day one is actively counterproductive. Comparing problem *types* — which is SBI — is what the same literature endorses.
- **Interleaved practice.** Adopt it, immediately, as the practice structure. A preregistered cluster-randomized trial with 787 students across 54 seventh-grade classes found **61% interleaved versus 38% blocked on an unannounced test one month later, d = 0.83** [T1] (Rohrer et al., 2020). **Two limits that matter for you and are easy to miss:** the four interleaved topics were *Expression, Inequality, Graph, and Circle* — **no ratio, rate, or proportion** — and the sample was an *Honors Advanced Grade 7* course that excluded nearly all students who had failed the state math test. So this is a strong general principle about practice structure being transferred to your content and your population, not ratio-specific evidence for a Title 1 room. It still costs nothing and it is still the best bet available: never let two consecutive problems take the same strategy.
- **Concreteness fading proper.** Genuine randomized support exists — undergraduates on modular arithmetic, where the fading condition beat both concrete and generic on transfer [T1] (McNeil & Fyfe, 2012), and early-elementary students on math equivalence [T1, retrieved at abstract level] (Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas, 2015). **Neither is on ratio.** The principle transfers plausibly; the demonstration does not exist.

**And here is the honest thing about the fade.** Illustrative Mathematics' Grade 6 Unit 2 sequences discrete diagram → double number line → table, and states in its own design rationale that the double number line is "more abstract and more general than discrete diagrams" and the table "an even more abstract representation." **That is concreteness fading, built specifically for ratio, by curriculum experts, with no efficacy trial behind that sequence.** Say that plainly when someone asks. It is defensible as a design commitment and indefensible as a research claim, and the distinction will matter the first time an evaluator presses on it.

**The representational bridge — what students draw and what it must preserve.** Moving from the batch to the tape diagram, the drawing must preserve *the parts as equal units*: three boxes and two boxes, not a blob labeled 3:2. That is what makes "5 parts → 85, so 1 part → 17" available. Moving from the tape to the double number line, the drawing must preserve *proportional spacing* — the tick marks have to be evenly spaced or the model is lying, and this is the most common student drawing error.

**The double number line has a documented failure mode you will not find in most curricula, and it is the most useful thing in this section.** Küchemann, Hodgen & Brown (2014) [T2], reporting item data from the ESRC-funded ICCAMS project, measured two things:

First, **every ratio task admits two different double number lines, and one of them is usually nonsense.** For a recipe task you can put people on one line and sauce on the other (meaningful), or Ant's quantities on one line and Bea's on the other — in which case, in their words, "the lines seem to be **hybrids**, representing both people and sauce... but what does a pair like 22, 50 mean?" They note that a published booklet solved one task with a double number line and then re-solved it with a ratio table matching the *other* line, concluding "the DNL and ratio table do not correspond here." The instructional consequence: **decide out loud which of the two lines you are drawing, and say why the other is weird.** This is invisible to the teacher and fatal to the student, and it is the likeliest source of a kid whose diagram is right and whose answer is meaningless.

Second, and this is the number to design around: **the direction of the easy multiplier determines whether the lesson works.** Two versions of the same recipe item, numbers swapped so the easy ×3 relation is either *within* one measure (scalar) or *between* the two measures (functional):

- Easy relation is scalar (11 people → 33 people): **91% facility**
- Easy relation is functional (11 people → 33 ml): **51% facility**

Same mathematics. Forty-point gap. Their explanation cites Vergnaud: students are far more likely to establish a relation within a measure space than between them. On a double number line, this means students will work *along* the lines rather than *between* them. So: **never introduce a double number line on a task whose friendly relationship runs between the lines.** Put the easy multiplier along one line first. The between-lines multiplier is the destination, not the entry.

**The fade trigger.** Move a student from batch to diagram when they can *predict* the result of doubling before doing it. Move from diagram to table when they reach for scale factors the diagram cannot physically hold — and note this is a trigger the *numbers* can create on demand, which means you control it. Move to symbolic recording when the student narrates the multiplication while drawing. "After two days" is a schedule; these are triggers.

**The return path.** The signal to drop back a stage is an answer that is *the right size but the wrong relationship* — a student who gets 15 kg of sugar has scaled something, so the arithmetic is fine and the object is not. Send them back to the batch, not to more practice. Frame the return as checking the recipe rather than as a demotion; sixth graders read the second framing accurately and will stop using the material.

**Can one teacher run this in one 50-minute period with a mixed-readiness room?** Yes, and that is a real point in its favor. SBI was delivered by 82 ordinary classroom teachers in their normal periods with two days of PD, took no extra instructional time, and produced the same result for novice implementers as for experienced ones. The problem-sorting and estimate-first routines are whole-class moves. The paired-numbers task is one task with two versions. Nothing here requires a parallel lesson or a co-teacher. The genuine cost is **duration**: the retrieved dose findings say ten lessons will not do it for your struggling students, and that is a scheduling decision you have to make deliberately against a district guide that packs percents, unit rates, *and* conversions into the following topic.

### 7. Scaffolding and differentiation

**The prerequisite chain, with real diagnostic items.**

| Prerequisite | Grade | Diagnostic item | What the wrong answers tell you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multiplicative comparison | 4.OA.A.1–2 | "One rope is 25 in. Another is 10 in. How many times as long is the first?" | *15* → additive comparison; the whole topic is blocked. *2.5* → intact. *"2 and a half times"* said aloud but not written → language gap, not concept gap |
| Equivalent fractions as multiply-both-parts | 4.NF.A.1 | "3/4 = ?/12" | *11* → the additive error already lives in fractions and will reappear in every ratio table. *9* → intact |
| Multiply/divide fluency | 6.NS.B.2 (required grade 6 fluency) | "336 ÷ 24" | Correct but slow → fluency bottleneck, hand them the tool. Wrong → procedural gap, but do **not** block ratio work on it |
| Reading a two-column table | 3.MD | "Here is a table of cups and dollars. What does the second row say?" | Reads one column only → the 60% absolute-comparison error, upstream of everything |

**Three gap types, three different responses.**

- **Conceptual gap (multiplicative comparison).** The model was never built. This cannot be scaffolded around and is the one thing worth a just-in-time re-anchor: five minutes of "how many times as long" immediately before the first equivalent-ratio lesson, not a week of review before the unit.
- **Procedural gap (equivalent fractions).** The model exists but the routine is unreliable. Responds to worked examples — clean, correct ones, with no self-explanation prompt on the example itself, per the meta-analytic moderator — and to interleaved practice.
- **Fluency bottleneck (division).** The student can do it but so slowly that working memory is consumed and no ratio reasoning can happen. **Hand out the calculator and the multiplication chart, and use them on the check too.** Ellington's (2003) meta-analysis of 54 studies [T1] found problem-solving skills improved with calculator involvement and that results were mixed only "when calculators were **not** part of assessment" — handing the tool out on hard days only is the condition where the benefit disappeared. Frame this to any reviewer as a cognitive-load argument: 6.RP does not assess long division, so arithmetic here is a confound. **Honest caveat: I found no research at all on multiplication charts as a scaffold.** That recommendation is reasoning from cognitive load theory, not a retrieved finding.

**Differentiation that is one task, not three lessons.**
- **Number choice within a single problem set.** Same context, scale factors of 2, 3, and 7/4. Everyone works the same problem; the ceiling is in the numbers.
- **The two-problem structure** from §5 — friendly numbers, then not.
- **Worked-example pairs** for students who need a model before attempting: a completed example beside a matched blank. The meta-analysis says use *correct* examples for this, and leave the self-explanation prompt off the example itself.
- **Problem sorting** as a whole-class entry that requires no computation, so a student two years behind participates on the actual mathematical decision rather than on arithmetic.

**Above grade level.** Extend by reversibility and by generalization, not by accelerating into grade 7. "What would the second quantity have to be for these two ratios to be equivalent?" "Here are three ratio tables; one has an error. Find it and say what the student was thinking." Error-finding is the highest-value extension because it recruits the same misconception analysis you want in the room anyway — and note the retrieved caveat that *incorrect* worked examples helped low-prior-knowledge algebra students in one study while the aggregate moderator favored correct-only, so use error-finding as an extension task rather than as core instruction until that tension is resolved.

### 8. Open questions and weak spots

**There is no meta-analysis of proportional reasoning instruction.** This was verified directly against ERIC with two query forms. Anyone citing "meta-analytic evidence for how to teach ratios" is citing something that does not exist, or is citing a rational-number meta-analysis in which ratio is a minority of the corpus.

**The one WWC recommendation on ratio is rated Minimal Evidence.** Recommendation 4 of the fractions practice guide — "develop students' conceptual understanding of strategies for solving ratio, rate, and proportion problems before exposing them to cross-multiplication" — is the lowest of three WWC levels. Its evidence base, in the WWC's own words, is that "three studies... demonstrated that manipulatives and visual representations can be beneficial teaching tools for teaching proportions to students in grades 4, 5, and 7," plus three more that did not meet standards. The recommendation is sound; it is not carried by strong evidence, and it should not be quoted as "research-based" without the qualifier.

**The proportional-reasoning-specific systematic review says the field is thin.** Nelson et al. (2022) [T1], grades 5–9, students with learning disabilities or mathematics difficulty: **nine studies met inclusion criteria**, effects ranged from **g = −0.10 to 1.87**, and "two of the nine studies were deemed high quality." The authors deliberately declined to pool. Their conclusion — that proportional reasoning intervention for this population "is under investigated" — is itself a finding worth stating, because ratio is designated major work of the grade for three consecutive grades.

**The RCT evidence base is one research program.** Every randomized trial retrieved on teaching ratio, proportion, and percent traces to Jitendra's schema-based instruction work under IES grant R305A110358. That is real evidence and I would act on it, but "the RCT evidence" and "the SBI evidence" are currently the same sentence. The rational-number meta-analysis flags the analogous concern in its own corpus ("large representation from certain research groups").

**No middle-school ratio-predicts-algebra longitudinal study was retrieved.** The famous Siegler et al. (2012) finding [T1] — that elementary fraction and division knowledge uniquely predicts high school algebra and overall math achievement, controlling for IQ, working memory, and family income — is about **fractions and division, not ratio**. The nearest analogue found was Matthews et al. (2016) [T2], showing that individual differences in *nonsymbolic* ratio perception predict performance on a university entrance exam in algebra — a different construct, a different population, and correlational. **If this brief claimed "ratio reasoning predicts algebra," it would be overclaiming.** The honest support for spending time here is the coherence argument (three consecutive grades of major work) and the misconception-persistence data (21.2% of tenth graders still additive), not a predictive study.

**Nothing transferred to standardized tests.** Both the main SBI RCT and the implementer study found null effects on the GMADE and on end-of-year state math achievement. Every positive effect in this brief is on researcher-developed proportional-problem-solving measures.

**Effects shrink in exactly your conditions.** Grades 7–9 < grades 3–6; school personnel < research personnel; large group < small group; students with mathematics difficulties got 0.32 SD where the full sample got 0.46 SD, **and their growth trajectories did not change** — the intervention lifted their level without accelerating their rate. Classrooms with higher concentrations of English learners scored lower throughout, and the intervention did not moderate that.

**Marked unverified.** (1) A search summary referenced Kaminski & Sloutsky (2009) reporting that kindergarteners learning proportions transferred only from *abstract* materials in the absence of explicit instruction — **[unverified — surfaced in search, not retrieved]**. It is flagged because if real it complicates the concrete-first default. (2) The widely repeated claim that Hart (1981) found roughly two-thirds of 13–15-year-olds wrong on the Mr Short / Mr Tall item, most answering 8 — **[unverified — widely cited, primary source not retrieved; the CORE download returned an empty file]**. (3) Four flagship Van Dooren papers (2005 *Cognition and Instruction*; 2008 and 2009 *JRME*; 2010 *Cognition and Instruction*) were paywalled with no reachable mirror. Everything attributed to that research program in this brief comes from their own IBE-UNESCO synthesis, their 2004 PME paper, or the Fernández co-authored working paper. **No percentages from those four papers appear anywhere above.**

**What I would want and could not find:** a study isolating whether early exposure to cross-multiplication *as one strategy among several* helps or hurts later strategy selection. That is the question your current practice turns on, and Jitendra's design cannot answer it because his students received the algorithm bundled with everything else.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

This maps cleanly onto the CRA class-state spine with **one honest exception**, stated at the end.

**Day 1 — Ratio as a composed unit**
- Warm-up (2 review + 3 current): review = the 25-inch/10-inch rope item and `3/4 = ?/12`, both prerequisite diagnostics from §7. Current Q3 targets **absolute-vs-relative comparison** — two mixes, ask which is stronger, no numbers to compute.
- Concrete: batch building. Representational: draw the batch. Abstract: name it "3 to 2."
- Exit: qualitative comparison of two mixes.

**Day 2 — Equivalent ratios and the paint problem**
- Warm-up Q3 targets the **colon-as-fraction-bar** error: "2 blue, 3 red. Ratio of blue to red as a fraction? What fraction of the tiles are blue?"
- Concrete: iterate and partition batches. Representational: tape diagram, same units only.
- Discussion: the Abby/Zack paint comparison, three ways.

**Day 3 — Tape diagram to double number line**
- Warm-up Q3 targets **which diagram for which units**.
- Note the Küchemann rule when you build the first double number line: put the friendly multiplier *along* one line. Do not open on a between-lines multiplier.

**Day 4 — Ratio tables**
- Warm-up Q3 targets the **additive read of a table** — show a table with a row added rather than scaled, ask what the student did.
- The table arrives because the numbers got too big for the diagram. Make that pressure real by choosing the numbers.

**Day 5 — Non-integer ratios**
- Warm-up Q3 is the Peter-and-Tom non-integer version. This is the day the additive error is *supposed* to appear.

**Day 6 — Is it even proportional?**
- Sorting task. Non-proportional situations interleaved. This is the assessment of the whole topic.

**The exception, stated plainly.** The retrieved dose evidence says six days is not enough for the students you are most worried about — Jitendra's 9–10 session versions produced *nothing* for students with mathematics difficulties, and the rational-number meta-analysis found the significant duration threshold at 20+ hours. The research-supported version of this topic is closer to **eight to ten days**, and it does not fit alongside a Topic 2 that carries percents, unit rates, *and* conversions. That is a pacing conflict between the evidence and the district guide, not something to resolve by compressing the days above. The honest options are: take the extra days from Topic 2's conversions strand (which is application, not new machinery), or accept the shorter version and plan **distributed re-exposure** — ratio problems in the warm-up spiral every week for the rest of the semester, which the interleaving evidence supports independently and which costs no instructional days at all.
