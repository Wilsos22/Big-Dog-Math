# 6th Grade Decimals — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Standard(s):** 6.NS.B.2 (fluently divide multi-digit numbers, standard algorithm) · 6.NS.B.3 (fluently add, subtract, multiply, divide multi-digit decimals, standard algorithm for each) · 6.NS.C.6c/7 (rational numbers on the number line; ordering) · touches 6.RP.A.3c (percent) and the decimal–fraction–percent web
**Compiled:** 2026-07-29
**Scope:** Covers decimal magnitude and comparison, the four decimal operations, decimal–fraction–percent translation, and the representations used to teach all of it. Deliberately left out: percent problem-solving as its own topic (6.RP), negative rational numbers and absolute value (the actual content of 6.NS.C.7 — see §2), and decimal work beyond 6th grade.
**Evidence at a glance:** 14 Tier 1 · 15 Tier 2 · 6 Tier 3 sources retrieved this session. The evidence is unusually **strong** on where 6th graders fail (large-scale error data with exact prevalence figures) and on practice scheduling. It is **thin to nonexistent** on the concrete-to-abstract question specifically for decimals — there is not one concreteness-fading study on decimals in the literature, and the CRA evidence-based-practice designation explicitly excludes them. Where I extrapolate from fractions, I say so.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*One page. Read this before you teach.*

### The short version

Sixth grade adds almost no new decimal *concepts* — the standards authors position 6.NS.B.3 as consolidating algorithms first met in 5th grade, and the genuinely new base-ten content in 6th grade is negative numbers. What sixth grade actually inherits is a room where roughly **70% of students do not reliably know which of two decimals is larger.** So this unit is a repair job wearing a fluency unit's clothes, and the highest-leverage thing you can do is diagnose *which* wrong rule each kid is running in the first week, because the two common wrong rules require opposite instruction.

The thing most likely to go wrong: your item diet quietly manufactures the misconception. When every comparison problem has the same number of decimal places, treating decimals as whole numbers always works — so students learn that it works. When the textbook almost never asks for `6 + 0.32`, students over-generalize right-edge alignment and get 0.38. Both of those are documented, and both are free to fix.

### Where to spend the time

- **Decimal magnitude and ragged-length comparison** — 6th grade Year-6 data shows only ~31% expert; longer-is-larger 33%, shorter-is-larger 19%. Everything else in the unit sits on this, and the errors persist into adulthood if unaddressed.
- **Ragged-length and whole-number-plus-decimal addition/subtraction** — 6th graders are 74% correct on `4.6 + 2.3` and **12%** correct on `5.3 + 2.42`. That cliff is a single, nameable place-value alignment failure. Highest points-per-minute in the unit.
- **Direction of effect for × and ÷ by numbers below 1** — 6th graders score **29%** and **21%** on "is `0.77 × 0.63` bigger than 0.77?" Below chance. And it is *uncorrelated* with being good at comparison (r = 0.01), so nothing else you teach will fix it.

**What gets compressed to pay for it:** decimal long division procedure (6.NS.B.2/B.3) gets the standard algorithm taught efficiently and then moved into spiral warm-ups rather than owning multiple days — the research says nothing special about it beyond whole-number division fluency, and it is the sub-skill most safely handled procedurally-first. Percent as a topic waits for the 6.RP unit; here it appears only as a third name for the same point on the line.

### The sequence

1. **Diagnose before you teach anything** (Day 1, 10 min) — a ragged-length decimal comparison test sorts the room into longer-is-larger / shorter-is-larger / expert. This routes the next four days.
2. **Build magnitude on a length model** — because magnitude is the thing every later error traces back to, and length is the only attribute students can compare by eye without computing.
3. **Iterate concept and procedure, do not block them** — the one directly-on-point 6th grade decimal experiment found alternating concept→procedure→concept→procedure beat teaching all the concepts first.
4. **Addition/subtraction with ragged and whole-plus-decimal items from the first problem set** — so alignment is the visible issue rather than an occasional exception.
5. **Multiplication via area before any decimal-point rule** — derive the rule; do not issue it.
6. **Direction-of-effect as its own explicit target** — "will the answer be bigger or smaller, and why," taught as a sentence that names *both* the operation and the size of the numbers.
7. **Division last**, whole-number division fluency first (the coherence map makes 6.NS.B.2 a prerequisite of 6.NS.B.3, not a sibling).
8. **Interleave from the midpoint on** — mixed problem sets, not blocked ones.

### The three misconceptions to plan for

- **Longer is larger** — writes `0.25 > 0.5` ("25 is bigger than 5") → surface it with ordering sets that mix digit lengths: 0.3, 0.4, 0.38, 0.312. Never a set like 0.34, 0.35, 0.36 — they get those right.
- **Shorter is larger** — writes `0.4 > 0.83` ("tenths are bigger than hundredths") → surface it with 0.75 vs 0.5 and 7.942 vs 7.63, and with an equal-length pair like 0.3 vs 0.4, which catches the reciprocal variant. **This is the one teachers don't know exists.**
- **Right-edge alignment / "multiplication makes bigger"** — writes `6 + 0.32 = 0.38` and answers TRUE to `0.77 × 0.63 > 0.77` → surface with a matched pair on the same warm-up: `4.6 + 2.3` next to `6 + 0.32`, and an estimate-first prompt before any computation.

### The progression at a glance

The evidence does **not** support a clean C→R→A withdrawal schedule for decimals — see §6, this is a real finding, not a hedge. What it supports is: pick a length-based model, show the notation alongside it from day one, and withdraw the model per student on demonstrated accuracy, not on the calendar.

| Stage | Students do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| **Length model + notation together** | Build 0.8, 0.39, 0.478 on deciwire / paper strips; the numeral is written beside the model from the first minute | Student can **predict** which is longer before building it |
| **Drawing that preserves length** | Sketch the same comparisons as marked segments on a number line; annotate the tenths gradations and say which digit decided it | Student marks the deciding place without prompting |
| **Notation alone** | Compare, order, and compute from symbols; model available on request | Exit ticket accurate on **ragged-length** items, not equal-length ones |
| **Return path** | Any student, any day, may pull the strips back out — it is a check, not a demotion | — |

### If they're two years behind

The blocking prerequisites are 4.NF.C.6/C.7 (decimal notation for tenths/hundredths; compare decimals to hundredths) and 5.NBT.A.1/A.3 (each place is 1/10 the place to its left; compare decimals to thousandths) — plus 5.NBT.B.5/B.6 whole-number multiplication and division fluency for the algorithms. Diagnostics: *"Which is larger, 0.4 or 0.83? Explain."* separates the two wrong rules in one item — a longer-is-larger student says 0.83 for the wrong reason, a shorter-is-larger student says 0.4 and gives you a *correct fraction argument*. Second item: *"3 tenths is how many hundredths?"* — only 39% of 6th graders get this. Just-in-time move: re-anchor "the decimal point marks the ones column" in five minutes before the alignment lesson rather than running a place-value unit first.

### Watch out for

- **Money is a trap, not a shortcut.** It permits complete success without any decimal understanding, caps at hundredths, and reading it aloud as "three dollars and seventy-six cents" actively teaches the two-separate-whole-numbers misconception. Use it for application late in the unit, never as the entry model.
- **Base-ten blocks carry interference.** Students trained that the unit cube is one now have to hold that the flat is one. The one decimal-specific comparison of manipulatives found a length model markedly more accessible than the volume model, partly *because* of that prior use.
- **"Count the decimal places" and estimation both have documented failure modes.** Estimation cannot place the point in `0.023 × 0.0045`; the counting rule without meaning produces the 73% of multiplication errors that are correct digits with a misplaced point. Teach the rule as a *conclusion* students derive, keep estimation as the plausibility check.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. What the research supports

**Magnitude understanding is the load-bearing idea, and it drives procedure more than the reverse.** In a deliberately low-prior-knowledge sample of 74 fifth graders — 25 additional students were excluded for already being able to do the procedures — conceptual magnitude knowledge at pretest significantly predicted procedural gain (ηp² = 0.33; 23% of variance after controlling for procedural pretest), while **procedural pretest predicted nothing at all** (Rittle-Johnson, Siegler & Alibali, 2001) [T1/T2]. The relationship is iterative rather than one-way — procedural gains then fed back into conceptual gains — but the entry point is magnitude. Siegler's integrated theory frames why: numerical development is the progressive broadening of the set of numbers whose magnitudes you can represent, and the number line is the one structure that carries whole numbers, fractions, and decimals at once [T3, theory].

**The number line is the best-evidenced representation, and the WWC rates it Strong.** The 2021 IES practice guide *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics* (grades K–6) rates all six of its recommendations at **Strong Evidence**, and Recommendation 4 — number lines, based on 14 studies — names decimals in grades 3–6 explicitly, instructing teachers to "use number lines to compare the magnitude of fractions and decimals" and to "expand the idea of equivalencies to include decimals and percentages" [T1]. A 2024/2025 meta-analysis restricted to studies meeting WWC Group Design Standards v4.0 (28 studies, 3,853 participants, grades 3–9 with mathematics difficulties) found a mean effect of **g = 0.68**, with **use of the number line (β = 0.47)** and **teaching mathematical language (β = 0.50)** the two components significantly associated with impact (Krowka et al.) [T1]. Restricted to magnitude-understanding outcomes, the number-line association rose to b = 1.02.

Two honest caveats on that. First, the meta-analysis's **95% prediction interval was −0.36 to 1.8** — "rational number intervention" as a category is not reliably effective; the specific components are what carry weight. Second, **19 of those 28 studies were fractions-only.** Decimals are genuinely under-studied, and the number-line recommendation reaches them partly by extension.

**But the number line only works when it is gradated and attention is directed.** The one decimal number-line experiment in the WWC fractions guide's evidence base — 61 students in grades 5–6, one ~40-minute session, three treatment arms — found that combining **tenths markings on the line AND a prompt to notice the tenths digit** produced an effect size of **0.57**, while the markings alone and the prompt alone each produced nothing significant [T1] (Rittle-Johnson, Siegler & Alibali, 2001, Exp. 2, as scored in WWC 2010). A blank 0-to-1 line is not the intervention. The guide's own caveat is worth keeping: both groups used number lines, so the study does not establish that number lines beat other representations.

**Explicit instruction and taught heuristics are the two highest-leverage components for below-grade-level students.** Gersten et al.'s meta-analysis of 42 interventions for students with learning disabilities found all components except peer-assisted learning and feedback-with-goal-setting produced significant effects (0.21 to 1.56), but hierarchical regression identified **explicit instruction and teaching students to use heuristics** as the two providing "practically and statistically important increases in effect size" [T1]. For decimals, that argues for an explicitly taught routine for *comparing two decimals* over another manipulative.

**Interleaving is the cheapest large effect available.** In 126 seventh graders across real classrooms for three months, with only the *order* of the same problems varied, interleaved practice beat blocked practice at **d = 0.42** on a 1-day test and **d = 0.79** at 30 days (Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic, 2015) [T1]. Note the direction: the advantage *grew* with delay. The complement is that blocked practice produces higher in-class accuracy and lower retention (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007, undergraduates) [T2] — expect your during-class accuracy to drop when you interleave, and read that drop as the intervention working. The WWC 2021 guide independently endorses this under Recommendation 1: "regularly present a variety of problems that require students to discriminate among problem types… so students do not overgeneralize" [T1].

**Showing wrong work beats showing only right work, including for low-prior-knowledge students.** 74 fourth and fifth graders were randomly assigned to compare correct *and incorrect* examples of decimal magnitude versus correct examples only. The incorrect condition produced more learning of both procedures and concepts, reduced misconceptions, and — the finding that matters most for a mixed-readiness room — **prior knowledge of decimals did not interact with condition** (Durkin & Rittle-Johnson, 2012) [T1 design / T2 scale, n=74, single brief session]. The usual worry that error-based examples confuse struggling students was not supported.

**Decimals are not the "easy" rational number.** A systematic review comparing decimals, fractions, and percentages concluded that "decimals are not generally easier to understand than fractions, though they are easier on some tasks," with similar difficulty in magnitude, arithmetic, and density (Tian & Siegler, 2018) [T3]. Critically, the apparent decimal advantage in comparison studies "may depend on the equality of the number of digits" — in almost every experiment finding a decimal advantage, the pairs had equal digit counts. **That is a warning about your assessment design, not just about the literature.**

### 2. Emphasis — where the leverage is

**A structural finding first, because it reframes the whole unit.** Pulling the raw graph data behind Achieve the Core's coherence map (480 standards, 757 directed edges): 6.NS.B.3 has exactly four prerequisites — 5.NBT.B.5, 5.NBT.B.6, 5.NBT.B.7, and **6.NS.B.2 as a same-grade prerequisite**, meaning whole-number long division must precede decimal division rather than run alongside it. And **6.NS.C.7 has no prerequisite edges and no outgoing edges**, with all five Illustrative Mathematics tasks tagged to it concerning integers and negatives, not decimals. Comparing and ordering decimals is formally **4.NF.C.7 → 5.NBT.A.3**, not a 6th grade standard.

The CCSS NBT progression document says the same thing directly: "At Grade 6, students extend their fluency with the standard algorithms, using these for all four operations with decimals… In Grade 6, students extend the base-ten system to **negative numbers**" [T1, standards authors].

So the emphasis argument is this: **6th grade decimals has no new concepts to displace.** Time spent on magnitude and comparison is prerequisite repair delivered inside a grade-level unit — defensible, standards-honest, and something you should plan and time-box as such rather than pretend is new content.

**Leverage 1 — decimal magnitude and ragged-length comparison. Argument type: misconception density plus durability.** The Melbourne Learning Decimals Project tested 3,204 students in years 4–10 across 12 schools every six months for four years with a 30-item Decimal Comparison Test. **Year 6 (n=262): 31% task expert, 33% longer-is-larger, 19% shorter-is-larger, 18% unclassified** [T1/T2]. Individual Year 6 classes ran to **37% shorter-is-larger** and **27% reciprocal-or-negative thinking**. The authors flag "task expert" as an *over-estimate*, because a memorized rule passes the test.

Durability is what makes this an emphasis argument rather than a curiosity. Probability of retesting longer-is-larger 6+ months later: **0.44**; shorter-is-larger **0.38**, rising to **0.49 at Year 8**. About **one in six** students holding the stickiest codes answered identically more than two years later, and **one in five** who once tested expert later failed to [T1/T2, Steinle & Stacey 2004]. It persists past school: only ~80% of pre-service teachers tested as experts, and **13% of a sample of over 500 chose 0 as larger than 0.6** [T1, Stacey et al. 2001].

Grade 6 is also the last clean window. Steinle & Stacey found students *move into* shorter-is-larger thinking in Years 7–8, attributing it to interference from newly-taught negative numbers and scientific notation.

**Leverage 2 — ragged-length and whole-plus-decimal addition/subtraction. Argument type: a documented cliff with an identified curricular cause.** From Hiebert & Wearne's 670-student grades 5–9 dataset (reported in Lortie-Forgues, Tian & Siegler, 2015) [T2 review of T1 data]: 6th graders were **74% correct on decimal addition when addends had equal decimal places (4.6 + 2.3) and 12% correct when they differed (5.3 + 2.42).** It persists — 9th graders were 90% on 0.60 − 0.36 versus 64% on 0.86 − 0.3.

Worse for whole-plus-decimal. Reanalyzing item-level data plus 3,359 grade-6 students across 73 districts, Tian, Braithwaite & Siegler found 7th graders **42% correct on `6 + .32`**, with the single answer **0.38 accounting for 45–50% of all errors** on that item; and **~38% correct on `4 + .3`** with **0.7 constituting 48–51% of errors** [T1]. Overall whole-plus-decimal vs decimal-plus-decimal addition/subtraction: **37% vs 70%.**

Their causal argument is the actionable part: textbooks under-present whole-plus-decimal addition, so students over-generalize right-edge alignment — a strategy that is correct on every whole-number problem and on every equal-length decimal problem they actually see. The same logic runs back to the origin of this literature: Sackur-Grisvard & Léonard (1985), and Resnick's explanation of why the whole-number rule is so common — instruction gives students practice mainly comparing decimals with the same number of digits, where treating them as whole numbers always works.

**Fixing this costs nothing.** It is a change in which problems you assign, not in how you teach.

**Leverage 3 — direction of effect for × and ÷ below 1. Argument type: prerequisite fan-out plus the fact that nothing else touches it.** Lortie-Forgues & Siegler (2017), 19 sixth and 36 eighth graders: sixth graders' accuracy on "true or false: 0.77 × 0.63 > 0.77" was **29% for multiplication and 21% for division** with operands below one, against 76% and 87% with operands above one [T1]. Eighth graders were *worse* (15% and 18%). **49% of students got all four below-one items wrong while getting all 12 other items right** — a clean systematic rule, not noise.

Two things make this high-leverage. First, it is **uncorrelated with magnitude comparison** (6th grade r = 0.01), so it is not a side effect of anything else you're teaching and won't be fixed by fixing comparison. Second, the explanation coding identifies the instructional target precisely: **56% of student explanations were unconditional operation statements** ("multiplication makes bigger"), and those students scored **under 10%**; students whose explanations cited *both the operation and the magnitude of the operands* scored **93% and 88%**. The teachable move is a sentence structure, not a rule.

It also blocks self-correction: because "multiplication makes bigger," a wrong answer like `5.5 × 0.6 = 33` *looks right* and the correct answer looks wrong. Students reject valid procedures on the strength of it. And it survives to adulthood — 41 pre-service teachers scored 33% and 29% on the fraction versions, below chance.

**What gets less time, and why that's defensible.** Decimal long division. Nothing in the retrieved literature identifies it as a distinct conceptual hurdle beyond whole-number division fluency — the progression document handles "move the decimal point in the divisor and dividend the same number of places" by re-unitizing (`7 ÷ 0.2` is `70 tenths ÷ 2 tenths` is `70 ÷ 2`), which is one explanation, not a week. Teach it explicitly, verify with a couple of exit tickets, and move it into spiral warm-ups. Also compressed: percent. It appears here only as a third label for a point on the line, deferred as a topic to the 6.RP unit.

### 3. Sequence and why

1. **Diagnose (Day 1, 10 minutes).** Ragged-length comparison test, sorted into three piles. *Makes possible:* everything after this is targeted rather than sprayed. The Melbourne group's own intervention data is the argument for spending the time — see §7.
2. **Magnitude on a length model, notation shown alongside.** *Makes possible:* every subsequent error has a physical referent you can send students back to. Length rather than area or volume for reasons in §6.
3. **Comparison and ordering with ragged items, plus density.** *Makes possible:* the density work ("how many numbers between 0.14 and 0.15?") is what actually tells you whether the number system has changed in a student's head — see §4.
4. **Addition and subtraction, ragged and whole-plus-decimal from the first set.** *Makes possible:* alignment becomes the visible question rather than a footnote.
5. **Multiplication via area, with estimation first, rule derived last.** *Makes possible:* the decimal-point rule arrives as a conclusion students can reconstruct.
6. **Direction of effect as an explicit named target.** *Makes possible:* plausibility checking on every subsequent answer.
7. **Division, after whole-number division fluency.** Required by the coherence map's 6.NS.B.2 → 6.NS.B.3 edge.
8. **Interleaved mixed practice from step 4 onward.**

#### Where the research disagrees

**Concepts-first versus iterating.** The instinct — front-load all the place-value conceptual work, then run the algorithms — was tested head-to-head against alternating, on decimals, with sixth graders, and lost. Rittle-Johnson & Koedinger (2009) ran two classroom experiments (N = 77 and N = 26) with six decimal lessons on an intelligent tutoring system: an **iterative** condition cycling concept → procedure → concept → procedure versus a **concepts-first** condition. "In both experiments, students in the iterative condition gained more knowledge of arithmetic procedures, including ability to transfer the procedures to problems with novel features. Knowledge of concepts was fairly comparable across conditions" [T1]. Effect sizes as scored by the WWC: **2.38** on the n=26 randomized study and **0.63** (not statistically significant) on the four-classroom study.

This is the single most directly transferable sequencing finding available — right grade, right topic, randomized. **Recommendation: iterate.** Treat direction as reliable and magnitude as not; the 2.38 comes from n=26.

**What would flip me:** if a class is so uniformly at longer-is-larger that computation days produce nothing but noise, front-load magnitude for two or three days before alternating. The Rittle-Johnson study's students were in a functioning 6th grade sequence, not two years behind.

**Which comes first, decimals or fractions.** Tian & Siegler's review concludes the evidence does not establish an ideal order. Moss & Case's (1999) percent-first experimental curriculum (n=16 treatment / 13 matched control, 4th grade, ~20 lessons) produced substantially better rational-number understanding, less whole-number reasoning on novel problems, and better compare-and-order performance (5.37/7 vs 3.45/7, t = 2.34, p < .03) — but **no difference on standard computation**, and the design is confounded: the treatment differed from control in sequence *and* in its magnitude emphasis *and* in linking to intuitive proportional sense, at two different schools, taught by the curriculum author. Tian & Siegler say so explicitly: "the effect of the sequence… remains to be established." (Note: their review describes the participants as randomly assigned; Moss & Case's own participants section describes two intact classes. Trust the primary source.)

**Recommendation: don't sequence decimals and fractions as separate units at all.** Teach them as one system with two notations — supported by Hurst & Cordes (2018), where children in grades 4–7 represented fraction and decimal magnitudes on a single continuum [T2, n=41], and by the durability of the NAEP-era finding that fewer than 40% of 13-year-olds identified 0.2 as equivalent to 1/5, with **38% choosing 0.5**. Borrow Moss & Case's *ingredient* (magnitude emphasis plus proportional intuition), not their claim.

### 4. Misconceptions and how to prepare

---

**Misconception:** Longer is larger (whole-number thinking)

- **Looks like:** `0.25 > 0.5`. Also `4.7 < 4.08` ("the zero doesn't matter and 8 is bigger than 7"), and `0.21 = 0.0021`.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** In whole numbers, more digits means bigger — 3 < 30 < 300. And comparing equal-length decimals as whole numbers **always works**, which is most of what they have practiced. This is a rule that used to be true, applied one domain too far.
- **Evidence:** Resnick et al. (1989), n=113 interviewed across US grade 5, Israel grade 6, France grades 4–5 — **88% of children could be classified as consistently using one of three rules**, making decimal bugs far more stable than subtraction or algebra bugs [T1]. US grade 5 whole-number rule prevalence .35; France grade 4 .41. Melbourne Year 6: 33% [T1/T2].
- **Surface it early with:** ordering sets that **mix digit lengths** — 0.3, 0.4, 0.38, 0.39, 0.312. Never 0.34, 0.35, 0.36, 0.37; they order those correctly and you learn nothing. Verbatim from the Victorian materials: "while there are likely to be gains in the short-term, in the long-term students tend to forget rules that are not supported by understanding."
- **When it shows up:** Do not reteach the comparison rule. Send them to the length model and have them build both numbers, then ask which is longer *before* they look at the numerals. The confusion is the engagement — "you built the one with more digits and it came out shorter, so what do you know now?"

---

**Misconception:** Shorter is larger (denominator-focused)

- **Looks like:** `0.4 > 0.83`. Student explanation, verbatim from Resnick's interviews: "4.7 > 4.08, since this has 8 hundredths and this has 7 tenths"; "7.457 > 4.4502, because hundredths are bigger than ten-thousandths."
- **Why it makes sense to them:** *When you cut a whole into more parts, the parts get smaller* — a genuinely true and important fraction fact. These students have **good place value** and a **correct fraction idea**; they are failing only to coordinate the size of the pieces with how many there are.
- **Evidence:** Resnick et al. (1989): fraction-rule prevalence Israel grade 6 = .33, US grade 5 = .18 [T1]. And it is the more *persistent* of the two: Zuker (1985, reported in Resnick) found the whole-number rule fell from 18% (gr 7) to 5% (gr 9) while **the fraction rule held at 23% in both**. Melbourne Year 6: 19%, up to 37% in one class.
- **Surface it early with:** 0.75 vs 0.5 · 7.942 vs 7.63 · 2.8325 vs 2.516 · 0.426 vs 0.3. These are the exact items the Decimal Comparison Test uses to separate S from L — note that a longer-is-larger student gets all of these *right*.
- **When it shows up:** **Do not reteach place value — they have it.** The move is to make them count as well as size: "You're right that a tenth is bigger than a hundredth. How many hundredths are in 0.83? How many hundredths are in 0.4?" This is the one where the *reasoning is nearly correct*, which is exactly the kind of confusion worth celebrating out loud.

**The teacher-facing part of this one matters as much as the student-facing part.** Stacey et al. (2001) found that pre-service teachers "were aware of longer-is-larger misconceptions in students, but had little awareness of shorter-is-larger misconceptions" [T1]. Roughly one in five of your kids holds an error most adults in the building don't know exists.

---

**Misconception:** Reciprocal / negative thinking (the sticky variant of shorter-is-larger)

- **Looks like:** `0.3 > 0.4` (reading them as 1/3 and 1/4), or `0.20 > 0.35` (reading them as −20 and −35). A tertiary student, verbatim: "I was thinking along a number line and considering decimal numbers to be equivalent to negative numbers. Therefore −20 was larger than −35."
- **Why it makes sense to them:** `1/12 > 1/3456` is completely correct fraction reasoning applied to the wrong notation; `−12 > −18` is completely correct integer reasoning applied to the wrong notation. Decimals, fractions, and negatives all arrive as "the opposite of the big-number operations."
- **Evidence:** Persistence 33% on retest, the highest of any non-expert code, and it *rises* with grade; Melbourne recorded 27% of one Year 6 class [T1/T2, Steinle & Stacey 2004]. Adults: 13% of 500+ pre-service teachers chose 0 as larger than 0.6.
- **Surface it early with:** **equal-length pairs** — `0.3 vs 0.4`. Resnick and Sackur-Grisvard never included these "because it is hard to imagine any student who could answer this incorrectly," which is exactly why this misconception stayed invisible for decades. And a **comparison with zero** — `0.6 vs 0` — which is the only item that separates negative thinking from reciprocal thinking.
- **When it shows up:** Physical placement on a line with zero marked. Also: when you teach negative numbers later in the year, use decimal examples deliberately, because Steinle & Stacey attribute the Years 7–8 spike in this error to negative-number instruction that never mentions decimals.

---

**Misconception:** Right-edge alignment

- **Looks like:** `6 + 0.32 = 0.38`. `4 + 0.3 = 0.7`. `5.3 + 2.42` treated as `.3 + .42`.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** *Right-align, then add* is correct for every whole-number addition problem and every equal-decimal-place problem — which is nearly the entire diet they've been fed.
- **Evidence:** 42% correct on `6 + .32` with 0.38 as 45–50% of all errors; 74% vs 12% on equal vs unequal decimal places (§2) [T1].
- **Surface it early with:** a **matched pair on the same page** — `4.6 + 2.3` immediately followed by `6 + 0.32`. The gap between the two *is* the diagnosis, and the student sees it too.
- **When it shows up:** "Estimate first — is `6 + 0.32` closer to 6 or closer to 0?" Then the decimal point marks the ones column, so line up the ones. The single motto — **"the decimal point marks the ones column"** — appears independently in both the Melbourne materials and the CCSS progression document, which is a good sign it belongs on your wall.

---

**Misconception:** Multiplication makes bigger / division makes smaller

- **Looks like:** answering TRUE to `0.77 × 0.63 > 0.77`; accepting `5.5 × 0.6 = 33`; rejecting a correct answer because it got smaller.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** For every whole number ≥ 1, multiplying does make it bigger. Nine years of consistent evidence.
- **Evidence:** 6th graders 29% (×) and 21% (÷); 49% missed all four below-one items while getting all 12 others right; uncorrelated with comparison ability (r = 0.01); **56% of explanations were unconditional operation statements**, associated with <10% accuracy (Lortie-Forgues & Siegler, 2017) [T1].
- **Surface it early with:** true/false plausibility items with numbers too awkward to compute — `0.77 × 0.63 > 0.77` — paired with **"explain why."** The explanation is the real diagnostic.
- **When it shows up:** Do not correct the answer. Get the sentence structure: the students who succeed say *both* the operation and the size of the numbers. "Multiplying by a number less than one makes it smaller, because you're taking part of it." Model that sentence, then make them produce it.

---

**Misconception:** Discreteness (density)

- **Looks like:** "There are no numbers between 3.14 and 3.15, because 14 and 15 are consecutive." Or "after 4.9 comes 4.10." Or the halfway version: "there are exactly nine numbers between 0.41 and 0.42."
- **Why it makes sense to them:** every natural number has a successor and there is nothing between 63 and 64.
- **Evidence:** van Hoof, Verschaffel & Van Dooren (2015), n=1343, grades 4–12: natural number bias was "weakest in size tasks, somewhat stronger in operations tasks, and **by far the strongest in density tasks**," equally strong for decimals and fractions, and it did **not disappear with grade** except for size tasks [T2]. Hart (1981): 22–39% of 12–15 year olds thought there were eight, nine, or ten decimals between 0.41 and 0.42. Vamvakoussi et al. (2018): students also believe the in-between numbers must share the endpoints' notation — only decimals between decimals [T1].
- **Surface it early with:** "How many numbers are between 3.14 and 3.15?" Score it **three ways, not two** — *zero* (successor principle), *exactly nine* (bounded by one extra place), *infinitely many* (correct). The middle category is progress, and needs a different next move than the first.
- **When it shows up:** The "Number Between" routine in §5. The Indonesian pre-service teacher study warns explicitly against the procedural fix — teaching "write 0.30 and 0.40, now put 0.31 in between" is "meaningless to students and therefore easily forgotten" without conceptual grounding [T2].

**Why density matters more than it looks:** if you only assess comparison, you will overestimate understanding. Ordering is the *easiest* of the three natural-number-bias tasks. Density is the diagnostic that reveals whether the number system has actually changed in a student's head.

### 5. Concrete activities

**A. Ragged Decimal Comparison Test → targeted routing.** Students circle the larger in ~24 engineered pairs, no working, no calculator. You sort by *pattern*, not score: Type 1 items (4.8/4.63, 0.5/0.36, 0.8/0.75, 3.92/3.4813, 0.37/0.216) catch longer-is-larger; Type 2 items (0.75/0.5, 7.942/7.63, 2.8325/2.516, 5.736/5.62, 0.426/0.3) catch shorter-is-larger. Add a **zero comparison** (0.6 vs 0), allow **"equal"** as a response so 0.8 vs 0.80 is live, and include **truncation pairs** (0.45 vs 0.453). *Materials:* one printed page. *Structure:* place value as the sole determinant of magnitude. *Cannot show:* anything about operations, or *why*; a memorized "annex zeros" rule passes it. *Time:* 10 min to give, 10 to sort. [T1/T2 — Melbourne Learning Decimals Project; validity evidence: across 3,531 tests the most common Type1/Type2 score pairs were (5,5), (0,5), (5,0), with all 33 other possible pairs accounting for only 28%.]

**B. Deciwire — a length model you can build for almost nothing.** Per pair: **1.2 m of curtain wire** with a threaded eye screwed in one end; **drinking straws cut to 12 cm** (tenths) and **12 mm** (hundredths); **hole-punched cardboard squares**, ten of which stack to 12 mm (thousandths). Different straw colors for tenths and hundredths at first, single color later. Students build 0.8, 0.39, and 0.478 and lay them side by side — the student who called 0.478 biggest watches it physically lose to 0.8. *Structure:* magnitude as **length**, plus the endless base-ten chain. *Cannot show:* multiplication by a decimal at all, and only selected quotitive divisions. *Critical framing:* the whole is ~1 m long — **do not call it a metre.** The source is explicit that naming units "may perpetuate misconceptions that the decimal point simply separates one whole-number quantity from another." Say "one." *Time:* 30–40 min to build once, 5–15 min per use thereafter. [T2 — Young-Loveridge & Mills (2011), *APMC* 16(2); the free substitute for commercial decipipes, which the authors note bend and ruin alignment.]

**C. Number Between.** Two students hold cards with two numbers and stand at the ends of an imaginary line across the room. A third writes a number in between and physically stands where it goes. If correct, an endpoint sits down and hands their card on; the interval halves, and halves again. *Structure:* **density.** *Design rules from the source:* **start with integers**, not 0-to-1, "to ensure students realise decimals exist between all integers and not just between 0 and 1"; deliberately mix decimal lengths in your pairs, because "one reason for students' misconceptions is that they don't encounter decimals of different lengths simultaneously, as they learn about tenths, hundredths and thousandths in successive units." *Materials:* laminated cards or index cards. *Cannot show:* composition of a decimal, or operations. Students who can *recognize* 0.35 as between 0.3 and 0.4 often cannot *produce* it — build it on the length model first. *Time:* 5–10 min, recurring. [T2 — Melbourne; Tromp (1999) *APMC* 4(3).]

**D. Hidden Numbers.** Two decimals on the board, one above the other, decimal points aligned and visible, every digit covered by a card. Teams **pay points to reveal one digit**, and may declare "larger," "smaller," or "equal" at any time. *Structure:* the strategy is the mathematics — a team that understands place value buys the tenths digit first and often needs nothing else; a team running a length rule wastes turns on the right-hand end. Include at least one genuinely equal pair (0.32 / 0.3200) so "equal" is live. *Materials:* whiteboard and index cards. *Cannot show:* magnitude by any physical means — purely symbolic. It is a **check** that the meaning built in B transferred to notation. *Time:* 10 min. [T2 — Melbourne.]

**E. Decimal Skip Counting.** Complete counting sequences, mentally where possible, on engineered fault lines: `0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, …` (the trouble spot is "zero point ten"); `0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.45, …` (the numeral alternates length while the value climbs — a counterexample to both wrong rules, generated by the students themselves); count from 0 by 0.125; count up from 0.88 by 0.02, then from 0.883 by 0.02; count down from 6.789 by 0.002. Instruction from the source that's easy to miss: **don't start every sequence at zero.** Self-check with the constant-addition function on a $1 calculator. *Structure:* notation under pressure. *Cannot show:* no magnitude model, no operational meaning. Its value is manufacturing cognitive conflict cheaply, repeatedly, in five dead minutes. *Materials:* none. *Time:* 5 min. [T2 — Melbourne.]

**F. Correct/Incorrect Example Pairs.** Show a **wrong** decimal comparison or computation side by side with a right one and have students explain the difference — not fix the wrong one, *explain* it. *Evidence:* the incorrect-examples condition outperformed correct-examples-only on both procedures and concepts, reduced misconceptions, and **prior knowledge did not interact with condition** (Durkin & Rittle-Johnson, 2012, n=74, randomized) [T1 design/T2 scale]. Students in the incorrect condition "more frequently discussed correct concepts, such as the magnitude of a decimal and identifying misconceptions." *Materials:* none. *Time:* 5 min, as a warm-up format. *This is the cheapest research-backed routine in the brief, and it maps directly onto your existing error-analysis practice days.*

**G. Tenths of Tenths → the area model for multiplication.** This covers exactly the gap the length model leaves. Show a unit square cut 10×10. Walk the scaling: side 100 → area 10,000, small square = 100; side 10 → area 100, small square = 1; **side 1 → area 1, small square = 0.01, its side = 0.1, so 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01.** Then zoom: take one small square as the new whole and repeat → 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001. **Then estimate first:** show 0.7 × 0.8 shaded and ask "roughly what fraction of the square is that?" — students say about half, so the answer is about 0.5 — *then* count: 7 × 8 = 56 hundredths = 0.56. *Structure:* why the decimal-place rule is true, and simultaneous inoculation against "multiplication makes bigger." *Materials:* photocopied 10×10 grids; put one in a sheet protector with dry-erase markers and print one page per student for the year. *Cannot show:* bounded at hundredths-of-hundredths visually, and area is genuinely harder than length for students two years behind — **run B before G.** *Time:* 20–30 min. [T2/T3 — Illustrative Mathematics task 2188; Melbourne area-multiplication lesson.]

**H. Get Closest to 1.** Warm-up: cards showing shaded 10×10 grids; students name each value as both a fraction and a decimal, then find two cards summing near one whole. Game: draw four digit cards 0–9, arrange as movable digits into **two decimal addends whose sum is as close to 1 as possible** (from 1, 4, 5, 8 → 0.15 + 0.84 = 0.99). Four-step recording sheet: place-value chart → shade the sum on a grid → is it greater or less than 1 → how far from 1. **Closing question, which is the whole lesson:** *"Which place matters more for getting close to 1 — tenths or hundredths?"* *Materials:* paper. *Cannot show:* hundredths only; and the flat-equals-100 versus flat-equals-1 collision is live — post the correspondences. *Time:* 25–35 min. [T2 — Suh, Johnston, Jamieson & Mills (2008), *MTMS* 14(1), developed in a **Title 1 school with high ELL and special-ed enrollment** — the closest population match in this literature.]

**On money.** Use it for application late in the unit, never as the entry model. The Melbourne materials: "there are no visual clues from the coins that 45 cents is a fraction of a dollar… dollars and cents are often seen as two separate numbers." Brekke (1996), quoted in a Cambridge Mathematics review: teachers see students solve money-context decimal problems correctly and "fail to see that the children do not understand decimal numbers in such cases, but rather that such understanding is **not needed**… it can be a hindrance to the development of a robust decimal concept" [T4 review of T2/T3 sources]. "Money thinking" is a named, coded misconception in the Melbourne taxonomy — students who believe 4.4502 equals 4.45. If you use money, **read amounts as decimals aloud** ("two point five four") and apply the same rule to metric ("230 cm," never "2 m and 30 cm"). One good use: display a real cafeteria price list containing an error — lollies listed as "0.5" when the price is 5c — and have students hunt it.

### 6. The progression to abstract

**Lead with what the evidence actually establishes, because it is not what the default assumes.**

There is **not one concreteness-fading study on decimals.** A full ERIC index search on "concreteness fading" returned 20 records; the empirical corpus is mathematical equivalence (`a + b = a + __`, ages 7–9), modular arithmetic (undergraduates), algebraic notation (grade 7), vector addition, and physics. None concern decimals or rational-number magnitude.

And the CRA evidence-based-practice designation **explicitly excludes decimals.** Bouck, Satsangi & Park (2018), applying formal EBP quality standards, determined CRA an evidence-based practice for students with learning disabilities "relative to **computational problems, such as addition, subtraction, and multiplication, largely with regrouping**" [T1]. Whole-number computation. Not decimals, not magnitude, not comparison.

The decimal-adjacent CRA evidence is **two single-case studies from one research group, total n = 6** — Flores, Hinton & Schweck (2024, *Learning Disability Quarterly*, n=3, grades 4 and 6; and 2024, *RASE*, n=3, grade 5), which demonstrated functional relations including "accuracy writing fractions as decimals" and "equivalent decimals" [T2]. Genuinely relevant to decimal–fraction translation; a scale that supports "promising," not "established."

**So what does the evidence support?**

**First: the fade itself is far less tested than its ubiquity implies, and where tested head-to-head it usually ties.**

- The only meta-analytic test of fade-versus-integrated: Ebner et al. (2025), meta-regressing 8 moderators across 30 single-case studies, found non-integrated (true sequential-fade) CRA increased effect size by **0.011 on a Tau-BC index bounded near 1.0**, p = .045 [T1]. Statistically significant, practically nil.
- The one randomized head-to-head **at your grade level**: Morano, Flores, Hinton & Meyer (2020) randomly assigned matched pairs of **28 fifth and sixth graders with disabilities** to sequential CRA versus **integrated CRA-I (no withdrawal)** on rational-number content. Result: "**CRA and CRA-I both led to similarly large pre-to-post performance gains** (ES = 0.95 on posttest and 0.80 on a fraction number line estimation test)" [T2]. Withdrawal was not necessary to get a large effect.
- Fading versus **simultaneous** presentation: a classroom experiment with N = 187 found **no significant difference**, with a formal equivalence test (bounds d = ±0.5) showing both approaches performed equally, and noted that fading's support "is supported mainly by studies in mathematics education, while the results in physics are equivocal" (Lichtenberger, Kokkonen & Schalk, 2024) [T1].
- Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014) — the review universally cited as "the evidence for fading" — uses the language "we **recommend**" and "**theoretical** benefits," is classified by ERIC as Information Analyses / Reports-Evaluative, and reports **no pooled effect size** [T1 venue, but an argument rather than a pooled estimate].

**Where fading did win, the comparison was against the reverse order, not against integration.** Fyfe & McNeil's four-condition study (n = 63, ages 7–9, low prior knowledge) found concrete→abstract beat concrete-only, abstract-only, **and abstract→concrete**, F(1,63) = 7.68, p = .007 [T2, poster]. McNeil & Fyfe (2012) found fading best in undergraduates learning modular arithmetic, with delayed transfer at 1 and 3 weeks. Ottmar & Landy (2017) found fading beat *concreteness introduction* in grade 7 algebra — but what was faded there was **dynamic on-screen motion of symbols**, not physical materials.

So the defensible claim is narrow: **starting concrete and ending abstract beats starting abstract and ending concrete, and beats staying at either pole.** That is real. It is not the same claim as "you must systematically withdraw the manipulative."

**Second: the WWC panel — the most conservative body here — endorses a mastery-gated, reversible fade, and says to show the notation simultaneously.** Recommendation 3 instructs teachers to "present the mathematical notation **simultaneously**" with concrete and semi-concrete representations, to connect representations to each other, and to **revisit them periodically.** Their obstacle-and-advice entry, verbatim:

> **OBSTACLE:** "I connected the abstract concepts and procedures to concrete and semi-concrete representations and then faded them, but I don't think my students fully understand the concepts."
> **PANEL'S ADVICE:** Only fade out concrete and semi-concrete representations as students become accurate with doing the work abstractly. If students do not fully understand the concepts, then fading out is not appropriate.

**Third — and this is the finding that should actually change what you do — which material you pick matters more than when you take it away.** Trninic, Kapur & Sinha (2020) showed the famous "abstract examples beat concrete" result dissolved once the concrete examples were improved: "the previously reported 'advantage of abstract examples' manifested not because abstract examples are advantageous in general, but because the concrete condition employed suboptimal examples" [T2]. And from the opposite direction, Stacey, Helme, Archer & Condon (2001) — **the only decimal-specific manipulative comparison that exists** — compared base-ten blocks (MAB) against Linear Arithmetic Blocks with 30 matched students in two teaching experiments. Finding: "**LAB is considerably more accessible for students**," attributed to three factors — "(1) LAB modeling number with **length rather than volume**, (2) MAB incorporating an **apparent dimensional shift**, and (3) [MAB] **having prior use**." Use of LAB "was associated with more active engagement by students and deeper discussion" [T2].

Read that precisely: the reported outcomes are **accessibility, engagement, and discussion quality — not achievement, and no effect size.** The paper does not establish that LAB outperforms base-ten blocks on a learning measure. What it does establish is a defensible structural argument: decimals are a magnitude-on-a-line idea, length is the attribute that carries magnitude, and **prior whole-number use of base-ten blocks actively interferes** because students arrive holding "unit cube = one." Note that the WWC's own decimal example (reassigning flat = one, rod = tenth, cube = hundredth) walks straight into that. If you use it, name the reassignment out loud as the point of the lesson.

**The recommendation.**

Use a **length model with the notation beside it from the first minute**, withdraw per student on demonstrated accuracy, keep the model available on request. Concretely:

- **What the concrete stage represents:** magnitude as continuous length, and the base-ten chain (cut a tenth into ten → a hundredth; cut that into ten → a thousandth; discuss what comes next even though you'll never build it). The material makes it *impossible* to conclude that 0.478 exceeds 0.8. It lets students do wrong: nothing about multiplication, and only selected quotitive divisions.
- **What students draw:** the same comparison as marked segments on a number line, **with the tenths gradations drawn in**, and an annotation naming which digit decided it. The drawing must preserve *relative length* and *the partition*. Both halves matter — the 0.57 effect size came from markings **and** a prompt to attend to the tenths digit; either alone was null.
- **The fade trigger:** not a date. The student **predicts** the comparison correctly before building it, twice in a row. Then the exit ticket on **ragged-length** items — equal-length items will pass a student who has learned nothing.
- **The return signal:** any answer that's wrong by an order of magnitude, or any explanation that names only the operation without the size of the numbers. Make going back to the strips a *move a strong student makes*, so it never reads as a demotion — this matters a lot for 6th graders' willingness to use it.
- **Model sequence: length before area.** Young-Loveridge & Mills argue linear before area because 1-D is easier than 2-D, and because a linear model can be un-built back to the whole where a cut decimat cannot. Then area for multiplication, which length genuinely cannot do.

**Can one teacher run this in one 55-minute period with a mixed-readiness room?** Yes — this is actually *cheaper* than a strict CRA schedule, because you are not managing three parallel stages. Everyone has the same materials on the desk; what varies is who is still touching them. The one real cost is the ~40-minute one-time build for deciwire, which can be an aide job, an after-school job, or a first-week activity that doubles as an introduction to tenths and hundredths.

### 7. Scaffolding and differentiation

**The prerequisite chain, with diagnostic items.**

| Prerequisite | Diagnostic item | What each wrong answer tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **4.NF.C.7** — compare decimals to hundredths | *"Which is larger, 0.4 or 0.83? Explain."* | "0.83, because 83 > 4" → passes by luck on a broken rule (longer-is-larger, will fail 0.5 vs 0.36). "0.4, because tenths are bigger than hundredths" → **shorter-is-larger; place value is intact, do not reteach it.** "0.83, because 0.4 is 40 hundredths and 83 > 40" → secure. |
| **5.NBT.A.1** — each place is 1/10 the place to its left | *"3 tenths is how many hundredths?"* | Blank or "3" → no re-unitizing between adjacent columns. **Only 39% of 6th graders answer this correctly** (Rittle-Johnson & Koedinger, 2009). This is the item that most often explains an alignment failure. |
| **5.NBT.A.3** — compare decimals to thousandths | *"Order: 0.3, 0.4, 0.38, 0.312"* | Any ordering that tracks digit count. Ragged by design — an equal-length set diagnoses nothing. |
| **4.NF.C.6** — decimal notation for fractions | *"Write 1/5 as a decimal."* | "0.5" → the classic; **38% of 13-year-olds chose it** in the NAEP-era data, the same proportion that answered correctly. "1.5" → >25% of 7th graders in a US national survey. |
| **5.NBT.B.5 / B.6** — whole-number × and ÷ fluency | *"48 × 7" and "852 ÷ 4"* | Slow-but-correct is a **fluency bottleneck**, not a conceptual gap — see below. |

**Distinguish the gap types, because they get different responses.**

- **Conceptual gap — the model was never built.** Longer-is-larger and density errors live here. Cannot be scaffolded around; requires the length model. Budget days, not minutes.
- **Procedural gap — the model exists, the routine is slow or error-prone.** Alignment errors in a student who *can* answer "3 tenths is how many hundredths" live here. Responds to worked examples, correct/incorrect example pairs, and practice structure. Budget minutes.
- **Fluency bottleneck — can do it, but so slowly that working memory is consumed.** A student who can reason about `2.4 × 0.6` but stalls on 24 × 6 is not having a decimal problem. **Give them the multiplication chart** so the cognitive load lands on the decimal placement. That is a load argument, not lowered expectations, and it is worth framing that way for anyone reviewing the plan.

**Acceleration over remediation** [T3 — practitioner/policy analysis, not experimental]. TNTP & Zearn's *Accelerate, Don't Remediate* (2021), analyzing ~6,000 elementary classrooms of platform data, reported that students who received grade-level content with just-in-time support completed **27% more grade-level lessons** than students who started at the same level but received remediation — **49% more** for students of color and low-income students, who were also more likely to be assigned remediation *even when they had already demonstrated success on grade-level content*. Observational, non-randomized, elementary — the confounds are obvious. But the direction converges with WWC Recommendation 1, and *The Opportunity Myth* (TNTP, 2018) [T3] adds a measurement warning worth internalizing: students succeeded on more than two-thirds of assignments given, but met grade-level standards only **17%** of the time on those same assignments. **Pin your exit tickets to 6.NS language, not to whether the worksheet got finished.**

Concretely for this unit: do not run a two-week place-value re-teach before touching 6.NS. Re-anchor "the decimal point marks the ones column" for five minutes immediately before the alignment lesson, and let the length model carry the students who need it through grade-level problems.

**Differentiation that is not three lessons.** Every structure in §5 is a single task with varying support. The number choices do the differentiating: within one problem set, `4.6 + 2.3` and `6 + 0.32` are the same task at very different difficulties. Strategic partnering — the Melbourne materials and the Suh et al. Title 1 lesson both pair a stronger and a less-strong student deliberately. Correct/incorrect example pairs give a model to students who need one before attempting, and the evidence says low-prior-knowledge students benefit **as much** as high.

**Above grade level, deepening rather than accelerating.** Reversibility: *"What would the second number have to be for the answer to be exactly 1?"* Generalization: from the digit-box task, describe the **whole solution set** — if the ones digit on the left is 0, 1, or 2 then the tenths and hundredths can be anything; if it's 3, the tenths digit becomes decisive. Error-finding: hand them the wrong work and ask them to diagnose *which rule* the fictional student was using. That last one is the extension that most directly serves your culture — it makes a student think about how someone else was thinking.

**The dose is small, which is the most encouraging finding here.** The Melbourne group ran a **one-hour** teacher PD on the length model plus targeted lesson plans in four low-achieving schools; three declined ("too busy — we know this feeling"). In the one school that took it, two classes did no decimal work and showed **no change**; two classes used the model on **one to four occasions** in August and were retested in late October: "**about one half of the children who had a misconception moved to expertise, and all the experts stayed as experts**" [T2/T3 — Helme & Stacey (2000), as reported in the project materials; the class-by-class figures run Class M 33%→63% experts, Class S 83%→100%, Classes X/Y and Z unchanged]. Small sample, no randomization, teacher self-selection. But the effective dose in this literature is **four well-targeted lessons on a diagnosed misconception**, not a rebuilt unit.

### 8. Open questions and weak spots

- **The single biggest gap: there is no concreteness-fading research on decimals, and no CRA research on decimal *operations*.** Everything in §6 about the fade is imported from mathematical equivalence, modular arithmetic, algebra, and physics. I flagged it rather than papering over it, and it is the reason the §6 recommendation is stated as "the evidence does not support a schedule" rather than "the evidence supports this schedule."
- **The Melbourne prevalence data is Australian, mixed-SES, and from 1995–99.** It is by far the largest and best dataset on decimal misconceptions in existence (3,204 students, ~10,000 test administrations, longitudinal). It is not a US Title 1 sample and it is nearly thirty years old. The US data that exists (Resnick et al., Hiebert & Wearne, Lortie-Forgues & Siegler) points the same direction with smaller samples, which is reassuring but not the same as replication.
- **Siegler's famous longitudinal result does not cover decimals.** I searched the full text of Siegler et al. (2012), *Psychological Science* — the study showing elementary fraction and division knowledge uniquely predicts high-school algebra (UK n = 3,677; a 1-SD increase in fractions knowledge associated with 0.15-SD in later algebra, net of IQ, working memory, and SES). **The word "decimal" appears zero times.** Anyone citing it as evidence for decimals is extending it past what it measured. The closest decimal-specific substitute is Schneider, Grabner & Paetsch (2009), n = 429 fifth and sixth graders, where number-line estimation **tested with decimal fractions** predicted mathematics achievement — but correlationally, not longitudinally [T2].
- **The Hiebert & Wearne (1985) figures are second-hand.** The 74%/12% split and the 670-student sample come from Lortie-Forgues, Tian & Siegler's (2015) retrieved review, not the primary source. The finding is reported consistently across multiple retrieved sources, but I did not read the original.
- **Small samples on the sequencing recommendation.** Rittle-Johnson & Koedinger's iterating result is the right grade and right topic, but the effect sizes come from n = 77 and n = 26. Direction is reliable; magnitude is not.
- **One unresolved conflict I could not close.** Hiebert, Wearne & Taber (1991), "Fourth graders' gradual construction of decimal fractions during instruction using different physical representations," appears in the Stacey et al. reference list, and a search snippet attributed to it a claim that low-achieving 4th graders responded **better to discrete (base-ten block) than continuous (number line) decimal representations** — which would directly contradict the length-over-volume recommendation in §6. **I could not retrieve the paper and could not verify that the claim comes from it.** I have not relied on it. It is the highest-value gap left: a decimal-specific comparison of representation types with low achievers is exactly the study this brief most needs.
- **Marked `[unverified]`:** Renkl, Atkinson & Große (2004) on faded worked examples — citation verified via CrossRef, findings not retrieved from source. The **expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003)** was not retrieved at all and is not cited anywhere above. Sackur-Grisvard & Léonard's (1985) Rule 1 prevalence figures (~40% of 4th graders, ~25% of 5th) come from an OCR'd scan of Resnick's summary where the percent signs render as "96" — I read them as 40%/25%, but treat those two numbers as provisional. Rule 2 (<6%) and Rule 3 (8%/14%) are unambiguous in the scan.
- **Watch this one downstream:** a web search summary claimed Agrawal & Morin (2016) demonstrates "strong effect sizes across multiple grade levels" for CRA. **The actual abstract makes no such claim** — it is a practitioner overview plus a case study with no sample and no pooled analysis. If that number-free claim acquires numbers somewhere, it did not get them from the source.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

This maps onto the 55-minute CRA spine without distortion, with **two exceptions named honestly at the end.**

### Where the pieces land

| State | Decimal-unit use |
|---|---|
| `warmup` (0–5) | 2 review + 3 current. Review slots carry the **prerequisite diagnostics** from §7 — they are the cheapest diagnostic delivery you have. Third current question is always the misconception item. |
| `launch` (5–9) | The hook is an estimation prompt, not a computation. "Will this be bigger or smaller than 6? Don't compute." |
| `review` (10–14) | Where the five-minute just-in-time re-anchor lives — "the decimal point marks the ones column." Skip it on days 5+ and give the minutes to `concrete`. |
| `concrete` (14–20) | Deciwire / paper strips, in pairs, **with the numeral written beside the model from the first minute** (this is the WWC simultaneity rule, not a CRA violation). |
| `discussion` (4 min, when used) | The "insight with no work" slot is where "you built the one with more digits and it came out shorter" gets said by a student, not by you. Capture two noticings verbatim and reuse the exact wording in the R demo. |
| `representational` (20–27) | `/number-line-plus`. Demo one, students run stated reps. The 0.57 finding says the line must show **tenths gradations** and the prompt must name the deciding digit. |
| `abstract` (27–34) | Notation alone. Model one, class works one. |
| `learning-check` (35–38) | Two problems, and they must be **ragged-length**. Equal-length items will route a student who has learned nothing. |
| `small-group` (38–51) | Routed by misconception, not by score. The two comparison errors get **opposite** small-group moves — see §4. |
| `exit` (51–54) | Independent evidence, ragged items, one plausibility item. |

### Warm-up misconception targets, day by day

Third current question each day, with the misconception named:

1. **0.25 vs 0.5** — longer-is-larger
2. **0.4 vs 0.83** — shorter-is-larger (denominator-focused)
3. **0.3 vs 0.4** — reciprocal thinking. *This is the item everyone skips because it looks trivial.*
4. **0.6 vs 0** — negative thinking (the only item that separates it from reciprocal)
5. **6 + 0.32** — right-edge alignment, placed directly under `4.6 + 2.3` as review Q2
6. **"How many numbers between 3.14 and 3.15?"** — discreteness. Score three ways.
7. **True or false: 0.77 × 0.63 > 0.77. Explain.** — multiplication makes bigger. *The explanation is the diagnostic.*
8. **4.45 vs 4.4502** — money thinking
9. **Write 1/5 as a decimal** — decimal–fraction translation
10. **0.9 vs 0.533** — digit count ignoring the point

### Misconception tags

The proficiency spine uses a finite 18-tag exact-match vocabulary and **adding a tag is a SQL migration**. Before authoring any of these lessons, check whether the `misconceptions` table already carries tags for longer-is-larger, shorter-is-larger, reciprocal/negative thinking, right-edge alignment, multiplication-makes-bigger, and discreteness. Anything missing needs the migration first, or the tag typed into Notion never clusters and `/teacher/rightnow` renders a blank prepared move.

### Two places the research does not fit the spine

**1. The diagnostic day does not have a home.** The Decimal Comparison Test is a 10-minute whole-class instrument whose *pattern* of responses — not score — routes the next four days. It is not a warm-up (too long, wrong shape), not a learning check (it precedes instruction), and not a lesson. **What it would take:** one `review`-state day-one variant where `review` runs 10–20 and `concrete` shrinks, or a practice-day slot the week before the unit opens. I would not try to spread it across five warm-ups — the diagnosis depends on seeing the whole response pattern at once, and split across a week you lose the students who shift.

**2. Direction of effect is a practice-day topic, not a CRA day.** "Is `0.77 × 0.63` bigger than 0.77?" has no concrete stage that helps — the area model shows *why* the answer is smaller once you already accept it, but the misconception is a verbal over-generalization and the fix is a sentence structure. It fits your **practice-day** shape much better: error analysis run extensively, students diagnosing which rule a fictional student used, with something written at the end that could be graded. Slotting it into a CRA day would force a manipulative onto a problem that does not have one, which is exactly the distortion worth avoiding.
