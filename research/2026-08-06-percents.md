# Percents — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Standard(s):** 6.RP.A.3c (find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100; solve problems involving finding the whole, given a part and the percent)
**Compiled:** 2026-08-06
**Module map note:** In the CCSD Grade 6 Acc Teacher Clarity Guides, percent lives in **Module 2 Topic 2, "Percents, Unit Rates and Conversions"** — six learning progressions covering percent, unit rate, *and* measurement conversion in one topic. Topic 3 is The Statistical Process (6.SP), not percents.

**Scope:** Covers percent as a rate per 100, the three problem types (find the part, find the percent, find the whole), benchmark decomposition, the percentage bar, and — because you named it — a direct assessment of the "___ is ___% of ___" positional proportion template with cross-multiplication. Deliberately left out: percent increase and decrease, simple interest, and percent of change, which are 7.RP.A.3 rather than grade 6 work.

**Evidence at a glance:** 6 Tier 1 · 8 Tier 2 · 3 Tier 3 · 1 Tier 4 sources retrieved this session. *(Tier definitions: **T1** = meta-analysis, RCT, or a WWC recommendation rated Strong or Moderate. **T2** = peer-reviewed study with a smaller sample or weaker design, well-designed qualitative or design-based research, or a WWC recommendation rated Minimal. **T3** = practitioner and professional consensus. **T4** = anecdotal or practitioner brief; never carries an effectiveness claim.)* **Percent is the least-evidenced sub-topic in the entire 6.RP standard.** There is no meta-analysis of percent instruction at any grade level — verified directly against ERIC. What does exist is unusually pointed for your purposes: large-sample data on *which* percent problems break and why, a documented decline in percent performance across three decades, and a clear convergence of expert design documents against the procedure you are currently using. The recommendation in this brief rests on that convergence plus measured student error patterns, not on an efficacy trial, and is labeled that way.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*Read this first. About ten minutes — the first two sections alone will get you through a day.*

### The short version

Percent is where the wheels come off, and there is data. In a study of 677 Year 8 and 9 students, only **62%** knew that 29 out of 100 is 29%, and only **39%** could find 30% of 60 — barely better than the 29% who managed a deliberately hard item. The authors' comment is the one to sit with: "the facilities are very much lower than desirable for a topic central to basic numeracy... Simple unit fraction equivalents seem to be emphasised, at the expense of fundamental definition ('out of a hundred')."

**About your current approach.** You teach percent by setting up `___ is ___% of ___` as a proportion with positional rules — first blank top left, second top right, third bottom left, percents with percents and numbers with numbers — then cross-multiplying and dividing. The template is not wrong and it is not a bad idea; it is a real algorithm that gets a right answer when it is set up correctly. The problem the research identifies is narrower and more specific: **it is the setup that fails, and the positional rule is precisely the thing that fails.** A documented student error, from a study of middle schoolers on proportion tasks: the student writes `12/32 = x/8` correctly, then "made the reduction between the incorrect number pairs" and produces `32/6 = x/6`. Another, from the percent literature: asked for 24 out of 500, the student computes **500 ÷ 24** — the bigger number went where their rule said the bigger number goes. The template is a memory aid for a placement decision, and when the memory aid is the only thing holding the placement, there is nothing to catch it when it slips.

The evidence's convergent recommendation is not "stop using it." It is that **the template cannot be the thing that organizes the topic** — it has to arrive as one option on a menu, after a representation that makes the relationship visible. The CCSS Progressions states flatly that "the Standards do not require this method in Grade 6" and that the traditional proportion setup "obscures" the constant of proportionality. NCTM recommends "delaying the instruction of the cross-multiplication algorithm until students have already gained experience with forming ratios and understanding proportions as an equivalence of ratios."

**And here is the encouraging part: the alternative is already in your district guide.** CCSD progression 3.2 and 3.3 already ask for double number lines to find the part and the whole, and progression 2.1 already asks for benchmark percents — 100%, 50%, 25%, 20%, 10%, and a rule for 1%. That is the research-supported approach, sitting in the clarity guide you already have. The change is one of *order and emphasis*, not of content: lead with the bar, let the proportion arrive late.

### Where to spend the time

- **Percent as parts of a bar, drawn before anything is computed.** The single most striking finding in the retrieved literature: given blank scrap paper and told to use it, **none of 14 students drew any model at all.** "Obviously arranging the relations between the given data was done 'in their heads', and often that went wrong." The bar has to be a required routine, not an offered option.
- **Benchmark decomposition — 50%, 10%, 5%, 1% — as the primary computational method.** This is the route through which "15% of 600" becomes 60 + 30 = 90 rather than a proportion to solve. It is also what makes the number sense visible enough that a wrong setup gets caught.
- **Finding the whole, given a part and the percent.** Documented as the hardest of the three problem types, and it is the one where the template's positional rule breaks most often. It also demands division by a decimal, which collides with a well-attested misconception (below).

**What gets compressed to pay for it:** the fraction-decimal-percent conversion drilling (CCSD 1.2, 1.4) — keep the benchmark set, drop the exhaustive matching; percent as a fourth notation for ratio (CCSD 1.3.b, 1.3.d) — one mention, not a lesson; and the "which method is more efficient" comparison (CCSD 3.1.a), which the comparison research says is a *consolidation* move for students who already own a method, not an introduction.

### The sequence

1. **Percent as "out of 100," on a bar, qualitatively.** *Makes possible:* the fundamental definition the retrieved data says is missing.
2. **Benchmark percents and their decomposition.** *Makes possible:* mental computation and the number sense that catches a wrong answer.
3. **The percentage bar as a required drawing routine, on all three problem types.** *Makes possible:* the relationship becoming visible before any operation is chosen — which is the actual failure point.
4. **Finding the whole.** *Makes possible:* the hardest case, given its own day rather than being appended.
5. **Proportion and cross-multiplication, if at all, as a late option.** *Makes possible:* nothing new — it is a compression of what students can already do, and it is optional in grade 6.

### The three misconceptions to plan for

| The wrong answer you will actually see | The task that surfaces it early |
|---|---|
| "€600 bike, 15% discount, what do you pay?" → student computes **600 ÷ 15** (8 of 14 students did this) | Use 15%, 35%, 11% — never 25%, 50%, 75%. Friendly percentages let students succeed by fraction-matching without engaging the relationship at all |
| "24 out of 500 is what percent?" → computes **500 ÷ 24.** The bigger number went where the rule put it | Give the same numbers in three different questions and ask which is being found — part, percent, or whole — before anyone computes |
| "60 books, 20 taken. What percent remains?" → answers **"20"** instead of "20%" or the correct value — 31% of 14-year-olds dropped the sign | Any problem where a bare number and a percent are both plausible answers. Ask what the number counts |

### The progression at a glance

Not CRA. The 2025 CRA meta-analysis covers 30 single-case studies and 116 students and contains **zero** ratio, proportion, or percent studies — see the ratios brief §6. The progression below is the percentage-bar trajectory from Dutch Realistic Mathematics Education, which is expert design work, not an efficacy trial.

| Stage | Students do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| **Qualitative bar** | Shade and estimate. "About what percent is shaded?" No computation. | The student can place 30% on an unmarked bar and defend it against 25% and 50%. |
| **Benchmark bar** | Mark 0%, 100%, and the known quantity. Build the answer from 50%, 10%, 5%, 1%. | The student marks 0% and 100% *unprompted* on a new problem. |
| **Bar as working surface** | Use the bar to hold intermediate steps — 50% → 300, 10% → 60, 5% → 30 — and combine them. | The student uses the bar on all three problem types without being told which type it is. |
| **Bar as thinking tool, then symbolic** | Compute directly, drawing only when stuck. | The student predicts the answer's size before computing — and *returns to the bar* voluntarily when a problem resists. |

**The critical caution about this progression:** the same bar in two teachers' hands can produce two different mathematical objects. An exploratory case study — Büscher (2021), retrieved at abstract level only, so weigh it accordingly — documented **"two fundamentally different utilization schemes"** — *Partitioning and Counting*, which "creates learning opportunities for the conceptual core of percentages and proportional reasoning," and *Mirror Movement*, which "only addresses scaling." The bar is not self-executing. What makes it work is the routine you enforce: mark 0%, mark 100%, mark what you know, *then* decide what to do.

### If they're two years behind

The prerequisites that actually block percent are **"out of 100" as a meaning rather than a conversion rule**, **multiplication and division by 10 and 100**, and **division with a decimal divisor** — the last of which is the required grade 6 fluency 6.NS.B.3 and is the direct gate on finding the whole.

Diagnostics for your two warm-up review problems: (1) "29 out of 100 students walk to school. What percent walk?" — only **62% of Year 9 students** answered it correctly in the retrieved data, so do not assume it. A wrong answer here means the fundamental definition is missing and nothing downstream will hold. (2) "What is 10% of 340?" then "What is 1% of 340?" — a student who can do the first and not the second has a place-value gap, not a percent gap.

**Just-in-time.** The "out of 100" meaning is conceptual, cannot be scaffolded around, and is worth a full re-anchor day if the diagnostic comes back badly. The decimal-division fluency should be **scaffolded around** with a calculator, used consistently including on the check — Ellington's meta-analysis of 54 studies found calculator use "did not hinder the development of mathematical skills," and that results went mixed only when calculators were *not* part of assessment. 6.RP.A.3c assesses percent reasoning; decimal division is a confound.

### Watch out for

- **Friendly percentages are hiding your problem.** This is the highest-leverage design change available and it costs nothing. Van Galen and van Eerde are explicit: "Problems with 25%, 75% and 20% can easily be solved by finding an equal proportion... That is not possible, however, with percentages like 15% and 35%." A unit built on quarters and halves will show you a class that looks competent. CCSD progression 2.1 lists exactly 100%, 50%, 25%, 20%, 10% as the benchmarks — teach those as *tools*, then assess with 15%, 35% and 11%.
- **Percent gains wash out.** In the one RCT that measured percent separately, the intervention improved percent scores at posttest for students with mathematics difficulties (0.32 SD) but **"treatment was not a significant predictor of percent scores on the delayed posttest"** at nine weeks — while ratio and proportion gains held. Treating percent as taught-and-done is contraindicated by the only trial that checked. It needs distributed re-exposure in the warm-up spiral for the rest of the semester.
- **CCSD success criterion 3.4.a** — "I can use an inverse operation, or division of the part by the percent, to determine the whole value" — points directly at the hardest documented item in the percent literature. In Baratta et al.'s 677-student study, the single hardest question was **accepting `145 ÷ 0.11` as a valid method**, attributed to the *Multiplication Makes Bigger, Division Makes Smaller* misconception. Notably, three of six surveyed Year 8 textbooks **avoided this method entirely**, apparently to dodge the problem. Teaching it is right; teaching it without the bar underneath it is teaching the thing students trust least.
- **The positional mnemonic is the weakest part of the template.** Not cross-multiplication itself — the *placement rule*. It is a memory aid holding a decision that should be made by reading the situation, and when it slips there is nothing to catch it. Even if you keep the template, replace "first blank goes top left" with "draw the bar, then write what you see."
- **Performance on percent items has measurably declined.** In a replication comparing nationally representative English samples from 1976 and 2008–09 (N ≈ 7,000), three of four percent items dropped: 85.7% → 70.6%, 45.5% → 32.3%, 39.6% → 29.5%. The one that *improved* — reduce £20 by 5%, and the rise was **not statistically significant** — the authors attribute to "greater emphasis being placed on mental and other 'informal' methods for calculating percentages." **The item that got better is the one least likely to be attacked with a proportion template.** That is not proof of anything, but it points the same direction as everything else here.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. What the research supports

**Start with the fundamental definition, because the data says it is missing.** Baratta et al. (2010) [T2] surveyed 677 Year 8 and 9 students across four Australian schools spanning an SES range, using calibrated items. Only **62% of Year 9 students knew that 29 out of 100 is 29%.** Only **39% could find 30% of 60** — and the authors flag this specifically because they had expected it to be easy: "we had expected that finding 30% of 60 could be handled by many students mentally (10% is 6, so 30% is 18) but the facility (39%) of this question is very close to the facility (29%) for the difficult number question." The mental scaling route was simply not there. Their conclusion names the cause: "Simple unit fraction equivalents seem to be emphasised, at the expense of fundamental definition ('out of a hundred')."

Two further findings from that study shape everything downstream. **The difficulty order is find-part < find-percent < find-whole** — the three problem types are not equivalent and should not be taught as one lesson. And students "were markedly better at **rejecting wrong** calculations (54% average) than **accepting right** ones (36% average)." Recognition of a valid method is the weak link, not error-spotting. That is a diagnostic asymmetry worth exploiting: a student who cannot generate a method may still be able to evaluate one, which makes "is this a valid way to solve it?" a more accessible entry than "solve it."

**Students will not draw a model unless required to.** Van Galen and van Eerde (2013) [T2] gave 14 grade 7 students a 15-item percentage test with designated scrap-paper areas and explicit instructions to use them. "Almost all students wrote calculations in these areas. **None of them, however, used the scrap paper to draw a picture or model** to clarify the relations between the given numbers. Obviously arranging the relations between the given data was done 'in their heads', and often that went wrong." On "a bike that normally costs €600 with a 15% discount," only 4 of 14 were correct, and **8 of 14 divided 600 by 15.**

Their diagnosis of why is the most transferable sentence in the percent literature: "the fact that... so many students divided 600 by 15... can be explained by the **expectation of students that within a test calculations will be kept simple.** They see that 100 cannot be divided by 15 easily, but 600 : 15 is doable. We may conclude that **we give students too often problems with friendly numbers and this prevents them from trying to understand what is being asked.**"

That is a claim about *curriculum design*, not about students, and it is directly actionable: the number choices in your problem sets are teaching students that percent problems are solved by finding the division that works out nicely.

**The percentage bar is the recommended representation, and the recommendation is design judgment rather than trial evidence.** Van Galen and van Eerde name three advantages: it forces the student to represent the relation between what is given and what is asked; it "offers scrap paper for the intermediate steps," so 50% → 300, 10% → 60, 5% → 30 can be laid down and combined; and it "offers a natural entry to calculating via 1%." They explicitly compare it to the percentage table and the double number line and state their preference: "These two models offer to some extent the same advantages as the bar. We prefer the bar because it gives a clear and concrete picture of the relations between the total and its parts." Their own claim is appropriately modest — students "immediately understood its purpose and most of them could solve problems that they probably could not have done otherwise" — and the study is n = 14 with a single teaching lesson. **Illustrative, not evidential.**

The CCSS Progressions [T3] independently recommends the same object, describing it as "a **blend between a tape diagram and a double number line diagram**," and works percent as *parts*: 75% of a budget is $1,200, so 75% is 3 parts, 25% is 1 part = $400, 100% = $1,600. Percent is handled as partitioning, not as an operator. This matters because it means "find the whole" is not a different procedure — it is reading the same picture from a different starting mark.

**The bar is not self-executing.** Büscher (2021) [T2 — exploratory case study, retrieved at abstract level] studied teachers' adaptations of the percentage bar and found **"two fundamentally different utilization schemes."** *Partitioning and Counting* "creates learning opportunities for the conceptual core of percentages and proportional reasoning." *Mirror Movement* "only addresses scaling." Same bar, two teachers, two different mathematical objects. His implication: "teacher educators need to provide new routines along with new materials." So the actionable version is a routine, stated as a rule: **mark 0%, mark 100%, mark what you know, then decide.** Not "here is a bar, use it if it helps."

**Percent gains do not persist.** [T1] In Jitendra et al.'s randomized trial of 806 grade 7 students with mathematics difficulties, the intervention produced 0.32 SD on the percent subscale at posttest — but at the nine-week delayed posttest, "treatment was not a significant predictor of percent scores," while the ratio and proportion subscale gains *held* (0.30 SD, still significant). Percent is the fragile one. Whatever you teach, plan the re-exposure before the unit ends.

**Percent performance has declined measurably over three decades.** [T1] Hodgen et al. (2024) compared nationally representative English samples from 1976–77 and 2008–09 (N ≈ 7,000 in the later sample) using the same CSMS Ratio test items with only minor language updating. Three of the four percent items declined: "4 out of 100 children, what percentage?" 85.7% → 70.6%; "6% of 250 children?" 45.5% → 32.3%; "24 out of 800 Avenger cars, what percentage?" 39.6% → 29.5%. The overall Ratio test fell from 47.0% to 40.0% mean facility with an effect size of −0.29, 17 of 24 items declining and none increasing significantly. Non-responses on ratio items rose from 7.6% to 17.9%.

The exception is instructive. The one percent item that improved — "£20 coat reduced by 5%, cost now?" 26.5% → 33.5% — the authors attribute to "greater emphasis being placed on mental and other 'informal' methods for calculating percentages." That item is the one a student is most likely to attack by taking 10% and halving it, and least likely to attack with a proportion setup. **This is suggestive, not causal, and should be presented that way.**

### 2. Emphasis — where the leverage is

**a. The fundamental "out of 100" meaning, on measured absence.** When only 62% of Year 9 students can say that 29 out of 100 is 29%, nothing built on top of it is stable. This is the cheapest thing to check and the most expensive thing to skip. It also has a diagnostic advantage: it takes one warm-up question and the answer is unambiguous.

**b. Finding the whole, on documented difficulty and misconception density.** The three problem types are not equal — Baratta et al. found find-part easiest, find-percent next, find-whole hardest — and find-the-whole is where two documented misconceptions converge. It requires either division by a decimal (colliding with *Multiplication Makes Bigger, Division Makes Smaller*, the source of the single hardest item in that 677-student study) or a scale-up on the bar. And it is exactly where a positional template fails, because the unknown is in the position the rule assumes is known. CCSD gives it one learning intention (3.4); the evidence says it deserves its own day.

**c. Benchmark decomposition as the primary method, on the number-sense argument.** Building 15% as 10% + 5% does two things at once: it computes the answer, and it leaves the student with a *sense of the size* of the answer, which is the only thing that catches a reversed operation. Students who compute 600 ÷ 15 = 40 for a 15% discount on €600 have produced a number with no felt relationship to 600. A student who has taken 10% (60) cannot then accept 40 as a 15% discount. This is the mechanism behind the SBI estimate-first step, which is part of the package with 0.46 SD behind it [T1].

**What gets less time, and why it is defensible.** Fraction-decimal-percent conversion drilling (CCSD 1.2.d–f, 1.4.a–c: thirds, eighths, tenths, matched three ways) is a memorization set. Keep the benchmark subset — halves, quarters, tenths, fifths — because those are the tools for decomposition; drop the exhaustive matching, which the retrieved data suggests is actively crowding out the fundamental definition. Percent as a fourth notation for ratio (CCSD 1.3.b, 1.3.d) is one sentence, not a lesson. And CCSD 3.1.a — comparing "multiplying a whole by a decimal equivalent" against "using benchmark fractions" and deciding which is more efficient — is a *comparison of solution methods*, which Durkin, Star and Rittle-Johnson's synthesis [T2] found effective "among students with some prior knowledge of one of the methods" and overwhelming for students without it. Move it to the end of the topic, or drop it for the students who need it least.

### 3. Sequence and why

**1. Percent as "out of 100," qualitatively, on a bar.** *Purpose:* install the definition the data says is missing. *Makes possible:* everything, and nothing works without it. CCSD 1.1 already does this ("estimate percents from pictures") — do not skip it as warm-up material.

**2. Benchmark percents and decomposition.** *Purpose:* the computational engine and the number-sense check simultaneously. *Makes possible:* mental estimation, which is the only defense against a wrong setup. CCSD 2.1.a already asks for "a rule to determine 1% or 10% of a number" — that rule is the load-bearing one.

**3. The bar as a required routine, across all three problem types together.** *Purpose:* make the relationship visible *before* an operation is chosen. *Makes possible:* the three problem types being one picture read from different starting marks, rather than three procedures. This is the Progressions' framing and it is the reason to teach them together rather than sequentially.

**4. Finding the whole, its own day.** *Purpose:* the hardest case with the most misconceptions, given room. *Makes possible:* honest assessment — a unit that only asks students to find the part has not assessed 6.RP.A.3c, which explicitly names "solve problems involving finding the whole, given a part and the percent."

**5. Proportion setup and cross-multiplication — late, optional, and as one of several.** *Purpose:* a compression of what students can already do. *Makes possible:* nothing new in grade 6. The Progressions: "the Standards do not require this method in Grade 6."

#### Where the research disagrees — and this is the section about your template

There are genuinely two positions here and they are worth laying out properly, because the honest answer is not "your method is wrong."

**Position one: delay or omit cross-multiplication in grade 6.** This is the position of every expert design document retrieved.

- The **CCSS Progressions** [T3]: "Although it is traditional to move students quickly to solving proportions by setting up an equation, **the Standards do not require this method in Grade 6.**" And, at grade 7, the reason: such problems can be framed in terms of "the constant of proportionality or unit rate, **which is obscured by the traditional method of setting up proportions.**"
- **NCTM's research brief** [T2]: teachers should promote proportional reasoning "by **delaying the instruction of the cross-multiplication algorithm** until students have already gained experience with forming ratios and understanding proportions as an equivalence of ratios."
- The **WWC fractions practice guide** [T1 guide, but this recommendation rated **Minimal Evidence**]: Recommendation 4 is to "develop students' conceptual understanding of strategies for solving ratio, rate, and proportion problems **before** exposing them to cross-multiplication." The companion PD transcript adds two specific warnings: "Once they have learned about cross-multiplication, students may attempt to apply the procedure **without regard for whether a problem is set up correctly.** Also, by using cross-multiplication exclusively, students may **fail to recognize more efficient ways** to solve a proportion."
- **Van Dooren, Vamvakoussi and Verschaffel's** IBE-UNESCO synthesis [T2] is the most direct. They are scrupulously fair first — "All strategies that were described above lead in principle to a correct answer to any proportional missing-value problem" — and then: "The strategies of creating equivalent fractions and cross-multiplication have as a major advantage that they are algorithmic in nature... **However, research points out that students themselves rarely choose them, and mistakes are very common. One of the main causes seems to be that these algorithms consist of the blind manipulation of numbers according to formal rules that have no transparent relation whatsoever with the original problem context.**" Their section heading: "Creating equivalent fractions and cross-multiplication are powerful methods but are not transparent to the learner."
- **Battista and Van Auken Borrow**, as quoted in the NC2ML practitioner brief [T4]: students are "not ready to understand why cross multiplication works until they have studied properties of algebra," which is later than middle school.

**Position two: teach it as one strategy on a menu.** This is what the *strongest experimental evidence* actually did. Jitendra's schema-based instruction — the only large RCT base on this content, 1,999 students, 0.46 SD — explicitly had teachers "encourage students to deeply process information about when, how, and why to use a **broad range of methods (e.g., equivalent fractions, unit rate, cross multiplication)** for a given class of problems," with selection based on the numbers. Cross-multiplication was in the room and the results were the best in the literature.

**What the disagreement hinges on:** whether the algorithm is the *organizing structure* of the topic or an *item on a menu the student chooses from*. Position one objects to the first; Jitendra implemented the second. Read that way they largely agree.

**Recommendation.** Keep the proportion template if you want it, but **move it to the end and strip the positional mnemonic.** Two reasons, and the second is the one I would defend hardest.

First, the empirical bridge. Fernández et al. (2010) [T2] studied 755 Spanish students, and the paper notes that the Spanish curriculum **introduces the rule of three (cross-multiplication in a proportion) in grades 5 and 6.** Those sixth graders had already been taught the algorithm, and **57.0% still solved proportional problems additively.** The algorithm did not install the concept. That is the closest thing to a direct test available, and it points one way.

Second, the specific failure is the setup, and the positional rule *is* the setup. The documented errors are placement errors, not computation errors:

- A student writes `12/32 = x/8` correctly, then "made the reduction between the incorrect number pairs" and produces `32/6 = x/6` — Çalışıcı (2018) [T3/T4], who notes "the students do not understand which number pairs among the four number pairs in the numerator and denominator to reduce."
- Asked for 24 out of 500, a student computes **500 ÷ 24** — the "percent means divide" error documented by Ryan and Williams and reported in Baratta et al. [T2]. The bigger number went where a rule put it.
- A student "thinks that the value of a fraction does not change when the same number is added to its numerator or denominator," and adds the difference between 32 and 16 to the other denominator — the additive misconception living *inside* the proportion template.
- The NC2ML brief [T4] states the mechanism: "Simple 'plugging in' numbers from a context into the a/b = c/d structure will often result in mistakes (e.g., inserting the incorrect numbers) or uninterpretable calculations **if the student does not first consider how the quantities relate.**"

A positional mnemonic is a memory aid for a placement decision. When the mnemonic is the *only* thing holding the placement, a slip produces a confidently wrong answer with nothing to catch it. Replace it with the bar: draw 0%, 100%, and what you know; *then* write the proportion if you want one. The bar is the "first consider how the quantities relate" step, made mandatory.

**What would flip me:** evidence that an explicit positional setup routine reduces setup errors relative to a representation-first routine. I searched for this and found nothing either way — it is a genuine open question and this recommendation is a bet on convergent design evidence plus the documented error taxonomy, not on a trial.

**One more thing worth saying plainly.** There is a real cost to changing this that the research does not weigh: you have a routine that works for *you*, that you can deliver consistently, and that students can execute. The retrieved evidence on teachers is relevant here — Van Dooren et al. report that "in-service teachers strongly favor the cross-multiplication approach... Often, they did not acknowledge the value of any of the other strategies... and considered them as less sophisticated or even wrong." That is not a criticism of you; it is a description of how strongly this method is embedded in the profession, and it is worth knowing that the pull toward it is systemic. The lowest-cost change is not abandoning the template. It is putting the bar in front of it and choosing harder percentages.

### 4. Misconceptions and how to prepare

**Misconception: divide the bigger number by the smaller (the "percent means divide" error)**
- **Looks like:** "€600 bike, 15% discount, what do you pay?" → **600 ÷ 15 = 40.** Or "24 out of 500 is what percent?" → **500 ÷ 24.**
- **Why it makes sense to them:** two previously-true rules collide. "Divide the bigger by the smaller" has never failed. And — this is the sharper diagnosis, from van Galen and van Eerde — the student expects a school problem to have a clean computation, sees that 100 does not divide 15 nicely, notices that 600 ÷ 15 does, and takes it. **The student is reasoning about the test, not about the mathematics, and they learned to do that from the problem sets.**
- **Evidence:** van Galen & van Eerde (2013) [T2], 8 of 14 students; Ryan & Williams via Baratta et al. (2010) [T2] for the 500 ÷ 24 form.
- **Surface it early with:** 15%, 35%, and 11%. Never 25%, 50%, or 75% on an assessment. The friendly percentages let the wrong reasoning produce the right answer.
- **When it shows up:** do not correct the operation. Ask for 10% first. A student who has said "10% is 60" cannot hold "15% is 40" in the same sentence. That is *what do you know* doing the work — the contradiction is theirs to notice, not yours to announce.

**Misconception: division makes smaller, so `145 ÷ 0.11` cannot be right**
- **Looks like:** rejecting a valid method. Given "11% of a number is 145, find the number," the student refuses `145 ÷ 0.11` because the answer would be bigger than 145, and reaches for multiplication instead.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** every division they have done for six years produced something smaller. The rule was true until decimals under 1 arrived.
- **Evidence:** Baratta et al. (2010) [T2], 677 students — this was **the single hardest question in the study**, attributed to MMBDMS (*Multiplication Makes Bigger, Division Makes Smaller*, citing Bell, Swan & Taylor, 1981). And revealingly, **three of six surveyed Year 8 textbooks avoided find-whole-by-division entirely**, apparently to dodge it. By contrast, `(145 ÷ 11) × 100` — the unitary method as a single calculation — was "relatively very well done."
- **Surface it early with:** estimation before computation on a find-the-whole problem. "11% of a number is 145. Is the number bigger or smaller than 145? How do you know?" The estimate makes the direction a *decision the student defends*, before any operation is chosen.
- **When it shows up:** go to the bar. If 11% is 145, then 1% is about 13, so 100% is about 1,300. The bar makes the answer's size obvious, and the division becomes a shortcut for something already known rather than an operation to be trusted on faith. **This is also why the unitary route through 1% is worth teaching as the primary method** — it produced far better results in the retrieved data than the decimal division did.

**Misconception: the percent sign dropped**
- **Looks like:** "60 books, 20 are taken. What percent remains?" → **"20."** Not 20% and not the correct value — the number without its meaning.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** the percent sign has been treated as notation rather than as a unit. Nothing in their experience makes "20" and "20%" different kinds of answer.
- **Evidence:** Ryan & Williams (2007), reported in Baratta et al. (2010) [T2] — 38% of 14-year-olds could compute the percentage remaining, and **31% answered "20" rather than "20%."** Described as "common across contexts."
- **Surface it early with:** problems where a bare count and a percent are both plausible answers to the question as written. Ask what the number counts.
- **When it shows up:** ask "twenty what?" It is a one-word intervention and it works because the student can answer it.

**Misconception: the additive error inside the proportion**
- **Looks like:** setting up `16/32 = x/48` and then adding the difference — since 32 + 16 = 48, writing x = 16 + 16 = 32. Or, in a table, adding a constant to both quantities.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** build-up by adding is a *legitimate* proportional strategy being mis-scoped (see the ratios brief §4). The proportion template does not prevent it — the student is now doing additive reasoning inside a multiplicative structure, which is harder to see because the notation looks correct.
- **Evidence:** Çalışıcı (2018) [T3/T4] documents this exact production — a student "thinks that the value of a fraction does not change when the same number is added to its numerator or denominator." And Fernández et al. (2010) [T2] is the scale finding: **57.0% of grade 6 students used additive methods on proportional problems, in a curriculum that had already taught them the rule of three.**
- **Surface it early with:** a proportion whose numbers make the additive answer visibly absurd — where the additive result is wildly the wrong size.
- **When it shows up:** the bar. Ask them to mark their answer on it. An additive answer lands in a place that does not match the percent, and the picture says so without you ruling.

### 5. Concrete activities

**Qualitative shading and estimation.** Students shade a bar or circle and estimate the percent, or are shown a shaded figure and estimate. No computation. *Structure it embodies:* percent as a proportion of a whole, prior to any arithmetic. *What it cannot show:* anything about the three problem types. *Materials:* printed bars, or a strip of paper. **Free.** *This is CCSD 1.1 already.* **~15 minutes.**

**Bar-building with benchmarks.** Given a total, students mark 50%, 25%, 10%, 5%, and 1% on a bar, computing each from the last. Then they are asked for 15%, 35%, and 11% and must build them. *Structure:* percent as additive decomposition of multiplicative parts, and the 1% route. *What it cannot show:* it presumes the total is known — it does not touch find-the-whole. *Materials:* paper strips or a printed bar template. **Free.** *Evidence:* this is van Galen & van Eerde's second claimed advantage, the bar as a working surface for intermediate steps [T2]. **~25 minutes.**

**Three questions, same numbers.** Give one situation — "45 out of 150 students ride the bus" — and three questions: what percent ride, how many is 30% of 150, and if 30% is 45 what is the total? Students sort which is being asked *before* solving any. *Structure:* the three problem types as one relationship read from different starting points. *What it cannot show:* nothing about computation — this is purely about the decision. *Evidence:* this is problem-type sorting, the SBI component with RCT evidence behind it [T1], and it is the differentiation move the comparison literature specifically endorses for low-prior-knowledge students [T2]. *Materials:* index cards. **~15 minutes, repeatable.**

**Accept-or-reject the method.** Present three calculations for a percent problem and ask which are valid. *Structure:* method evaluation rather than method generation. *Evidence:* Baratta et al. [T2] found students markedly better at rejecting wrong calculations (54%) than accepting right ones (36%) — so this is a genuinely more accessible entry point for a student two years behind, *and* it targets the weaker skill directly. Include `145 ÷ 0.11` deliberately; it was the hardest item in their study. **~15 minutes.**

**The unfriendly-percentage set.** A problem set where every percentage is 11%, 15%, 35%, or 7%. *Structure:* it forces the relationship into view because no fraction shortcut exists. *What it cannot show:* it teaches nothing on its own — it *reveals*. Pair it with the bar. *Evidence:* van Galen & van Eerde's explicit design claim [T2]. **Free — it is a number change.** **~20 minutes.**

### 6. The progression to abstract

**The progression is the percentage-bar trajectory, and its provenance should be stated honestly: it is Dutch Realistic Mathematics Education design work, not an efficacy trial.** Van den Heuvel-Panhuizen (2003) [T2, abstract-level retrieval only] describes the bar developing "from a drawing that represents a context related to percentage, to a strip for estimation and reasoning, to an abstract tool that supports the use of percentage as an operator" — beginning with qualitative "so-many-out-of-so-many" situations, through student sketches and an "occupation meter," to a full percent bar, and eventually into a double number line. The CCSS Progressions independently recommends the same object.

**Why not CRA.** The 2025 CRA meta-analysis [T1] covers 30 studies, all single-case design, 116 students total, researcher-implemented in 73% of cases, with only two of eleven SES-reporting studies including low-SES students — and its study table contains **no ratio, proportion, or percent study.** Using CRA as the frame here would be a triple extrapolation. See the ratios brief §6.

**What I considered and why they lost.** *Worked examples* — strong evidence, g = 0.48 across 55 studies [T1] — is a component to use inside the sequence, not a spine. *Comparing solution methods* would win for a class with prior knowledge and loses here; Durkin, Star and Rittle-Johnson found students without prior knowledge "learned best from either comparing problem types or studying examples sequentially," and that comparison "may be too overwhelming... without sufficient prior knowledge" [T2]. That finding is directly relevant to CCSD 3.1.a. *Concreteness fading proper* has genuine randomized support on other content — undergraduates on modular arithmetic, early elementary on math equivalence [T1] — but **not on percent**, and the bar trajectory is a fade in all but name anyway.

**What the concrete stage embodies, and what it lets students do wrong.** The bar embodies percent as *parts of a whole with a fixed total*, which is what makes all three problem types one picture. What it lets students do wrong is treat it as decoration — drawing a rectangle and then computing in their heads exactly as before. That is not hypothetical: it is Büscher's *Mirror Movement* utilization scheme, where the bar "only addresses scaling" and never touches the conceptual core. **The material does not carry the mathematics; the routine does.**

**The representational bridge — what students draw and what it must preserve.** The drawing must preserve **proportional length**. A bar where 10% and 50% are drawn the same width is not a model, it is a label. This is the single most common student drawing error and it destroys the bar's entire value, because the whole point is that the picture can be *read* for size. Require that 50% land in the middle. Beyond that, the drawing must carry **both scales** — percent above, quantity below — which is what makes it the blend of tape diagram and double number line the Progressions describes.

**The fade trigger.** Move from the qualitative bar to the benchmark bar when the student can place 30% on an unmarked strip and defend it against 25% and 50%. Move from bar-required to bar-optional when the student **predicts the size of the answer before computing** — not when they get answers right, because a right answer can come from a rule. Move to symbolic recording when the student narrates the partitioning while computing.

**The return path, and it matters more here than in the other two briefs.** Percent is the topic where a student who has faded too early produces a confidently wrong answer with no felt sense that it is wrong — 600 ÷ 15 = 40 looks like a fine number. So the return signal is **an answer whose size is not defensible**, and the response is to go back to the bar for that problem, not to reteach the procedure. Van Galen and van Eerde's warning applies directly: "teachers should promote drawing the percentage bar for **quite a long period**, because the students need time to learn to appreciate the bar as a mathematical tool that can be applied in all situations. Eventually the percentage bar should come to function as a model for thinking, but that will only happen if the students are thoroughly familiar with it." And their sentence about the alternative: "if procedures are insufficiently anchored in understanding, **they will quickly become vulnerable tricks.**"

**Can one teacher run this in one 50-minute period with a mixed-readiness room?** Yes. Nothing here requires materials beyond paper, and the bar is a whole-class routine rather than a differentiated one. The genuine costs are two. **Duration** — Jitendra's shorter intervention versions produced nothing for students with mathematics difficulties, and percent is sharing a district topic with unit rates and conversions. And **your own consistency** — Büscher's finding is that the same bar in two teachers' hands produces two different mathematical objects, which means the routine has to be enforced the same way every single day or it degrades into decoration.

### 7. Scaffolding and differentiation

**Prerequisite chain with diagnostic items.**

| Prerequisite | Grade | Diagnostic item | What the wrong answers tell you |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Out of 100" as a meaning | 5.NBT / 4.NF.C.6 | "29 out of 100 students walk to school. What percent walk?" | Wrong → the fundamental definition is absent. only **62% of Year 9 students** answered it correctly in the retrieved data — do not assume it. Nothing downstream is stable until this is fixed |
| Multiply and divide by 10 and 100 | 5.NBT.A.2 | "What is 10% of 340? What is 1% of 340?" | First right, second wrong → place-value gap, not a percent gap. Both wrong → this blocks every benchmark method |
| Fraction–decimal–percent benchmarks | 4.NF.C.6 | "Write 1/4 as a decimal and as a percent" | Wrong → the decomposition tools are missing; re-anchor the benchmark set only, not the full conversion table |
| Division with a decimal divisor | 6.NS.B.3 (required grade 6 fluency) | "145 ÷ 0.11" | Refuses because the answer is bigger → MMBDMS, conceptual. Attempts and errs → fluency; give the calculator |

**Three gap types, three responses.**

- **Conceptual (out of 100; division making bigger).** The model was never built. Neither can be scaffolded around. "Out of 100" deserves a full re-anchor if the diagnostic fails; the division-direction belief is best handled by *routing around it* — teach the 1% unitary route as primary, which Baratta et al. found was "relatively very well done" where the decimal division was the hardest item in the study. That is not avoidance; it is choosing the method students can hold.
- **Procedural (benchmark conversion, scaling on the bar).** Responds to clean **correct** worked examples. The meta-analysis found correct-only outperformed incorrect-only and correct-plus-incorrect, and that **adding self-explanation prompts moderated the effect negatively** (Barbieri et al., 2023, g = 0.48) [T1] — so pair a completed bar with a matched blank one, and skip the "explain your thinking" box.
- **Fluency bottleneck (decimal division, multi-digit multiplication).** Calculator and multiplication chart, consistently, **including on the check**. Ellington (2003) [T1], 54 studies: results went mixed specifically "when calculators were not part of assessment." 6.RP.A.3c assesses percent reasoning, not decimal division. **Honest caveat: I found no research at all on multiplication charts as a scaffold** — that is reasoning from cognitive load theory, not a retrieved finding.

**Differentiation as one task.**
- **Number choice inside one problem set.** Same context; 10%, then 15%, then 35%, then 7%. Everyone works the same problem. The ceiling is in the percentage.
- **Problem-type sorting** as a whole-class entry requiring no computation, so a student two years behind participates on the mathematical decision. RCT-backed [T1] and specifically endorsed for low-prior-knowledge students [T2].
- **Accept-or-reject tasks** as the accessible entry — Baratta et al.'s 54%-versus-36% asymmetry means evaluation is genuinely easier than generation for this population, and it targets the weaker skill.
- **Pre-drawn bars** for students who need them, blank paper for those who do not. Same task, same problems, different starting scaffold.

**Above grade level.** Reversibility and error analysis, not acceleration into percent-of-change. "A student found 15% of 80 and got 12. Write a different problem that also has the answer 12." Or: "Here are three student bars for the same problem. Two are wrong. What was each student thinking?" Error analysis is the highest-value extension because it produces exactly the misconception vocabulary you want circulating — though note the tension flagged in §8 about incorrect worked examples, so keep it as extension rather than core.

### 8. Open questions and weak spots

**There is no meta-analysis of percent instruction at any grade level.** Verified directly against ERIC. There is no meta-analysis of proportional reasoning instruction either. Percent is the thinnest sub-area in the entire 6.RP standard, and it is worth saying that out loud to anyone who asks for "what the research says" — because the honest answer is that the research base here is a handful of small studies, a few large descriptive surveys, and a strong convergence of expert design opinion.

**The percentage bar recommendation rests on small qualitative studies and design documents, not on trials.** Van Galen and van Eerde is **n = 14**, a single teaching lesson, at an international school. Van den Heuvel-Panhuizen (2003) I retrieved at abstract level only. Büscher (2021) is an exploratory case study retrieved at abstract level. The CCSS Progressions is expert consensus with no data. That is a real limitation. The recommendation is defensible as a design commitment and should not be presented as "research shows the bar works."

**Parker & Leinhardt (1995), "Percent: A privileged proportion," *Review of Educational Research* 65(4), 421–481 — the major review of this topic — could not be retrieved.** The fetch exceeded the token limit. Third-party paraphrase attributes to them four causes of difficulty: the complexity of the content, the diversity of relations describable by percent, the fact that those relations are often not explicitly treated in curricula, and a *linguistic* difficulty in which the mathematical relations are "invisible in the language," with percent's language having "misleading additive terminology for multiplicative meanings, and... multiple uses for the preposition 'of.'" **[unverified — paraphrase only, primary source not read.]** I have not built anything on it, but it is the single highest-value document to retrieve if you want a deeper account of *why* percent is hard, and if the linguistic diagnosis holds it is a strong independent argument for the bar — because a diagram forces an invisible relation onto paper.

**The one WWC recommendation touching ratio, rate, and proportion is rated Minimal Evidence.** Recommendation 4 of the fractions practice guide — the "before cross-multiplication" one this brief leans on — is the lowest of three WWC levels. Its evidence base, in the WWC's own words, is "three studies... that manipulatives and visual representations can be beneficial teaching tools for teaching proportions to students in grades 4, 5, and 7," plus three that did not meet standards. Do not let it be quoted as "research-based" without the qualifier.

**The case against the positional template is convergent, not experimental.** No trial compared a positional-setup routine against a representation-first routine for percent. The argument rests on: expert design documents (Progressions, NCTM, WWC panel), a documented error taxonomy showing that setup is the failure point, and one indirect empirical bridge — Fernández et al.'s finding that Spanish sixth graders taught the rule of three still reasoned additively 57% of the time. That bridge is suggestive and it is not a controlled comparison; the Spanish study did not manipulate whether students got the algorithm.

**The strongest experimental evidence taught cross-multiplication.** Jitendra's SBI package included it explicitly as one of a broad range of methods, and produced the best results in the literature. Anyone defending the template can point at this legitimately. The counter is that SBI also required a diagram and an estimate *before* strategy selection, and that "procedural flexibility... based on the numbers in the problem" is a different thing from a positional rule applied uniformly — but that is a reading of the intervention, not a result from it.

**Percent gains washed out in the only trial that measured them separately.** For students with mathematics difficulties, percent was significant at posttest and **not** at the nine-week delayed posttest, while ratio and proportion held. Plan re-exposure.

**Effects shrink in your conditions.** Grades 7–9 < grades 3–6 (p < .001); school personnel < research personnel; students with mathematics difficulties got 0.32 SD where the full sample got 0.46 SD, **and their growth rates did not change**. Classrooms with higher concentrations of English learners scored lower throughout.

**Nothing transferred to standardized tests.** Null on the GMADE and on end-of-year state math achievement in two separate analyses.

**Marked unverified.** (1) Parker & Leinhardt (1995) — **not read**, paraphrase only, as above. (2) Ryan & Williams (2007), *Children's mathematics 4–15* — the source of the "% sign ignored" 31% figure and the "percent means divide" 500 ÷ 24 example — **retrieved only as quoted by Baratta et al.; primary source not retrieved.** (3) Bell, Swan & Taylor (1981), the origin of MMBDMS — **cited within a retrieved source, primary text not retrieved.** (4) Van den Heuvel-Panhuizen (2003) and Büscher (2021) — **abstract-level retrieval only.** (5) The Hodgen et al. attribution of the improved £20-coat item to "informal methods" is the *authors'* interpretation of their own data, not a tested claim; I have presented it as suggestive and it should stay that way.

**What I would want and could not find:** a controlled comparison of percent-bar-first instruction against proportion-setup-first instruction in a middle-grades classroom. That is the exact question your practice turns on. It does not appear to exist, and the honest thing to say to anyone who challenges the recommendation is that this is a bet on convergent design evidence and a documented error taxonomy — a well-founded bet, and a bet.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

Five days on the CRA class-state spine. **Read the pacing note at the end.**

**Day 1 — Percent means out of 100**
- Warm-up (2 review + 3 current): review = "29 out of 100 students walk to school — what percent?" and "10% of 340? 1% of 340?" — the two prerequisite diagnostics from §7. Current Q3 targets the **dropped percent sign**: "60 books, 20 taken. What percent remains?"
- Concrete: shade and estimate on paper strips. Representational: sketch the bar. Abstract: name the percent.
- If the 29-out-of-100 diagnostic comes back badly, spend the whole day here. Nothing downstream holds without it.

**Day 2 — Benchmarks and the 1% route**
- Warm-up Q3 targets **friendly-number dependence**: "What is 25% of 80? What is 15% of 80?" The gap between those two answers is the diagnosis.
- Build 50%, 10%, 5%, 1% on the bar. Then build 15%, 35%, 11% from them.
- The routine gets named today and does not change again: **mark 0%, mark 100%, mark what you know, then decide.**

**Day 3 — Three questions, one picture**
- Warm-up Q3 targets **problem-type confusion**: give three questions with the same numbers, ask which is being found.
- All three problem types on the same bar. Sort before solving.
- Exit: sort five problems by type. No computation.

**Day 4 — Finding the whole (the hard day)**
- Warm-up Q3 targets **MMBDMS**: "11% of a number is 145. Is the number bigger or smaller than 145?"
- Bar first, always. The unitary route through 1% is the primary method; decimal division is shown as a shortcut *after* the bar has made the answer's size obvious.
- Accept-or-reject task with `145 ÷ 0.11` deliberately included.

**Day 5 — Unfriendly percentages, and the proportion arrives**
- Warm-up Q3 is a 15%-of-a-non-round-number problem. This is the day the divide-bigger-by-smaller error is supposed to appear.
- **If you are going to teach the proportion template, this is where it goes** — as a compression of what students can already do on the bar, with the bar drawn first every time. Drop the positional mnemonic; "draw the bar, then write what you see" is the replacement.
- Exit: three problems at 11%, 35%, and 7%.

**Two pacing notes, both honest.**

**First, the topic is overloaded — and the combined view says something the individual briefs cannot.** The three Appendices across these briefs total **15 days** (6 ratios, 4 unit rates, 5 percents), while the ratios brief separately argues its own topic wants closer to eight to ten — so the evidence-supported total is nearer **17 to 19 days**. And **all three briefs propose taking the slack from the same conversions strand.** It cannot absorb all three. Decide which topic takes the hit before day one rather than discovering it in week three.

Percent is sharing CCSD Topic 2 with unit rates *and* measurement conversion, across six learning progressions. Five days for percent is generous inside that topic and thin against the evidence — Jitendra's shorter intervention versions produced *nothing* for students with mathematics difficulties, and the rational-number meta-analysis puts the significant duration threshold at 20+ hours across a whole rational-number unit. The place to find slack is the conversions strand, which is ratio-table machinery applied in a new context rather than new mathematics.

**Second, and this is the one that matters most for percent specifically: plan the re-exposure now.** The only trial that measured percent separately found the gains gone at nine weeks for students with mathematics difficulties, while ratio and proportion gains held. Percent problems belong in the warm-up spiral every week for the rest of the semester — which the interleaving evidence supports independently (Rohrer et al., 2020: 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83, on an unannounced test a month later) and which costs zero instructional days. If you do one thing from this brief besides changing the percentages in your problem sets, do the spiral.
