# Unit Rates — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Standard(s):** 6.RP.A.2 (understand the concept of a unit rate a/b associated with a ratio a:b with b ≠ 0, and use rate language) · 6.RP.A.3b (solve unit rate problems, including unit pricing and constant speed)
**Compiled:** 2026-08-06
**Module map note:** In the CCSD Grade 6 Acc Teacher Clarity Guides this lives in **Module 2 Topic 2, "Percents, Unit Rates and Conversions"** — sharing one topic with percents and measurement conversion across six learning progressions. It is not its own topic in the district guide. Topic 3 is The Statistical Process (6.SP), not percents.

**Scope:** Covers the unit rate as a concept, the ratio-vs-rate distinction, better-buy and constant-speed problems, and the specific trouble you named — that students do not see a unit rate as a ratio with a denominator of 1. Also covers where unit rate belongs in the sequence, which turns out to be the most consequential question in this brief. Deliberately left out: unit conversion (CCSD progression 4), which is an application of ratio-table machinery rather than new mathematics, and the rhombus-diagonal work in CCSD 6.2, which has no research base I could find and reads as a Carnegie-specific task.

**Evidence at a glance:** 7 Tier 1 · 5 Tier 2 · 4 Tier 3 sources retrieved this session. *(Tier definitions: **T1** = meta-analysis, RCT, or a WWC recommendation rated Strong or Moderate. **T2** = peer-reviewed study with a smaller sample or weaker design, well-designed qualitative or design-based research, or a WWC recommendation rated Minimal. **T3** = practitioner and professional consensus. **T4** = anecdotal; never carries an effectiveness claim.)* **This is the thinnest of the three briefs, and you should know why before you read it.** I searched specifically for empirical research on unit-rate misconceptions and found essentially none — no study measuring whether students understand a unit rate as a ratio with denominator 1, no study on reversed-division errors in better-buy problems. That absence is real, not a search failure. What this brief rests on instead is (a) a *measured performance asymmetry* that explains why unit rate is hard, which is the single most useful finding here, (b) the RCT evidence on delivery that covers this content, and (c) expert design documents on where unit rate belongs. Every one of those is labeled.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*Read this first. About ten minutes — the first two sections alone will get you through a day.*

### The short version

Your instinct that students "struggle to differentiate that unit rates are proportions with a denominator of 1" is correct, but the evidence suggests the difficulty is one layer deeper than the definition. **Finding a unit rate and applying a unit rate are two structurally different operations, and there is measured reason to think students are much worse at the second one.** Küchemann, Hodgen and Brown measured the underlying asymmetry directly: on the same recipe task, when the friendly multiplier ran *within* one measure (people → people), 91% of students got it; when the identical mathematics ran *between* the two measures (people → millilitres), **51%** did. Finding the unit rate is a within-measure move — divide both quantities by 11. *Using* it to get the answer is a between-measure move.

**Flag this honestly, because it is the load-bearing claim of the brief and it is an inference.** That study is about double number lines and the scalar/functional distinction; it does not measure unit rate. The mapping from "the between-measure move is hard" to "applying a unit rate is where students fail" is mine. I think it is sound — it explains a classroom phenomenon most teachers recognize, students who find the rate and then sit there — but it is a bridge, not a finding, and it should be presented that way to anyone who asks.

Which means: teaching the definition harder will not fix this. **"A unit rate is a ratio with denominator 1" is a true sentence that does not do any work,** because the student's problem is not naming the object; it is executing the move the object licenses. The instructional consequence is that unit rate should arrive as the *answer to a question* — "what if I only want one?" — after students have iterated composed units, not as a definition at the front of the topic.

And do not spend a lesson on "a ratio is not a rate." The CCSS Progressions declines to teach that distinction, notes explicitly that "some authors distinguish ratios from rates... others use ratio to encompass both," and states that "the Standards use ratio in the second sense." It is a terminology dispute among textbook authors, not a student learning goal.

### Where to spend the time

- **Iterating and partitioning a composed unit down to one.** "One batch is 3 cups grape to 2 cups peach. Here's half a batch. Here's a third of a batch. What if I only want *one* cup of peach?" This is the route to unit rate that runs *along* the line, where students function at 91% rather than 51%.
- **Applying a known unit rate to a new quantity — the between-measure move.** On the reading above, this is the actual bottleneck and it deserves more time than finding the rate does. Most curricula spend it the other way around. (The 40-point gap is measured; that it is *the unit-rate bottleneck* is the inference flagged above.)
- **Better-buy comparison, because it is the one context where the unit rate is obviously worth having.** But note the retrieved data: on a washing-powder better-buy item, **60.0%** of Year 5–6 students made an absolute comparison rather than a relative one — they compared price alone, or loads alone. That is not a unit-rate error. It is a failure to form the ratio at all, and it is upstream.

**What gets compressed to pay for it:** the ratio-vs-rate definitional distinction (CCSD 5.1.c) — fold the vocabulary in, skip the lesson. The rhombus-diagonal unit rate task (CCSD 6.2) — I could find no research base and it is a strange context in which to meet a rate. Unit conversion (CCSD progression 4) rides on ratio tables you have already built and does not need its own conceptual introduction.

### The sequence

1. **Iterate a composed unit, then partition it down.** *Makes possible:* unit rate as a member of a family of equivalent ratios rather than a new object.
2. **"What if I only want one?"** *Makes possible:* the unit rate arriving with a reason to exist. The CCSS Progressions models exactly this — for "2 pounds of beans cost $5, how much for 15 pounds," it presents build-up as Method 1, find-one-pound-first as **Method 2**, and one-step scaling as Method 3.
3. **Double number line, with 1 marked.** *Makes possible:* seeing the unit rate as the pair `(1, r)` — "as in tables, unit rates appear paired with 1." This is the representation that makes the denominator-of-1 idea visible instead of asserted.
4. **Applying the rate to a non-integer quantity.** *Makes possible:* the between-measure move, deliberately, with the friendly numbers removed so students cannot build up instead.
5. **Better-buy comparison.** *Makes possible:* the reason a unit rate is useful — a comparison that is impossible without it.
6. **Constant speed and rate as slope, lightly.** *Makes possible:* the grade 7 and grade 8 connection. One day, as a look-ahead, not as mastery.

### The three misconceptions to plan for

| The wrong answer you will actually see | The task that surfaces it early |
|---|---|
| Better buy: "**A costs the least**" (compared price alone) or "**B costs more but you get 10 more loads**" (compared loads alone) — 60% made an absolute comparison | Two products where the cheaper one is the *worse* buy. Design the numbers so the single-attribute answer is wrong |
| Divides in the wrong direction: for "6 balloons for $24, cost of 5," writes **24 ÷ 5** or **6 ÷ 24** | Ask for the unit rate *both ways* on the same item — "dollars per balloon" and "balloons per dollar" — and ask which one answers the question |
| Finds the unit rate correctly and then cannot use it — gets `$4 per pound` and stalls on "what about 7 pounds?" | Any two-step rate problem with a non-integer target quantity. This is the between-measure case (the inference flagged above) and it will not show up if every target is a whole multiple |

### The progression at a glance

Not CRA. See the ratios brief §6 for the full argument — the 2025 CRA meta-analysis covers 30 single-case studies, 116 students, and contains zero ratio, proportion, or percent studies. What has RCT evidence on this exact content is **schema-based instruction**; what has expert-design support is a representational fade.

| Stage | Students do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| **Composed unit, partitioned** | Physically halve, third, and quarter a batch. Find "one" by dividing the batch, not by dividing two numbers. | The student can produce the one-unit case without being told which number to divide by which. |
| **Double number line with 1 marked** | Draw the line, place the known pair, find and label the point above 1. | The student marks 1 unprompted, and can say what the number above it means in the situation's words. |
| **Apply the rate** | Multiply the unit rate by a new quantity — including a non-integer one. | The student *predicts the size* of the answer before computing. This is the estimate step and it is load-bearing. |
| **Choose the rate** | Decide which of the two possible unit rates ("per dollar" or "per item") answers the question asked. | The student can justify the choice from the question, not from a rule about which number goes first. |

### If they're two years behind

The prerequisites that block this are **division as sharing into equal groups** (grade 3–4), **division with a decimal or fractional quotient** (a student who believes division always makes a whole number cannot find $4.33 per pound), and **multi-digit division fluency** (6.NS.B.2, a required grade 6 fluency).

Diagnostics for your two warm-up review problems: (1) "$12 for 5 identical notebooks. What does one cost?" — an answer of *$2* means the student rounded to keep it whole; an answer of *$2.40* is intact; an answer of *$7* is a subtraction default. (2) "Which is more: 3 ÷ 4 or 4 ÷ 3?" — a student who says they are the same has the division-direction problem that generates every reversed unit rate you will see.

**Just-in-time.** The division-direction confusion is conceptual and needs a five-minute re-anchor immediately before the first better-buy lesson — not a week of review. The computational fluency gap should be **scaffolded around**: calculator and multiplication chart, consistently, including on the check. Ellington's meta-analysis of 54 studies found calculator use "did not hinder the development of mathematical skills," with results going mixed only when calculators were *not* part of assessment. Unit rate assesses proportional reasoning; long division is a confound.

### Watch out for

- **Do not lead with the unit rate.** This is the strongest recommendation in the brief and it runs against how most textbooks and the CCSD guide are laid out (CCSD 5.1 opens with "the definition of a unit rate and how they are calculated"). The Progressions places unit rate *after* students are comfortable with fractional and decimal entries in ratio tables; the WWC panel's sequence puts unit ratio third, after build-up and ratio tables, and justifies it by a problem type — "there is an advantage to using a unit ratio strategy... in a problem involving ratios *without an integral relation*." Unit rate is the destination, not the entry.
- **Do not teach "a ratio is not a rate" as a distinction.** The Standards deliberately collapse it. Teaching it costs a day and creates a category error students then have to police.
- **The Progressions warns specifically:** "Unit rates should not be confused with unit fractions." Watch for students reading "1/4 pound per dollar" as the fraction one-quarter divorced from its units.
- **"3 m = 2 sec" is a real error worth intercepting.** From the Progressions: "Students sometimes use the equals sign incorrectly to indicate proportional relationships... In fact, 3 meters is not equal to 2 seconds. This relationship can be represented in a table or by writing '3 m → 2 sec.'" The arrow is a cheap fix and it prevents a habit that becomes expensive in grade 7.
- **The evidence here is thin and you should say so if pressed.** There is no meta-analysis of proportional reasoning instruction, the one WWC recommendation touching ratio and rate is rated *Minimal Evidence*, and I found no empirical study on unit-rate misconceptions specifically.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. What the research supports

**The measured asymmetry is the finding this brief turns on.** Küchemann, Hodgen and Brown (2014) [T2], reporting item data from the ESRC-funded ICCAMS project in England, ran two versions of the same "Spicy Soup" recipe item with the numbers swapped so that the easy ×3 relation fell either within one measure space or between the two:

- Easy relation **within** a measure (11 people → 33 people): **91% facility**
- Easy relation **between** measures (11 people → 33 ml): **51% facility**

Their explanation, citing Vergnaud (1983): "students are far more likely to establish a relation that is within a measure space (what he calls a scalar relation) than between measure spaces (a function relation)." A geometric-enlargement item in the same study showed a parallel gap in the opposite direction — between-objects ×4 at 75%, within-object ×4 at 36% — and both were "substantially lower than the corresponding Spicy Soup facilities."

**Why this is the unit-rate finding even though the study is not about unit rate.** A unit rate strategy is structurally a hybrid: *finding* the rate is a within-measure operation (divide both quantities by 11 — you stay on your own line), and *applying* it is a between-measure operation (multiply across to the other measure). So "find the unit rate, then multiply" routes every student through the exact move the data says they are worst at, and it does so at the moment they think they have already done the hard part. **That is my interpretation, not the study's claim** — Küchemann et al. do not analyze unit rate as such. But the structural mapping is not a stretch, and it explains a classroom phenomenon most teachers recognize: students who can find the unit rate and then sit there.

**The delivery model with the best evidence for this content is schema-based instruction.** [T1] Jitendra et al. (2015, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) ran a cluster RCT with 82 teachers, 1,999 grade 7 students, 58 schools, 50 districts in one upper-Midwest state. Both conditions taught ratio, proportion, and percent daily for six weeks; only delivery differed. Effect: **0.46 SD** posttest, **0.32 SD** at nine weeks, on proportional problem solving — **null on the norm-referenced GMADE** and null on the state test in a companion analysis. Notably, the SBI package explicitly taught unit rate as *one strategy on a menu*: teachers "encouraged students to deeply process information about when, how, and why to use a broad range of methods (e.g., equivalent fractions, unit rate, cross multiplication) for a given class of problems." Unit rate is presented as a *choice*, selected "based on the numbers in the problem" — not as the method.

The six-step student routine includes a step that matters more for unit rate than anywhere else: **estimate the answer before computing.** For a student who has just found "$4.33 per pound" and is about to multiply by 7, an estimate ("more than 28, less than 35") is the only thing standing between them and a reversed operation.

**One instructional moderator applies with unusual force here.** [T1] In Krowka et al.'s (2025) meta-analysis of 28 rational-number interventions for students with mathematics difficulties (grades 3–9, all meeting WWC standards, overall g = 0.68), two instructional practices survived the adjusted model: **"teaching and use of accurate mathematical language" (b = 0.50, p = .001)** and the use of number lines (b = 0.47, p = .01). The language finding is the one that bites here. Unit rate is a topic whose entire difficulty is arguably linguistic — *per*, *for each*, *for every*, *at this rate*, *dollars per pound* versus *pounds per dollar*. The word "per" carries the whole mathematical relationship and students read past it. Insisting on the full phrase every time, out loud, is the cheapest high-leverage move available and the one with the best moderator evidence behind it.

**Dose is a real constraint.** [T1] The same research group's shorter versions of SBI — "about 450–500 min (9–10 sessions of 50 min)" — produced *non-significant* results for students with mathematics difficulties; only the six-week daily version worked. Krowka et al. independently found the significant duration threshold at **20+ hours**. Unit rate sits inside a district topic that also carries percents and conversions, which is a pacing problem worth naming before the module starts rather than in week three.

### 2. Emphasis — where the leverage is

**a. Applying a unit rate (the between-measure move), on misconception density and measured difficulty.** The 91%/51% gap is the sharpest quantitative argument in this brief. Instruction that spends its time on *finding* the rate is spending it on the 91% side. The leverage is entirely on the other side of that gap, and it is invisible unless the target quantities are non-integer — because with integer targets, students can quietly build up instead of applying the rate and get the right answer with the wrong reasoning.

**b. Forming the ratio at all, on error rate.** The highest single error rate retrieved on a genuine unit-rate task is not about unit rate. Hilton et al. (2012) [T3] gave 140 Year 5–6 students a better-buy comparison (A: 1 kg, $4, 20 loads; B: 1.5 kg, $6.50, 30 loads) with four options: "A costs the least" (price only), "B costs a bit more but you get 10 more loads" (loads only), "The cost per load of washing is less" (correct, relative), and "Both are the same value." **60.0% made an absolute comparison instead of a relative one**; 35.0% "considered one value without a related value." Both distractors are recognizably reasonable-sounding student sentences. The instructional consequence is that the better-buy context does not teach unit rate to a student who has not yet formed a ratio — it just gives them a new place to compare one column.

**c. Partitioning a composed unit, on prerequisite fan-out.** [T2] The NCTM research brief names "forming a composed unit... and iterating and partitioning it" as one of four components of conceptual understanding of ratio. Unit rate is the terminal case of partitioning — the batch divided down until one of the quantities is 1. Building it that way means unit rate is not a new object requiring a new definition; it is the family member that happens to have a 1 in it. That framing is what makes the double number line's "unit rates appear paired with 1" land as an observation rather than an assertion.

**What gets less time, and why it is defensible.** The ratio-vs-rate definitional distinction is explicitly declined by the Progressions — "some authors distinguish ratios from rates, using the term 'ratio' when units are the same and 'rate' when units are different; others use ratio to encompass both kinds of situations. The Standards use ratio in the second sense." Teaching a distinction the standards deliberately collapse costs a day and buys a category students then misapply. The Progressions is equally clear that a rate is something a ratio *has* — "Ratios have associated rates" — with unit rate being "the numerical part of the rate." Say that in one sentence and move on. Unit conversion (CCSD progression 4) is ratio-table machinery in a new context, not new machinery. And I could find no research base whatsoever for the rhombus-diagonal unit rate task (CCSD 6.2); it is a defensible enrichment and an indefensible core lesson.

### 3. Sequence and why

**1. Partition a composed unit down toward one.** *Purpose:* make unit rate a member of a family, not a new species. *Makes possible:* everything downstream, because a student who reaches "one" by halving twice understands what the 1 means; a student who reaches it by dividing 24 by 6 has performed an operation.

**2. "What if I only want one?"** *Purpose:* give unit rate a reason to exist before it has a name. The Progressions models exactly this progression for "2 pounds of beans cost $5, how much for 15 pounds": Method 1 build-up to 14 pounds then add one more; Method 2 find one pound first; Method 3 the same in one step. **Unit rate is Method 2, arrived at because Method 1 got tedious.** Presenting it first removes the reason.

**3. Double number line with 1 marked explicitly.** *Purpose:* make the denominator-of-1 idea *visible*. The Progressions: double number lines "can help make visible that there are many, even infinitely many, pairs in the same ratio, including those with rational number entries. As in tables, unit rates appear paired with 1." *Design rule from Küchemann et al.:* when you introduce this, the friendly relationship must run **along** one line, not between them. Do not open on a between-lines multiplier.

**4. Apply the rate to a non-integer target.** *Purpose:* force the between-measure move. This is the hard step and it needs its own day. With integer targets, students build up and you learn nothing.

**5. Better-buy comparison.** *Purpose:* the payoff — a comparison genuinely impossible without a common unit. Design the numbers so the single-attribute answer is wrong, or the 60% absolute-comparison error succeeds and hides.

**6. Constant speed and the graph, one day.** *Purpose:* the forward connection. The Progressions places the real work at 7.RP.2a, where "the unit rate appears in the equation and graph as the slope of the line, and in the coordinate pair with first coordinate 1." One day as a look-ahead is proportionate; the retrieved data shows students in this band misread distance–time graphs at measurable rates (10% outright, plus 47.2% reading accurately then choosing an irrelevant answer).

#### Where the research disagrees

**Unit rate first, or unit rate late?** Most textbooks — and the CCSD clarity guide, whose progression 5 opens with "the definition of a unit rate and how they are calculated" — introduce unit rate as a definition and then apply it. The Progressions and the WWC panel both put it third, after build-up and ratio tables.

The disagreement hinges on whether unit rate is a *definition to be applied* or a *strategy to be discovered*. **Recommendation: late.** Three retrieved sources converge, and the third is the decisive one. (1) The Progressions makes unit rate useful only "as students become comfortable with fractional and decimal entries" in tables. (2) The WWC panel's PD transcript justifies it by problem type — "Once students have a good understanding of buildup strategies and ratio tables, teachers can present more challenging problems in which there is an advantage to using a unit ratio strategy... best shown by solving for x in a problem involving ratios without an integral relation." (3) Küchemann et al.'s 91%/51% split says the *second half* of the unit-rate strategy sits on the hard side of a measured performance cliff — which means leading with it front-loads the difficulty at the moment students have the least support.

**What would flip me:** evidence that an early explicit unit-rate definition improves later strategy selection or reduces the between-measure gap. I searched for this and found nothing either way. It is a genuine open question, and the recommendation above is a bet on converging design evidence plus one measured asymmetry — not a finding.

**Should cross-multiplication appear here?** No, and the Standards agree: "the Standards do not require this method in Grade 6." See the percents brief, where the full argument lives.

### 4. Misconceptions and how to prepare

**Misconception: absolute comparison in place of relative comparison**
- **Looks like:** on the washing-powder better-buy item, "**A costs the least**" or "**B costs a bit more but you get 10 more loads.**" Both are complete sentences that sound like reasoning.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** for the entire prior history of their schooling, "which is more?" was answered by looking at one number. Nothing before grade 6 required holding two quantities simultaneously.
- **Evidence:** Hilton et al. (2012) [T3], 140 Year 5–6 students, two-tier diagnostic — **60.0%** made an absolute comparison; 35.0% considered one value without a related value; 15.7% considered one value without considering the total.
- **Surface it early with:** two products where the cheaper one is the worse buy. Design that deliberately; if the cheap one is also the better value, the error produces the right answer and stays hidden.
- **When it shows up:** do not correct the comparison. Ask a different question — "how much for one load?" — which forces both quantities into the same sentence. Then re-ask the original. Confusion is step one; the *what do you know* is that they know two things about each box and have used one.

**Misconception: dividing in the wrong direction**
- **Looks like:** "6 balloons for $24. What do 5 balloons cost?" → computes **24 ÷ 5** (divides by the number in the question) or **6 ÷ 24 = 0.25** and then reports $0.25 per balloon.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** two previously-true rules are colliding. One is "divide the bigger by the smaller," which has never failed them. The other is that division has always produced a smaller, and usually whole, result. The second is documented in the percent literature as MMBDMS — *Multiplication Makes Bigger, Division Makes Smaller* — which Baratta et al. (2010) [T2] invoke to explain why the single hardest item in their 677-student study was accepting `145 ÷ 0.11` as a valid method.
- **Evidence:** I found **no study measuring reversed division in unit-rate problems specifically.** The MMBDMS attribution is from the percent literature [T2] and the "divide bigger by smaller" habit is documented in the percent context — van Galen & van Eerde (2013) [T2] found **8 of 14** students computing "15% discount on €600" as **600 ÷ 15**, and attributed it to students expecting test calculations to be doable. Both transfer plausibly to unit rate. **Label this as transfer, not as a unit-rate finding.**
- **Surface it early with:** ask for **both** unit rates on the same item — "dollars per balloon" and "balloons per dollar" — and then ask which one answers the question. Both are legitimate; the choice is the mathematics.
- **When it shows up:** estimate first. "About how much should one balloon cost — more than a dollar or less?" A student who has said "about $4" cannot then accept $0.25 without noticing.

**Misconception: finds the rate, cannot apply it**
- **Looks like:** correctly computes "$4.33 per pound," writes it down, and stops. Or computes it and then adds it to the target quantity.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** they have completed a within-measure operation, which is the one they are good at, and the next move is between-measure, which is the one they are not. From the student's side, the problem feels finished — a number has been produced.
- **Evidence:** Küchemann, Hodgen & Brown (2014) [T2] — 91% within-measure versus 51% between-measure on identical mathematics. This is inference from a structural mapping, not a direct measurement of this error; see §8.
- **Surface it early with:** any rate problem whose target quantity is non-integer, so build-up is unavailable. With integer targets this error is invisible because students bypass the rate entirely.
- **When it shows up:** put it on the double number line. The unit rate is the pair at 1; the answer is the pair at 7. Ask what is between them. This is the moment the double number line earns its place in the topic — it makes the second move a *drawing* rather than a decision about which operation to reach for.

**Misconception: reading a ratio statement as an equation**
- **Looks like:** writing **"3 m = 2 sec."**
- **Why it makes sense to them:** the equals sign has meant "the answer is" for six years, and it is the only relational symbol they have been issued.
- **Evidence:** [T3] CCSS Progressions names it directly: "In fact, 3 meters is not equal to 2 seconds. This relationship can be represented in a table or by writing '3 m → 2 sec.'"
- **Surface it early with:** ask students to read their own notation aloud. "Three meters equals two seconds" is a sentence a sixth grader will hear as wrong once they say it.
- **When it shows up:** give them the arrow. It is a one-symbol fix that prevents an expensive grade 7 habit.

### 5. Concrete activities

**Partition-to-one.** Students take a physical batch (say 12 cubes to 8 cubes) and halve it, halve again, third it — recording each pair — until one of the quantities is 1. *Structure it embodies:* unit rate as the terminal member of an equivalent-ratio family, reached by the same operation that produced every other member. *What it cannot show:* it works cleanly only when the numbers cooperate; a batch of 7 to 3 cannot be physically partitioned to 1 without fractions, which is precisely why this activity has to hand off to the double number line. *Materials:* countable objects in two colors. **Free substitute:** paper squares, tally columns, or coins. **~20 minutes.** *Design principle* [T2]: NCTM's iterate-and-partition framing.

**Both-ways unit rate.** Give one rate situation. Students compute *both* unit rates — per dollar and per item, or per minute and per mile — and then answer two questions, one of which each rate answers well. *Structure:* that a ratio yields two rates, and choosing between them is a reading decision, not a computation rule. *What it cannot show:* nothing about scaling; this is purely about direction. *Materials:* none. **~15 minutes.** *Evidence:* this is the "procedural flexibility... based on the numbers in the problem" component of SBI [T1], adapted.

**Better buy with a designed trap.** Two products where the cheaper item is the worse value. Students commit to an answer before computing, then compute. *Structure:* the necessity of a common unit. *What it cannot show:* it will not surface the between-measure application error, because better-buy stops at the rate. Pair it with the next activity. *Materials:* two real grocery labels, or invented ones. **~20 minutes.** *Evidence:* the 60% absolute-comparison rate [T3] is what makes the designed trap necessary rather than optional.

**Non-integer target application.** "Gas is $3.89 per gallon. What do 7.4 gallons cost?" or "You walk 3 miles in 50 minutes. How far in 35 minutes?" Students must apply a rate to a quantity they cannot build up to. *Structure:* the between-measure move, isolated. *What it cannot show:* it presumes the rate is already found; do not use it as an introduction. *Materials:* calculator. **~20 minutes.** *This is the highest-leverage activity in the brief* — it targets the 51% case directly, and it is invisible in a problem set built on whole multiples.

**Estimate-first protocol, embedded everywhere.** Before any rate computation: "will the answer be bigger or smaller than the number we started with, and roughly how much?" *Evidence:* this is a named component of the SBI package with 0.46 SD behind it [T1] — the PD covered "generating 'ballpark' estimates (quick and easy based on benchmark numbers and fractions)." **Free. Costs 30 seconds per problem.** One honest caution from the percent literature: Baratta et al. (2010) [T2] found that among Year 8 students, 56% correctly estimated an answer was over 1000, but "the group of students who estimated correctly was **no better** at selecting the calculations correctly" (15% vs 17%). Estimation and calculation can be decoupled — so the estimate has to be *used* as a check, out loud, not merely produced.

### 6. The progression to abstract

**The progression is a representational fade, not CRA, and the reason is in the ratios brief §6 — the 2025 CRA meta-analysis (30 single-case studies, 116 students, mostly researcher-delivered, mostly arithmetic) contains no ratio, proportion, or percent study at all.** What has evidence on this content is schema-based instruction. What has expert-design support — and Illustrative Mathematics' Grade 6 Unit 2 is the clearest published example, sequencing discrete diagram → double number line → table — is a fade, offered as design rationale with no efficacy trial behind that specific sequence. Say that distinction plainly if anyone asks.

**What the concrete stage represents, and what it lets students do wrong.** Partitioning a physical batch embodies the idea that the unit rate is reached by the *same* operation as every other equivalent ratio. What it lets students do wrong is treat "one" as a special stopping point rather than as an arbitrary member of the family — which is exactly the framing that makes them unable to go *back up* from it.

**The representational bridge.** From batch to double number line, the drawing must preserve **proportional spacing and the position of 1**. Not a schematic with tick marks wherever they fit — the marks have to be evenly spaced, or the model is lying about magnitude, which is the entire reason to use a number line rather than a table. What the drawing adds beyond the batch is *the continuum*: the Progressions notes that double number lines make visible "that there are many, even infinitely many, pairs in the same ratio, including those with rational number entries." That is the property that makes 7.4 gallons thinkable.

**The critical design rule, and it is measured.** Küchemann et al. found that on a double number line, students will work *along* the lines rather than *between* them, because along is within-measure. So **the first double number line a class ever sees must have its friendly multiplier along a line, not across.** They document what happens otherwise: their own cheesecake example, requiring a general ×1.25 between-lines multiplier, "caused considerable confusion — among students, teachers on the project, and ourselves," and "some teachers were put off the DNL." A teacher abandoning the representation because the first example was badly chosen is a documented outcome, not a hypothetical.

**They also document a trap most curricula never mention:** every ratio task admits **two** different double number lines, and one of them produces hybrid lines whose pairs are meaningless. Decide which one you are drawing, out loud, and say why the other is strange. This is invisible to the teacher and fatal to the student.

**The fade trigger.** Move from batch to double number line when the student asks for — or the problem requires — a partition the objects cannot physically make. You control that trigger by choosing the numbers. Move from double number line to bare computation when the student narrates the multiplication while drawing, and predicts the answer's size before computing.

**The return signal.** A unit rate computed in the wrong direction is the signal to go back. It means the object is not built — the student is operating on two numbers rather than on a batch. Send them to the partition activity, not to more rate problems. And frame it as checking the batch, not as going back a level; sixth graders read a demotion accurately and will refuse the material afterward.

**Can one teacher run this in one 50-minute period with a mixed-readiness room?** Yes. SBI was delivered by 82 ordinary teachers with two days of PD, in normal periods, with no extra instructional time, and a companion study found **first-year implementers got the same results as experienced ones** [T1] (Jitendra et al., 2016). Every activity above is one task for the whole room with the range in the numbers. The real cost is duration, and it collides with a district topic that also carries percents and conversions.

### 7. Scaffolding and differentiation

**Prerequisite chain with diagnostic items.**

| Prerequisite | Grade | Diagnostic item | What the wrong answers tell you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Division as equal sharing | 3.OA.A.2 | "$12 for 5 identical notebooks. What does one cost?" | *$2* → rounded to keep it whole; the decimal quotient is the gap. *$7* → subtraction default. *$2.40* → intact |
| Division direction | 4.NBT / 5.NBT | "Which is more, 3 ÷ 4 or 4 ÷ 3?" | "Same" → the direction confusion that generates every reversed unit rate. This is conceptual and must be re-anchored |
| Quotients less than 1 | 5.NF.B.3 | "12 ÷ 20 = ?" | *0.6* → intact. "You can't" or *1.67* → the student believes division cannot make a number below 1, which blocks "per dollar" rates entirely |
| Multi-digit division fluency | 6.NS.B.2 (required grade 6 fluency) | "336 ÷ 24" | Correct but slow → fluency bottleneck, give the tool. Wrong → procedural gap; do not block rate work on it |

**Three gap types, three responses.**

- **Conceptual (division direction, quotients under 1).** The model was never built. These cannot be scaffolded around and deserve a five-minute just-in-time re-anchor before the better-buy lesson — not a week of pre-teaching. The Tier 3 practitioner consensus favors grade-level content with just-in-time prerequisite support over pulling students back first; label that as practitioner consensus, because it is.
- **Procedural (equivalent ratios, scaling).** Responds to clean **correct** worked examples — the meta-analysis found correct-only examples outperformed incorrect-only and correct-plus-incorrect, and that adding self-explanation prompts moderated the effect *negatively* (Barbieri et al., 2023, g = 0.48 across 55 studies) [T1]. So: a completed example beside a matched blank, and skip the "explain your thinking" box.
- **Fluency bottleneck (division).** Give the calculator and the multiplication chart, consistently, **including on the check.** Ellington (2003) [T1], 54 studies: results went mixed specifically "when calculators were not part of assessment." Handing the tool out only on hard days is the condition where the benefit disappeared. Frame it as a construct argument to anyone reviewing: 6.RP.A.3b assesses rate reasoning, not long division. **Honest caveat: I found no research at all on multiplication charts as a scaffold** — that recommendation is reasoning from cognitive load theory, not a retrieved finding.

**Differentiation as one task, not three lessons.**
- **Number choice within one problem set.** Same context; target quantities of 4, 10, and 7.4. Everyone works the same problem. The ceiling is in the numbers and the between-measure demand rises with them.
- **Problem-type sorting before solving.** Card sort: which of these needs a rate, and which is asking for a total? This is the SBI component with RCT evidence [T1], and it is the one move the comparison literature specifically endorses for low-prior-knowledge students — Durkin, Star & Rittle-Johnson [T2] found students without prior knowledge "learned best from either comparing problem types or studying examples sequentially," and that side-by-side method comparison "may be too overwhelming... without sufficient prior knowledge."
- **Both-ways rates as a leveler.** Computing both unit rates requires no more knowledge than computing one, and the *choosing* is where the depth is. A student two years behind can participate on the decision.

**Above grade level.** Reverse it. "The unit rate is $2.75 per pound. Write a ratio that produces it." Or: "Here are two rates from the same situation, one per-dollar and one per-item. Show that they multiply to 1, and explain why." Or error-finding: "A student got 0.25 dollars per balloon for '6 balloons for $24.' What did they do, and what question *does* their answer answer?" That last one is the most valuable extension because it produces exactly the misconception analysis you want circulating in the room.

### 8. Open questions and weak spots

**This is the thinnest of the three briefs, and the thinness is concentrated in exactly the place you asked about.** I searched specifically for empirical research on unit-rate misconceptions — the belief that a unit rate is not recognized as a ratio with denominator 1, reversed division in better-buy problems, ratio-versus-rate confusion — and **retrieved essentially nothing.** Multiple targeted searches returned curriculum materials and commercial worksheet products, not studies. Several search summaries offered confident-sounding findings about reverse-division errors; none could be verified against retrieved text and none appear in this brief.

**The central argument of this brief is an inference, and it should be labeled as one whenever it is repeated.** Küchemann et al.'s 91%/51% finding is real, measured, and published — but the study is about double number lines and the scalar/functional distinction, not about unit rate. The mapping from "the between-measure move is hard" to "applying a unit rate is where students fail" is mine. I think it is a sound mapping and it explains a phenomenon teachers recognize, but it is a bridge, not a finding.

**There is no meta-analysis of proportional reasoning instruction.** Verified directly against ERIC with two query forms. There is no meta-analysis of unit rate instruction at any grade level.

**The one WWC recommendation touching ratio and rate is rated Minimal Evidence** — the lowest of three WWC levels. Its base, in the WWC's own words: "three studies... demonstrated that manipulatives and visual representations can be beneficial teaching tools for teaching proportions to students in grades 4, 5, and 7," plus three more that did not meet standards. The sequence it recommends is sound and I am recommending it; it is not carried by strong evidence.

**The systematic review of proportional reasoning interventions for students like yours says the field is thin.** Nelson et al. (2022) [T1], grades 5–9, students with LD or mathematics difficulty: nine studies met inclusion, effects ranged **g = −0.10 to 1.87**, only two rated high quality, "very few studies included participants with LD." The authors declined to pool and concluded the area "is under investigated."

**The RCT base is one research program.** Every randomized trial retrieved on teaching ratio, proportion, and percent traces to Jitendra's schema-based instruction under IES grant R305A110358.

**Effects shrink in your conditions, and the growth finding is the one to sit with.** For the 806 students with mathematics difficulties in the SBI trial, the effect was 0.32 SD posttest versus 0.46 SD for the full sample — and **"growth rates did not vary significantly between students and, thus, treatment did not moderate student growth."** The intervention lifted the level without changing the rate of learning. Krowka et al. independently found grades 3–6 outperformed grades 7–9 (p < .001) and research personnel outperformed school personnel.

**Nothing transferred to standardized tests.** Null on the GMADE and on end-of-year state math achievement.

**Marked unverified or transferred.** (1) The MMBDMS misconception (*Multiplication Makes Bigger, Division Makes Smaller*), attributed to Bell, Swan & Taylor (1981) — retrieved only as cited by Baratta et al. (2010); **primary source not retrieved.** (2) Vergnaud (1983), "Multiplicative structures," the origin of the scalar/functional distinction — **citation confirmed inside a retrieved source, primary text not retrieved.** (3) Lamon's developmental levels for proportional reasoning and the units-coordination stage framework — surfaced in searches, **not retrieved**; if you want named developmental levels for a coach conversation, that is an open retrieval. (4) Four flagship Van Dooren papers (2005 *C&I*; 2008 and 2009 *JRME*; 2010 *C&I*) were paywalled with no reachable mirror; nothing from them is quoted numerically anywhere in these briefs.

**What I would want and could not find:** a study comparing unit-rate-first against ratio-table-first sequencing for middle-grades students. That is the single decision this brief makes most confidently against common practice, and it rests on converging design documents plus one measured asymmetry rather than on a trial. If someone challenges the recommendation, that is the honest answer to give them.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

Four days, mapped to the CRA class-state spine. **Read the pacing note at the end before committing to four.**

**Day 1 — Partitioning down to one**
- Warm-up (2 review + 3 current): review = "$12 for 5 notebooks, what does one cost?" and "which is more, 3 ÷ 4 or 4 ÷ 3?" — both prerequisite diagnostics from §7. Current Q3 targets **quotients less than 1**: "12 ÷ 20."
- Concrete: partition-to-one with physical batches. Representational: record each pair in a table. Abstract: name the last one.
- The words "unit rate" arrive at the *end* of this lesson, as a name for something already built.

**Day 2 — Double number line, with 1 marked**
- Warm-up Q3 targets **notation**: "A student wrote 3 m = 2 sec. Is that right? What would you write?"
- Build the first double number line with the friendly multiplier running **along** a line, per Küchemann. Do not open on a between-lines multiplier.
- Mark 1. The unit rate is the pair at 1 — an observation students make, not a definition you give.

**Day 3 — Applying the rate (the hard day)**
- Warm-up Q3 targets **division direction**: "6 balloons for $24. A student wrote 24 ÷ 5 to find the cost of 5. What were they thinking?"
- Non-integer target quantities throughout. Estimate before every computation, out loud, and *use* the estimate to check.
- This is the 51% day. Expect it to be harder than day 2 and plan the time accordingly.

**Day 4 — Better buy, both ways**
- Warm-up Q3 is the washing-powder item with its four distractors — the 60% absolute-comparison trap.
- Both-ways unit rate: compute per-dollar and per-item, decide which answers the question.
- Exit: a better-buy problem where the cheaper item is the worse value.

**The pacing conflict, stated plainly.** Four days is what fits. The retrieved dose evidence says it is not enough for the students you are most worried about — Jitendra's 9–10 session versions of this intervention produced *nothing* for students with mathematics difficulties, and the rational-number meta-analysis puts the significant duration threshold at 20+ hours. And unit rate is sharing a district topic with percents *and* conversions, so there is no slack inside Topic 2 to take.

The honest options are two, and neither is compressing the days above. **Either** take days from the conversions strand (CCSD progression 4), which is ratio-table machinery applied in a new context rather than new mathematics and can largely be absorbed into the ratio-table work you have already done — **or** accept four days and commit to **distributed re-exposure**: rate problems in the warm-up spiral every week for the rest of the semester. The interleaving evidence supports the second option independently and it costs zero instructional days. If you can only do one thing, do the spiral.
