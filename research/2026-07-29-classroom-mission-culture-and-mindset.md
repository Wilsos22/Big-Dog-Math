# Classroom Mission, Culture, and the "How to Think" Question — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Compiled:** 2026-07-29
**Scope:** Four questions, answered against retrieved literature: (1) is there a defensible evidence base for a classroom mission statement or values framework; (2) is "teach how to think" more effective than explicit instruction; (3) is growth mindset a proven instructional framework; (4) what classroom culture and norms produce the best outcomes both for running the room and for student growth after they leave it. Deliberately left out: schoolwide/administrative reform, curriculum adoption, and topic-specific math pedagogy — this is about the operating stance, not the content.

**Evidence at a glance:** ~24 Tier 1 · ~12 Tier 2 · ~10 Tier 3/4 sources retrieved this session. The evidence is **strong** on what teachers *do* daily (explicit instruction for novices, domain-embedded metacognition, autonomy support, utility-value framing, teacher expectations, classroom management as a bundle) and on which student outcomes carry long-run weight. It is **thin to nonexistent** on mission statements themselves, on co-constructed norms as an isolated practice, and on the entire family of professionally-endorsed math-discourse frameworks. Growth mindset is **contested**, and the version teachers actually implement is the version that has repeatedly failed.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*One page. Read this before you write a word of the mission statement.*

### The short version

Nobody has ever shown that a mission statement changes a student. The two empirical papers on school mission statements are a coding-methodology study and a correlational extreme-groups comparison whose own authors write that "cause and effect conclusions are not warranted." So write the mission — but write it as a **constraint on your own decisions**, not as a claim about children. Its job is to make you choose the same way on a Tuesday in February as you would in August.

The good news: what the mission should *contain* is very well evidenced, and your existing instincts are closer to right than most classroom philosophies. "How to think, not what to think" is directionally correct and, as usually implemented, wrong — because the evidence says thinking is taught **explicitly, inside math content, by a teacher modeling it aloud**, not discovered and not taught as a separate strand. Your three-step routine (confused → what do you know → try something) is a metacognitive self-questioning protocol, and teacher-modeled self-questioning is one of only two moves in the federal grades 4–8 math problem-solving guide rated **Strong**. You are already holding the right thing. The upgrade is to *model it out loud on your own confusion* rather than post it on a wall.

### The four questions, answered

1. **Best mission framework?** Self-Determination Theory — autonomy and competence support — has by far the strongest evidence of any framework here (51 intervention studies including 38 RCTs). CASEL and character frameworks are taxonomies, not interventions, and the largest federal RCT of schoolwide character/SEL programming found *nothing*. Build on SDT plus utility-value.
2. **"How to think" vs. explicit instruction?** False dichotomy, and the evidence resolves it cleanly: **explicitly teach domain-specific thinking**. Generic thinking-skills instruction does not transfer; unassisted discovery loses to explicit instruction at every age (d = −0.38, largest gap in adolescents). But *enhanced* discovery — generate, explain, get feedback — wins (d = +0.30), and 6th–10th grade is the first band where problem-solving-before-instruction flips positive (g = 0.50).
3. **Is growth mindset proven?** **No — not as an instructional framework.** As a one-hour standardized module delivered *to* students it produces ~0.05–0.14 SD, contested. As teacher PD plus teacher-delivered lessons — the version schools buy — a 101-school, 5,018-pupil trial got ES = −0.01 in maths. Yeager and Dweck concede this in print. Your growth mindset matters as *context*; teaching it as *curriculum* is not supported.
4. **What culture produces the best outcomes?** A room where errors are safe (this is measurable and it decays by default in grades 5–6), relationships are strong (correlations are *larger* in low-SES and secondary samples), expectations are high and acted on (g = 0.30), students get real choices inside structured tasks, and every task has a stated reason it matters.

### What to build the mission on — the four load-bearing clauses

| Clause | The evidence behind it |
|---|---|
| **Confusion is information, not failure** | Error climate; willingness to engage with mistakes *declines* across grades 5–6 unless the classroom counteracts it (1,641 students, 69 math classrooms) |
| **You will always know why this matters** | Utility-value RCTs; effects concentrated in students with *low* success expectations — exactly your kids |
| **I hold the standard, and I expect you to meet it** | Teacher-expectation interventions, g = 0.30 on achievement; wise feedback raised revision rates most among low-trust students |
| **You get real choices inside a structured task** | Autonomy support: 38 RCTs; autonomy g = 1.14, competence g = 0.48 in experimental designs |

Note the one thing *not* on this list: belonging as a stated value. It's a good value and I'd keep it — but relatedness was the one SDT leg that came back **non-significant** in the 2024 meta-analysis, so don't build the defensible argument on it.

### What to optimize for — the actual answer to "growth beyond my class"

Teacher effects on test scores and teacher effects on *behavior* correlate only **0.22**. They are nearly different skills. And the behavior dimension is worth roughly **12:1 over test-score value-added** for whether a student graduates. In a randomized experiment, classroom-quality effects on test scores **faded**; the non-cognitive gains **persisted**, and the earnings effect tracked the part that lasted.

So the metrics your mission should commit you to: **attendance, work completion, on-time progression to the next course, math self-efficacy, and whether kids feel connected.** Nobody else is covering these, and they're the part of your effect that still exists in ten years.

### What to stop doing, or never start

- **A "thinking skills" strand or unit.** EEF is explicit: metacognitive strategies "should be taught and applied to the usual curriculum content, rather than taught discretely through 'thinking skills' lessons."
- **Growth mindset posters, praise-the-effort, and mindset language without changed practice.** Dweck's own 2015 commentary reports that math teachers who endorsed growth mindset and said the words but didn't follow through in practice had students who endorsed **more** of a fixed mindset about math ability.
- **Grit as a brand.** Meta-analytically deflated (584 effect sizes, 66,807 people); "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness," construct validity in question. If you target anything here, target *perseverance of effort* on hard tasks — the one facet that survived.
- **Buying a character-education or SEL package on the strength of "SEL works."** 93 studies of 41 character programs went to WWC; 18 studies of 13 programs met standards. The 84-school, three-year federal RCT of seven schoolwide social/character programs found **no differences** on any student outcome.

### Watch out for

- **The most popular math-culture frameworks are the least tested.** NCTM's eight Mathematics Teaching Practices, the Five Practices for orchestrating discussion, Accountable Talk, 2×10, and greeting at the door are professionally endorsed and, as named packages, experimentally untested — one of them was reviewed by WWC and rated "Does Not Meet Evidence Standards." Use them; don't cite them as evidence.
- **Effects fade when support is withdrawn.** The dialogic-teaching RCT got +1 month in maths — and a year later, no effect on national tests. Culture is not a thing you install; it's a thing you run.
- **Your best culture moves are cheap and one-off.** The single most transferable study in this brief is a **one-session** reading-and-writing activity with 1,304 transitioning 6th graders that cut disciplinary incidents 34%, raised attendance 12%, and cut failing grades 18% district-wide.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. Mission statements: the honest answer

There is no causal evidence that a classroom or school mission statement improves anything. This is not a search failure — it is the state of the field, and the researchers say so themselves.

Stemler, Bebell & Sonnabend (2011) coded 421 high school mission statements from random samples across ten states and found that schools "vary systematically and sensibly" in their themes and that principals regard the statements as important tools [T4]. That paper contains **no student-outcome analysis at all**; its contribution is that mission statements can be reliably coded, which makes them a useful *data source* for researchers.

Slate et al. (2008) is the closest thing to an outcome study [T3]. They compared 100 Texas elementary schools drawn from the extremes of the state rating scale — 50 "Exemplary," 50 "Academically Unacceptable." Six of fifteen coded themes differed significantly. Academic Success appeared in 36 Exemplary vs. 18 Unacceptable missions (χ² = 13.04, p < .0001, ES .36); a Challenging environment in 19 vs. 4 (χ² = 12.70, p < .0001, ES .36). A discriminant function correctly classified 74% of schools (Wilks' λ = .68, canonical r = .56). Striking detail: in the low-performing schools, academic success appeared in **18%** of missions and a challenging environment in **4%**. The authors' own limitation section is the load-bearing sentence: "the quantitative data are correlational… cause and effect conclusions are not warranted… both may be attributable to a common factor such as high quality administration." They add that "experimental manipulation of the mission statements of schools is neither practical nor ethical."

So the defensible claim is narrow and worth stating precisely, because it is the version that survives a skeptical department chair: **high-performing schools name academic success and challenge in their missions far more often than low-performing schools, and nobody has shown the statement causes it.**

**The instructional coherence argument** is the strongest adjacent case, and it is weaker than its reputation. Newmann, Smith, Allensworth & Bryk (2001) surveyed 222 Chicago elementary schools in 1994 and 1997 (5,358 and 5,560 teachers), merged with ITBS scores for ~81,493 students per year, and found via HLM that "students in Chicago elementary schools with stronger program coherence show higher gains in student achievement" [T3]. Coherence was *measured by teacher survey*, not assigned. ERIC classifies the companion EEPA article's publication type as "Reports — Descriptive" [T3/T4]. The strongest causal hint for coherence is actually indirect: in the Responsive Classroom RCT (24 schools, 2,904 children, grades 2–5), assignment to the program produced **no** achievement advantage, but *use of the practices* predicted achievement (ES 0.26 math, 0.30 reading; 0.89 for math among students with low initial math achievement) [T2, and note WWC rated this study "Does Not Meet Evidence Standards"]. That is a fidelity finding, not a causal coherence finding — but it points the same direction as de Boer et al.'s result that teacher *support for* an intervention, not its type, moderated effectiveness.

**Recommendation.** Write the mission. Justify it instrumentally, not empirically: it is a decision constraint that keeps your February choices consistent with your August ones, and consistency of enactment is the variable that keeps showing up as the moderator. Do not tell anyone the research says a mission statement raises achievement. It doesn't say that.

**What would flip me:** a randomized or well-matched quasi-experimental trial of stated-and-enacted classroom values vs. business-as-usual with student outcomes. As far as I can retrieve, it doesn't exist.

### 2. "How to think" vs. explicit instruction — the dichotomy is wrong, and the resolution is specific

This is the question where the literature is richest and where your framing needs the most sharpening.

**Unassisted discovery loses, badly, and worst for adolescents.** Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum (2011) meta-analyzed 164 studies [T1]. Unassisted discovery vs. explicit instruction: 580 comparisons, N = 25,986, **d = −0.38, 95% CI [−.44, −.31]**, favoring explicit instruction. Every age group favored explicit instruction, and **adolescents showed the largest gap (d = −0.53)**. Broken out by what discovery was compared against: worked examples d = −0.63, feedback −0.46, direct teaching −0.29.

**But enhanced discovery wins.** Same paper, second meta-analysis: 360 comparisons from 56 studies, **d = +0.30 [.23, .36]** favoring *enhanced* discovery — defined as generation, elicited explanation, or guided discovery. The authors' own summary: "unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations do." One caution for your band: the effect was significantly larger for adults than for children (children coded as ≤12 / through 6th grade).

**Guidance amount, not inquiry-or-not, is the real variable.** Lazonder & Harmsen (2016) meta-analyzed 72 inquiry studies [T1]: guidance within inquiry produced d = 0.66 on learning activities, 0.71 on performance success, and **0.50 on learning outcomes**. Their taxonomy runs least→most specific: process constraints → status overviews → prompts → heuristics → scaffolds → explanations. That taxonomy is a better planning tool than the word "inquiry."

**Sixth grade is the flip point for problem-solving-first.** Sinha & Kapur (2021) meta-analyzed 53 productive-failure studies / 166 comparisons [T1]. Overall **g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51]** favoring problem-solving-before-instruction. The moderator table is what matters to you:

| Band | Effect |
|---|---|
| 2nd–5th grade | **g = −0.09** (favors instruction-first) |
| **6th–10th grade** | **g = 0.50** (favors problem-solving-first) |
| Undergraduates | g = 0.28 |
| **Domain-general skills** | **g = −0.17** (favors instruction-first) |
| Mathematics | g = 0.48 |

Two conditions attach. First, the effect depends on actually implementing the productive-failure design features — fidelity was a significant predictor (β = 0.0065, p < .001), and specifically for 6th–10th graders (β = 0.0190, p < .0001). Second, interventions spanning a few *days* had far higher fidelity than a-few-*hours* ones (mean 74.45 vs. 42.01); the authors warn that "cramming too many design features within a short amount of time may not be optimal."

**The counterweight, and it's a real one.** Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller ran two randomized experiments with Year 5 students [T1]. Experiment 1 (N = 64): explicit instruction then problem solving beat the reverse on similar problems, tie on transfer. Experiment 2 (N = 71, higher element interactivity): instruction-first beat problem-solving-first on **both** similar and transfer problems. Their conclusion: "for learning where element interactivity is high, explicit instruction should precede problem solving." Note this tested Year 5 — squarely in the band where Sinha & Kapur's own table predicts instruction-first should win. These two findings are less contradictory than they look.

Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) supplies the mechanism [T3 — it is an argument paper, not a meta-analysis, and should be cited as such]. Their claim is narrower than it's usually reported: guidance is superior for *novices*, and "the advantage of guidance begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge to provide 'internal' guidance." They also make a point worth stealing for your mission: "it may be a fundamental error to assume that the pedagogic content of the learning experience is identical to the methods and processes (i.e., the epistemology) of the discipline being studied." Doing math like a mathematician is not the same thing as learning math efficiently. Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007) reply that Kirschner et al. "mistakenly conflated PBL and IL with discovery learning" and that real PBL is heavily scaffolded [T3] — which, given Alfieri, is probably the correct read of the whole fight.

**Does "critical thinking" transfer? Mostly no — and this is the part that should change your language.** Willingham's synthesis is blunt: "Critical thinking is not a set of skills that can be deployed at any time, in any context. It is a type of thought… very much dependent on domain knowledge and practice" [T3]. In the classic transfer experiment he cites, **19%** of subjects spontaneously transferred a solution to a structurally identical problem; **35%** did when explicitly told the two were similar. He notes that when transfer has been tested in curricular thinking-skills programs, "positive results have been absent or modest and quick to fade."

He also names two techniques with evidence behind them that you can use tomorrow: **problem comparison** (present two structurally identical, superficially different solved problems side by side) and **subgoal labeling** (label each substep with the goal it serves, so a small variation doesn't strand the student).

Abrami et al.'s meta-analyses cut the same way despite sounding contrary. The 2008 synthesis (117 studies, 20,698 participants, 161 effects) found **g+ = 0.341**, highly heterogeneous, with the conclusion that "improvement in students' CT skills and dispositions cannot be a matter of implicit expectation… educators must take steps to make CT objectives explicit in courses" [T1]. The 2015 update reports overall **g = 0.30**, but the breakdown is the tell: **content-specific critical thinking g = 0.57** vs. **disposition g = 0.23**. Teacher questioning g = 0.38; whole-class guided discussion 0.42; small-group guided discussion 0.41. And the qualitative finding: promotion "was most effective when abstract principles were explicitly taught and trained and then illustrated, applied, and deepened using concrete topics." *(These 2015 numbers come from a research-summary PDF, not the original article — see Sources.)*

**Metacognition is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost thing in this entire lane, and it must live inside math.** EEF's metacognition and self-regulation strand: **+8 months** additional progress, "high impact for very low cost based on extensive evidence," 355 studies [T1]. The strand's own guidance: "Research suggests these should be taught and applied to the usual curriculum content, **rather than taught discretely through 'thinking skills' lessons**." Also from that page: impact is similar across age groups, "approaches in mathematics [are] very successful," "studies with higher numbers of disadvantaged pupils had a high impact," and — the sentence you should tape to your desk — "**Some studies suggest disadvantaged pupils are less likely to use metacognitive and self-regulation strategies unless explicitly taught to do so.**"

Two meta-analyses corroborate, and both find math is where this pays best. Dignath & Büttner (2008): 49 primary + 35 secondary studies, 357 effect sizes, **average effect size 0.69**, with interventions attaining higher effects "in the scope of mathematics than in reading/writing or other subjects" [T1]. Donker et al. (2014): 58 studies, 180 effect sizes, mean **Hedges' g = .66**; by domain writing 1.25, science .73, **math .66**, reading comprehension .36 [T1]. Their conclusion: "the most effective strategies are planning and the motivational aspect task value, and instructing metacognitive knowledge enhances effectiveness of strategies."

A methodological warning both papers hand you: effects shrink on outcome measures the researchers didn't write. Donker's overall drops from g = .78 on self-developed measures to **.45** on intervention-independent ones. Math is the exception — it *rose*, .61 → **.84**. Dignath & Büttner similarly found effects larger when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered training. Discount headline effect sizes; math-specific strategy instruction survives the discount better than most.

**The federal guides put a floor under all of this.** *Improving Mathematical Problem Solving in Grades 4 Through 8* (IES, 2012, rev. 2018) rates exactly two recommendations **Strong**: (2) assist students in monitoring and reflecting on the problem-solving process, and (3) teach students how to use visual representations [T1]. The evidence summary notes **five studies found performance improved when teachers modeled a self-questioning process and then had students practice it**. Its *weakest*-rated recommendation, at **Minimal**, is "prepare problems and use them in whole-class instruction" — worth knowing before you spend a planning weekend hunting for the perfect rich task.

And for the bottom of your range: *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades* (WWC, 2021) rates **all six recommendations Strong** — systematic instruction, clear and concise mathematical language, concrete and semi-concrete representations, the number line, deliberate instruction on word problems, and regular timed fluency activities [T1]. For students two years behind, the whole stack is explicit and systematic. This is the least ambiguous evidence in the brief.

**The middle ground that actually works, at your grade level.** Jitendra et al. (2012), a cluster-randomized experiment with 7th graders on ratios/rates/percents, found schema-based instruction beat business-as-usual at **g = 1.24** posttest and **g = 1.27** at six-week retention [T1]. The follow-up randomized trial with **806 seventh graders with mathematics problem-solving difficulties** across 82 randomly assigned classrooms found SBI students **0.32 SD** higher at posttest and **0.25 SD** higher nine weeks later [T1]. What moved these students was explicitly teaching the *schema of a problem type* — not a general heuristic.

#### Where the research disagrees

The genuine disagreement is Sinha & Kapur (problem-solving-first, g = 0.36) vs. Ashman/Kalyuga/Sweller (instruction-first, both experiments) vs. Alfieri (explicit beats unassisted discovery at every age). It hinges on three moderators that the sources themselves name: **age** (the flip is between 5th and 6th grade), **element interactivity** (the instruction-first advantage grows with complexity, and Sinha & Kapur don't code for it), and **fidelity/duration** (the PF advantage requires actually running the design features over days, not a single period). There's also a publication-bias asterisk on both sides: Sinha & Kapur's bias-corrected estimate is *larger* than the raw one (unusual), and Alfieri found unpublished datasets on enhanced discovery ran the opposite direction (d = −0.54) from journal articles.

**My call.** For 6th grade math content with your spread: **give the problem first, but only when the content is low-to-moderate element interactivity, only with real scaffolds inside the attempt, and always with explicit consolidating instruction after.** For anything high-element-interactivity, or for your students furthest behind, teach first and let the problem come second — WWC 2021's six Strong recommendations are unambiguous about this population. Never run a thinking-skills strand.

**What would flip me:** if a study coded element interactivity within the 6th–10th band and found the PS-I advantage holds even at high interactivity, I'd extend problem-first to the harder units. Right now the two literatures pass each other without meeting on that variable.

**And on your slogan.** "How to think, not what to think" is right in spirit and dangerous in shorthand, because the two things it most often licenses — unguided discovery and generic thinking instruction — are the two things this literature rejects most clearly. The precise version is: **think out loud in front of them, on real math, and make them practice the same questions you asked yourself.** Your confused → what do you know → try something sequence *is* a self-questioning protocol, and teacher-modeled self-questioning is one of the two Strong-rated recommendations in the grades 4–8 guide. Model it; don't post it.

### 3. Growth mindset — not a proven instructional framework

Short answer: **the psychological lever has small, contested, conditional evidence. The instructional framework has repeatedly failed. They are not the same intervention.**

**The best case for it.** Yeager et al. (2019), *Nature* [T1]: the National Study of Learning Mindsets. Stratified random sample of 65 US public high schools, **12,490 ninth-graders individually randomized within school**, two online sessions under an hour total, self-administered, teachers blind to condition, pre-registered, independent data build by MDRC, blinded Bayesian causal forest corroboration. For the pre-registered lower-achieving subgroup (n = 6,320): **+0.10 grade points [0.04, 0.16], SMD = 0.11**; poor-performance rate fell from 46% to 41%. Advanced math enrollment in 10th grade rose 3 pp (33% → 36%, p = 0.001) — exploratory, not pre-registered, but the most interesting result for a math teacher because course-taking is a durable channel.

Critically, the effect on GPA **varied across schools** (τ̂ = 0.09, p = 0.038) while the effect on mindset beliefs did not. It was smaller in higher-achieving schools (β = −0.25) and larger where peer norms already favored challenge-seeking (β = 0.23). Yeager et al. (2022, *Psychological Science*) matched 9,167 student records to 223 math teachers and found students "could not independently implement growth mindsets in any classroom culture" — effects required teachers who themselves held growth mindsets [T2].

**The case against.** Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023), *Psychological Bulletin* [T1]: 63 studies, N = 97,672, **d̄ = 0.05 [0.02, 0.09]**, "nonsignificant after correcting for potential publication bias." Restricting to studies that demonstrably shifted mindsets: 13 studies, N = 18,355, **d̄ = 0.04 [−0.01, 0.10]**, n.s. Highest-quality evidence only: 6 studies, N = 13,571, **d̄ = 0.02 [−0.06, 0.10]**, n.s. **No theoretically meaningful moderator was significant** — not academic challenge, not SES, not dosage, not classroom integration, not who administered it. Published effects d̄ = 0.09 vs. unpublished 0.04. Their abstract states that "authors with a financial incentive to report positive findings published significantly larger effects."

Sisk et al. (2018) found the mindset–achievement correlation accounts for about **1% of variance** across k = 273, N = 365,915, and intervention **d̄ = 0.08 [0.02, 0.14]** across k = 43, N = 57,155 [T1]. Their own hedge is important for you: "some results supported specific tenets of the theory, namely, that students with low socioeconomic status or who are academically at risk might benefit."

**The fairest single number.** Burnette et al. (2023), *Psychological Bulletin*, randomized designs only, 53 independent samples [T1]. Under the best conditions the friendly meta-analysis can specify — targeted subsamples *and* high implementation fidelity — **academic achievement d = 0.14 [.06, .22]**. But the **95% prediction interval for a new study runs −0.08 to 0.35.** Plan around 0.14 at best; expect a range that includes zero.

**The finding that decides this for a classroom teacher.** Every trial that worked used a short, standardized, self-administered module delivered *around* the teacher. Every trial of the teacher-delivered version failed:

- **Foliano et al. (2019), EEF "Changing Mindsets" effectiveness trial** [T1, EEF security rating: **high**]. 101 schools, 5,018 Year 6 pupils, teacher PD then teachers delivering weekly sessions over 8 weeks plus embedded growth-mindset feedback. **KS2 maths ES = −0.01 [−0.04, 0.01]. Reading −0.00. GPS −0.00. FSM subgroup: also zero.** All four non-cognitive outcomes n.s. Process evaluation found it was delivered as intended and well received; teachers *perceived* increased enthusiasm and perseverance.
- **Rienzo et al. (2015), EEF** [T1]. Teacher-PD arm (30 schools, 1,505 pupils): no additional progress in maths, *less* progress in English than control (n.s.). The pupil-workshop arm got maths g = 0.10 and English g = 0.18, both non-significant — EEF describes this as "two additional months' progress… not statistically significant."
- **Ganimian (2020)**, 202 public secondary schools in Salta, Argentina, fidelity verified in 90% of treatment schools [T1]: "no evidence that it affected students' propensity to find tasks less intimidating, school climate, school performance, achievement, or post-secondary plans. **I rule out small effects.**" Hints of *negative* effects on female students, low-income students, and grade-repeaters.

Yeager & Dweck (2020) concede it in print: "**So far, teacher-focused growth mindset interventions have not worked (Foliano et al., 2019; Rienzo et al., 2015), even though they were developed with great care and were labor-intensive**" [T2].

**And Dweck's own warning about the classroom version.** In her 2015 *Education Week* commentary [T4] she writes that "perhaps the most common misconception is simply equating the growth mindset with effort," that praise is "too often" given to students "who are putting forth effort, but not learning," and that she fears the concepts "will be used to perpetuate" the failed self-esteem movement. She cites Kathy Liu Sun's finding that **math teachers who endorsed growth mindset and said the words in class but didn't follow through in practice had students who endorsed *more* of a fixed mindset about math ability.** She also says: "if we use mindset measures for accountability, we will create false growth mindsets on an unprecedented scale."

**Dosage runs the wrong way, which kills the obvious rebuttal.** Macnamara & Burgoyne found number of sessions non-significant. Foliano's 8-week embedded version got zero; Yeager's two-session, under-an-hour version got 0.11. "The null trials just didn't do enough of it" is not supported.

**Better-evidenced substitutes, in order of how well they fit your room.**

1. **Reappraising adversity at the 6th grade transition — the single best-fitting study in this brief.** Borman, Rozek, Pyne & Hanselman (2019), *PNAS* [T1]: district-wide **double-blind randomized field trial, n = 1,304 transitioning sixth graders**, all middle schools in Madison. A brief reading-and-writing activity teaching that middle-school adversity is common, short-lived, and externally caused. **Disciplinary incidents down 34%. Attendance up 12%. Failing grades down 18%.** Group differences were not statistically significant, though descriptively larger for historically underserved minority students and boys. 80% of long-term GPA effects were accounted for by changes in students' attitudes and behaviors. *(A direct replication exists — Borman et al., 2025, AERA Open — which I identified but did not read. Read it before you lean hard on this.)*
2. **Utility value.** Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009), *Science*, randomized field experiment [T2]: having students write about how course material connects to their lives "increased interest in science and course grades **for students with low success expectations**." Rozek et al. (2017), *PNAS* [T2]: a three-part utility-value intervention delivered to *parents* increased math/science ACT scores (ES 0.26) and 11th/12th grade STEM course-taking (ES 0.22), with STEM career pursuit five years later — though that last link is associational.
3. **Wise feedback.** Yeager et al. (2014), *JEP: General* [T2]: three double-blind randomized field experiments. Adding one sentence to critical written feedback — that you hold high standards *and* believe the student can meet them — raised revision rates. **Among low-trust African American 7th graders, 87% vs. 62% revised**, and the two-year decline in trust seen in the control condition was halted.
4. **Belonging** has the best-replicated large-scale evidence but a hard condition attached. Walton et al. (2023), *Science* [T1]: 26,911 students, 22 institutions, online activity under 30 minutes, raised first-year completion especially in historically lower-progressing groups — but "**the intervention was effective only when students' groups were afforded opportunities to belong.**" The original Walton & Cohen (2011) *Science* study was **N = 92**; WWC's 2022 synthesis of 7 qualifying studies (all postsecondary) rates academic achievement **"Mixed effects," improvement index +6**.

**My call.** Do not run growth mindset as curriculum, PD, or wall art. *Hold* it yourself and enact it in task design and feedback — that's the moderator with support. Where you want a motivational lever, spend it on utility value (cheapest, best-fitting, effect concentrated in low-expectancy students) and wise feedback (one sentence, and the effect was largest for exactly the students who trust school least). If you want a single first-week move, the 6th-grade adversity-reappraisal activity is the best-evidenced thing available for your exact population.

**What would flip me:** a well-powered, independently evaluated trial of *teacher-delivered* mindset practice showing a non-trivial achievement effect. Three large ones have now failed, and the proponents concede it, so I'd want more than one.

### 4. Classroom culture and norms — what carries weight

**The single most on-target finding in this whole run.** Dresel, Daumiller, Spear, Janke, Dickhäuser & Steuer (2024/25), *British Journal of Educational Psychology* [T3]: **1,641 students in 69 mathematics classrooms, five measurement points across fifth and sixth grade**, growth-curve modeling. Two findings. First, on average, students' individual reactions to errors **developed *negatively* over the two years** — willingness to engage adaptively with mistakes decays by default across grades 5–6. Second, that decline **varied substantially between classrooms and systematically depended on perceived error climate**: a more positive error climate meant a less negative trajectory. The authors conclude there is "a strong need and considerable room" for teacher support here.

This is empirical backing for "confusion is step one" that is math-specific, sits exactly on your grade band, and — crucially — establishes that the willingness to share a wrong answer is *not stable*. It erodes unless the room actively counteracts it, and the classroom is the unit where the difference shows up. It's correlational, not causal, so state it as "classrooms differ and the difference tracks error climate," not "positive error climate causes retention of error tolerance."

**Relationships.** Roorda, Koomen, Spilt & Oort (2011), 99 studies [T3]: positive relationships × engagement **r = .39**; negative × engagement **r = −.32**; positive × achievement **r = .16**; negative × achievement **−.15 to −.18**. A method artifact worth knowing: positive relationships correlate **r = .24 with grades but only r = .07 with test scores** — much of the "relationships raise achievement" story lives in teacher-assigned grades. The moderators are directly relevant: effects are **larger in higher grades**, positive relationships matter more in secondary, and **SES was a significant moderator in the same direction for all four associations — associations were larger in samples with more low-SES students.** The 2017 meta-analytic SEM update [T3] found engagement only *partially* mediates: positive → engagement β = .29, engagement → achievement β = .24, total effect of positive relationships on achievement **.14**. Practical read: convert rapport into participation; rapport alone isn't the mechanism.

Allen, Kern, Vella-Brodrick, Hattie & Waters (2018), 51 studies, N = 67,378 [T3]: of ten predictors of school belonging, **teacher support was the strongest, r = .46 [.37, .54]** — ahead of personal characteristics (.44), parent support (.33), and peer support (.32).

**Classroom management as a bundle.** Korpershoek et al. (2016), 54 controlled studies, primary grades [T1/T2]: overall **g = 0.22**; academic .17, behavior .24, social-emotional .21, motivation **.08 (n.s.)**. Trim-and-fill adjusted to g = 0.17. The 2025 update (76 studies through 2022) found **g = .23** with **no publication bias detected**. Three findings you can act on: the **social-emotional-development component** was the only one that meaningfully raised effects (.24 with vs. .15 without; on SEL outcomes .25 vs. .04); **low-SES g = .20 vs. mid/high .21 — no significant difference**, so this works the same in Title 1; and **duration made no difference** (<13 weeks vs. >1 year, Q = 0.18, p = .92). Also worth swallowing: students report less improvement than adults do (student-rated .16 vs. observer .30).

The 1982 Emmer et al. junior-high field experiment (18 treatment vs. 20 control teachers) remains the classic basis for explicit expectation-setting in the first days of school [T2] — treatment teachers "established classes with more appropriate, task-oriented student behavior." The outcome is observed behavior, not achievement, and n = 38 teachers.

**Autonomy support — the best-evidenced *stance* in this brief.** Su & Reeve (2011), 19 studies [T1]: autonomy-support training was effective at **d = 0.63** — but note the outcome is whether training changed the *teacher's* behavior, not student achievement. Reeve & Cheon (2021) [T1] report that **51 autonomy-supportive teaching interventions, including 38 RCTs**, show teachers can learn the stance and that once learned it produces "a wide range of educationally important student, teacher, and classroom climate benefits." Wang et al. (2024), 36 studies, 137 effect sizes [T1]: in experimental/quasi-experimental designs, **autonomy g = 1.14, competence g = 0.48, intrinsic motivation g = 0.58 — and relatedness g = 0.44, p > .05, not significant.** Cheon, Reeve & Marsh randomized 80 classrooms / 2,227 secondary students and found the stance increased class-wide need satisfaction, supportive climate, and prosocial behavior while decreasing need frustration and antisocial behavior [T2 — PE context, Korea, self-reported outcomes, no achievement measure].

**Teacher expectations — cite the meta-analysis, never Pygmalion.** de Boer, Timmermans & van der Werf (2018), 19 interventions [T1]: "it was possible to raise teacher expectations and subsequent student achievement… summary effects of **Hedges' g = 0.38 and 0.30**, respectively." Intervention type did not affect effectiveness; **teacher support for the intervention did.** Jussim & Harber's (2005) review concludes self-fulfilling prophecies do occur but are typically small and may "dissipate rather than accumulate," with powerful effects more likely "among students from stigmatized social groups" [T1 review; abstract obtained via search result, not publisher page].

**Discourse norms — the honest tiering.** The one thing in this space with a randomized trial behind it is **dialogic teaching**: EEF's cluster RCT, 76 schools, 4,985 Year 5 pupils, ~35% EverFSM, 20-week intervention [T1]. Result: **+2 months English and science, +1 month maths**; **FSM pupils +2 months in all three**. And the caveat you must carry: a 2019 addendum found **no impact on KS2 results a year later**. Cost £52/pupil/year; most teachers said two terms wasn't long enough to embed it.

Everything else in this space is **T4** — professionally endorsed, not experimentally tested as a named package: NCTM's *Principles to Actions* eight Mathematics Teaching Practices; Stein/Smith's Five Practices (explicitly offered to make discussion *manageable*, not as an efficacy claim); Michaels/O'Connor/Resnick's Accountable Talk (the foundational paper is philosophical). The Chapin/O'Connor "Project Challenge" outcome figures that circulate widely could not be traced to any primary source this session — don't repeat them.

**Group work and status.** Roseth, Johnson & Johnson (2008), *Psychological Bulletin*: **148 independent studies, 8 decades, over 17,000 early adolescents** — higher achievement and more positive peer relationships under cooperative rather than competitive or individualistic goal structures [T1/T2; **effect sizes not retrieved**, publisher blocked]. Cohen & Lotan (1995), 13 elementary classrooms using a common curriculum [T3]: frequency of "status treatments" (assigning competence, multiple-ability orientation) was associated with **higher participation by low-status students with no effect on high-status students' participation** — the ceiling doesn't drop. Group work does not distribute participation on its own; the deliberate status move is what raises the floor.

On Boaler & Staples' Railside study [T3]: the general claim that heterogeneous grouping with status-conscious structure can work is supported by Cohen & Lotan and Roseth. The specific Railside *magnitude* claim is contested — Bishop, Clopton & Milgram (2012) pulled California state data and argue "there is only one year in the last five where any of these various measures for any cohort of students gives any advantage to the Railside students," allege a population mismatch between treatment and comparison samples, and judge the post-tests to be "roughly 3 years below the grade level where they were being administered." That critique ran in *Nonpartisan Education Review*, not a mainstream peer-reviewed venue, and Boaler has published a response alleging the critics breached confidentiality protections. Don't cite Railside magnitudes as settled either way.

**Behavior systems.** PBIS reduces exclusions and doesn't move much else: Gage et al. (2018) report **g = −.86 for school suspension and no treatment effect for office discipline referrals** [T1/T2]; Bradshaw et al.'s 37-school RCT found significant reductions in suspensions and ODRs [T1, effect sizes not retrieved]; but **Korpershoek's meta-analysis put SWPBS at g = 0.03, not significant, and lowest of any named program on social-emotional outcomes.** The Good Behavior Game has the most impressive long tail — the Baltimore RCT (19 schools, 42 first-grade classrooms, n = 1,084 and 1,117) showed no main effects at the 6-year follow-up but significant impacts at 14 years on lifetime alcohol abuse/dependence and antisocial behavior [T1] — with two caveats Blueprints states plainly: only one of ten reviewed studies meets their standards and it was conducted by the developer, and benefits concentrate in males on the high-aggression trajectory.

**Practices that are popular and weakly evidenced.** Positive Greetings at the Door: an RCT exists, and **WWC rated it "Does Not Meet Evidence Standards"**; the widely circulated "20 percentage points" and "12 minutes per hour" figures do not appear in the retrieved abstract. **2×10**: no peer-reviewed controlled trial found at all; the circulating "85% reduction in negative behaviors" traces to a book and an unpublished master's thesis with four students. Establish–Maintain–Restore has a matched randomized study with 4th/5th grade teachers showing improvements in observed academic engaged time and disruptive behavior, but the authors call it "preliminary," and the "33% / 75%" figures circulating online could not be verified against the article. **CLASS** as a validated lever: the one meta-analysis retrieved (35 studies, preschool/ECEC) found only two significant associations out of fourteen — Classroom Organization × Pencil Tapping pooled **r = .06** — with most studies at moderate-to-high risk of bias.

**The big null nobody cites.** The Social and Character Development Research Consortium (2010), IES/NCER with Mathematica and CDC [T1]: seven universal schoolwide social/character programs, each "coherent… based on a theory of action," schools randomized at 7 sites, one cohort followed 3rd through 5th grade, **84 schools**. Finding: "**there were no differences in students' social and emotional competence, behaviors, academic performance, or perceptions of school climate**" between program and control schools. Control schools were already doing such activities as standard practice, at lower levels. Any brief that says "SEL works" without citing SACD is not a defensible brief.

Against that, the SEL meta-analytic literature is genuinely large. Durlak et al. (2011): 213 programs, 270,034 students, an **11-percentile-point gain in achievement**, and "school teaching staff successfully conducted SEL programs" [T2]. Taylor et al. (2017): 82 interventions, 97,406 students, **mean age 11.09**, mean 41.1% low-SES, follow-ups from **6 months to 18 years**; benefits "similar regardless of students' race, socioeconomic background, or school location," and "postintervention social-emotional skill development was the strongest predictor of well-being at follow-up" [T2]. Cipriano et al. (2023): 424 studies, 53 countries, 575,361 students — significant improvements across skills, behavior, climate, and achievement, but with **significant heterogeneity in content, features, context, and implementation quality moderating outcomes** [T2].

**Reconciling those:** the meta-analyses evaluate *specific curricula run well*; SACD evaluated *schoolwide values programming as a bundle* against a counterfactual that was already doing some of it. Both are true. The lesson for you is that the value is in specific, well-implemented practices — not in adopting a framework.

### 5. What to optimize for, if you care about growth beyond your class

This is where the literature is unusually clear and unusually underused.

**Teacher effectiveness is at least two-dimensional, and the dimensions barely overlap.** Jackson (2018), *Journal of Political Economy* [T2 — quasi-experimental value-added with balance and selection tests, not randomized]: 573,963 North Carolina 9th graders, 872 schools, 5,195 English and 6,854 math teachers. He builds a **behavior index** from absences, suspensions, 9th-grade GPA, and on-time enrollment in 10th. Teacher effects on test scores and teacher effects on the behavior index correlate only **0.22** — "less than 5 percent of the variation in teacher effects on the behavior index is associated with teacher effects" on test scores.

**And the behavior dimension is the one that predicts the outcomes you care about.** In Jackson's models, 1 SD of teacher test-score VA raises graduation **+0.118 pp**; 1 SD of behavior VA raises it **+1.46 pp** — roughly 12:1. Adding behavior VA raises explained variance of teacher effects by **305% for graduation, 326% for dropout, 228% for SAT-taking, 182% for four-year-college intentions, 607% for high-school GPA**. His lifetime-earnings translation ($126,286 per year of students taught) grafts a correlational earnings gap onto a causal graduation effect, and he says so — don't use that number in a meeting.

**It replicates in a district that looks like yours.** Jackson, Porter, Easton, Blanchard & Kiguel (2020) [T2]: Chicago Public Schools, 133 high schools, 157,630 students, **86% economically disadvantaged**. Value-added computed on a short *student self-report survey* (interpersonal skills, school connectedness, academic engagement, grit, study habits) factoring into "social well-being" and "hard work." Per 1 SD of school VA on four-year college-going: test-score 1.83 pp, social 2.67 pp, hard-work 3.13 pp. **Socio-emotional VA raises explained variance by over 180% relative to test-score VA alone; adding test-score VA on top of SED VA adds only 18%.**

**In a randomized experiment, the test-score part is the part that fades.** Chetty et al. (2011), *QJE*, Project STAR — **11,571 Tennessee students randomly assigned to classrooms**, linked to tax records into adulthood [T1 for the experimental contrasts]. Students randomly assigned to higher-quality classrooms had higher earnings and college attendance. And the sentence: "**The effects of class quality fade out on test scores in later grades, but gains in noncognitive measures persist.**"

**Why fadeout happens, and what to do about it.** Bailey, Duncan, Odgers & Yu (2017) [T4 — a synthetic framework, not an effect estimate] identify three sustaining processes. The most useful for planning is the **"trifecta" test**: a skill worth investing in must be malleable, fundamental, **and would not have developed eventually without the intervention**. If your students would have picked it up by 8th grade anyway, expect the gain to fade. The second is **foot-in-the-door** — the right capacity at the right time to avoid an imminent risk or seize an emerging opportunity, which for you means getting a kid into the next math course rather than the remediation track. Note that Yeager's advanced-math-enrollment result is exactly this channel.

**The levers you personally control.** Blazar & Kraft (2017) [T2/T3]: "upper-elementary teachers have large effects on self-reported measures of students' self-efficacy in math, and happiness and behavior in class," and these are "predicted by teaching practices most proximal to these measures, including teachers' **emotional support and classroom organization**." Same paper: "teachers who are effective at improving test scores often are not equally effective at improving students' attitudes and behaviors." Kraft (2019), exploiting **random roster assignment** in the MET Project, found large teacher effects on complex open-ended math and reading tasks as well as on growth mindset, grit, and effort in class — with "weak relationships between these effects and performance measures used in current teacher evaluation systems." Gershenson (2016): teachers have "arguably causal, statistically significant effects on student absences that persist over time," and the teachers who improve test scores are not necessarily the ones who improve attendance.

**Two corrections to the popular story.** Grit: Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017), **584 effect sizes, 88 samples, 66,807 individuals** [T2] — "the higher order structure of grit is not confirmed," it's "only moderately correlated with performance and retention" and "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness," and "interventions designed to enhance grit may only have weak effects." The perseverance-of-effort facet holds up; consistency-of-interest doesn't. Use "perseverance of effort," not "grit."

Chetty, Friedman & Rockoff (2014)'s famous **$250,000 per classroom** figure is under live peer-reviewed dispute: Rothstein (2017, *AER*) replicated in North Carolina and found "the quasi-experiment is invalid, as teacher switching is correlated with changes in student preparedness," estimating bias "perhaps 10–35 percent as large, in variance terms, as teachers' causal effects," and concluding the long-run results "cannot support strong conclusions"; Chetty et al. rebutted in the same issue. If you use the number, use the caveat.

**Net.** Optimize for attendance, work completion, on-time progression, math self-efficacy, and connectedness. They're (a) causally within your reach through emotional support and classroom organization, (b) nearly uncorrelated with your test-score effect, so nobody else is covering them, and (c) the part of your effect that survives.

### 6. The mission architecture I'd defend

Four clauses, each traceable to a study, each stated as something *you* do:

**1. Confusion is information.** *"Being stuck is the signal you're in the right place. What do you know? Now try something."*
Evidence: error climate decays across grades 5–6 unless the room counteracts it [T3]; teacher-modeled self-questioning is Strong-rated in the grades 4–8 math guide [T1]; EEF metacognition +8 months, disadvantaged pupils less likely to use these strategies unless explicitly taught [T1].
Enactment: you narrate your own confusion aloud on a real problem, at least weekly. The three steps are a script students hear you use, not a poster.

**2. You will always know why this matters.**
Evidence: utility-value RCTs, effects concentrated in low-expectancy students [T2]; "task value" named as one of the two most effective strategies in Donker's meta-analysis [T1].
Enactment: every unit opens with a use, and at least once per unit students write about the connection themselves rather than being told.

**3. I hold the standard and I expect you to meet it.**
Evidence: teacher-expectation interventions g = 0.30 on achievement [T1]; wise feedback raised revision rates 87% vs. 62% among low-trust students and halted a two-year decline in trust [T2].
Enactment: the wise-feedback sentence attached to critical feedback, verbatim, every time. This is a one-sentence template, not a program.

**4. You get real choices inside a structured task.**
Evidence: autonomy support across 51 interventions including 38 RCTs [T1]; autonomy g = 1.14 in experimental designs [T1].
Enactment: choice of problem set difficulty, choice of representation, choice of who to work with — inside a task you designed and a standard you set. Autonomy support is not less structure; it's structure with rationale and voice.

What's deliberately absent: growth mindset language, grit, and belonging-as-a-claim. Keep belonging as a value you hold — teacher support is the single strongest predictor of it (r = .46) — but don't put it in the defensible-evidence column.

The instrumental case for writing this down at all: **consistency of enactment is the moderator that keeps recurring** — teacher support for the intervention rather than intervention type (de Boer); implementation fidelity rather than assignment (Responsive Classroom, Cipriano); PF design fidelity rather than PF as a label (Sinha & Kapur). A mission statement's defensible function is to make *you* consistent. Say that out loud when someone asks for the research.

### 7. Open questions and weak spots

- **The mission-statement question has no direct evidence and probably never will.** The Slate authors say experimental manipulation is "neither practical nor ethical." Everything above is inference from adjacent literatures.
- **Almost everything on classroom culture is correlational.** Error climate, teacher–student relationships, school belonging, classroom climate, instructional coherence — all measured, not assigned. The exceptions (dialogic teaching, autonomy support, GBG, PBIS, the reappraisal and belonging trials) are the ones I've leaned on hardest for that reason.
- **Populations don't always match.** The autonomy-support RCT with the cleanest design is Korean physical education with self-reported outcomes. The strongest belonging evidence is postsecondary. Korpershoek's classroom-management meta-analysis is **primary grades only**. Roseth's cooperative-learning meta-analysis is the right age band but I could not retrieve its effect sizes.
- **Outcome-measure inflation is a real threat across this whole brief.** Donker et al. found the overall strategy-instruction effect drops from g = .78 on researcher-written measures to .45 on independent ones. Math held up better than anything else (.61 → .84), which is genuinely reassuring, but discount headlines accordingly.
- **The growth-mindset publication-bias allegation is unresolved and I did not check it.** Macnamara & Burgoyne state that "a subsample of over 6,000 participants included in a preprint (Yeager et al., 2018) yielding a null result was removed from the published version (Yeager et al., 2019)." That's checkable against the preprint. Someone should check it before it gets repeated.
- **Items I could not retrieve and would want before a formal defense:** Borman et al. (2025) replication findings; Roseth et al. effect sizes; the Steuer et al. (2013) error-climate construct paper (the only open-access copy is an image scan with no text layer); Wang & Degol (2016) abstract; Bradshaw et al. (2010) effect sizes; the Abrami 2015 original article (I have its numbers only via a secondary research summary).
- **Marked [unverified] in this brief:** Mueller & Dweck (1998) details (retrieved only via Li & Bates's description); Rosenthal & Jacobson (1968) and all its critiques — which is why Pygmalion does not appear as a citation anywhere above; Raudenbush (1984) expectancy effect sizes; Lazowski & Hulleman (2016) d = 0.49; Reeve & Cheon (2021) journal venue; Cook et al. Positive Greetings and EMR percentage figures; Jubilee Centre and KIPP character-card figures. None of these carry a claim in this brief.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

This one maps to *routines and surfaces*, not to the CRA state spine, because it's about the operating stance rather than a day's mathematics. I did not load the state list this session, so nothing below asserts a state name.

**Warm-up (2 review + 3 current).** The format is already a diagnostic delivery mechanism; the addition here is a **think-aloud slot**. Once a week, you work the third current question aloud *before* students do — narrating being stuck, naming what you know, choosing something to try, and being wrong at least sometimes. That is the Strong-rated move (teacher models self-questioning, students then practice), and it is the only thing in this brief that directly counteracts the documented grade-5-to-6 decline in error tolerance.

**Projector surface.** The four mission clauses belong here as *live prompts tied to the current task*, not as static wall text. Specifically: a "why this matters" line rendered with each unit (utility value), and the self-questioning prompts visible during independent work (metacognition, applied to curriculum content).

**Control panel.** Two additions worth building, both cheap and both traceable to Jackson: a per-class **work-completion and attendance strip**, and a once-a-quarter three-item student self-report (connectedness, math self-efficacy, effort) modeled on the Chicago SED survey. These are the outcomes the evidence says carry the long-run signal, and they're the ones no state test reports back to you.

**Feedback templates.** Add the wise-feedback sentence as a reusable stamp wherever you return graded work: *I'm giving you these comments because I have high standards and I know you can meet them.* Verbatim, every time. One sentence, best-evidenced effect for the students least likely to trust the comment.

**First week of the year.** Run the adversity-reappraisal activity — a short reading plus a writing task establishing that middle-school difficulty is common, temporary, and not about them. It is the single best-evidenced move available for 6th graders specifically, and it is one class period, once.

**What does not belong in the OS.** A mindset module, a grit tracker, a character-strengths rubric, or a thinking-skills strand. All four are either unsupported or have failed in trials larger than anything you'd run.
