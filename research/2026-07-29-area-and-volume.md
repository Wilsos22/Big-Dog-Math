# Area and Volume — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Standard(s):** 6.G.A.1 (find area of triangles, special quadrilaterals, and polygons by composing into rectangles or decomposing into triangles and other shapes) · 6.G.A.2 (find volume of a right rectangular prism with fractional edge lengths by packing with unit fraction cubes; apply V = lwh and V = Bh) · 6.G.A.4 (represent 3-D figures using nets; use nets to find surface area)
**Compiled:** 2026-07-29
**Scope:** Covers area of triangles/parallelograms/composite polygons, volume of right rectangular prisms including fractional edge lengths, and surface area via nets. Deliberately left out: 6.G.A.3 (polygons in the coordinate plane), circles/circumference (7th grade), and volume of non-prism solids. The prerequisite analysis traces back to 3.MD.C.5–8, 4.MD.A.3, 5.MD.C.3–5, and 5.NF.B.4.
**Evidence at a glance:** 11 Tier 1 · 23 Tier 2 · 9 Tier 3 · 1 Tier 4 (with real response data) sources retrieved this session — 44 entries, a few of which bundle two closely related documents. **The evidence is strong on how students' understanding develops and what errors they make** — the spatial-structuring and misconception literature is deep, converges across four decades and multiple countries, and maps cleanly onto 6.G. **The evidence is weak on which instructional approach works best for this specific content.** There is no WWC-qualifying intervention study in geometry, no RCT on area/volume instruction in grades 5–8, and the CRA evidence base contains zero geometric-measurement studies. Section 6 deals with that honestly rather than papering over it.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*One page. Read this before you teach.*

### The short version

The formulas are not the content. The content is whether a student can *see structure* in a shape — rows and columns in a rectangle, layers of cubes in a prism — because a formula is nothing but a compressed record of that structure. Battista and Clements documented across two decades that many students never impose that structure on their own, and Huang & Wu found fifth graders using V = l×w×h correctly with no idea what it meant. So a right answer is not evidence of understanding here, and a unit built on formula practice will produce students who pass your quiz and cannot find the area of an L-shape.

The single thing most likely to go wrong: you hand out cubes and tiles, students count happily, and counting *replaces* structuring instead of leading to it. Outhred & Mitchelmore found exactly this — manipulatives can obscure the row-and-column structure and reinforce one-by-one counting over multiplicative thinking. The manipulative is not the intervention. The narration and the fade are.

### Where to spend the time

- **Spatial structuring — rows × columns, and layers of cubes.** Highest misconception density and the widest prerequisite fan-out; it's the thing that makes every formula in the unit meaningful, in both 2-D and 3-D. This is where the extra days go.
- **Base × perpendicular height, built by decomposing and rearranging.** 91% of 11–12 year olds in one study drew triangles only with horizontal bases; 39% always marked the height as a vertical line; 56% always put it inside the figure. Every one of those students will grab the slant side. Build the height concept, not the formula.
- **Fraction multiplication as the gate on 6.G.A.2.** A student can fully understand packing a prism with ½-inch cubes and still fail the standard on the arithmetic. This sub-skill double-dips into 6.NS — which *is* major work of the grade — so time spent here pays twice.

**What gets compressed: nets and surface area.** Surface area is genuinely just "add up the areas of faces you already know how to find." The *mental folding* skill is a separate spatial capacity that Wright & Smith found does not transfer between solids without explicit prompting, and Knight & Wright found success rates swinging from 100% to 26% based on which net you pick. Don't spend days building mental-folding fluency. Physically unfold real boxes, teach surface area as an application of 6.G.A.1, and move on. This is defensible partly because **6.G.A is a *supporting* cluster in grade 6, not major work** — confirmed in three independent standards documents.

### The sequence

1. **Structure the rectangle** — establish rows × columns as a *seen* structure, so that area is a multiplicative idea before it's a formula.
2. **Conserve area under rearrangement** — cut and rearrange, so students accept that moving pieces doesn't change the amount. Everything after this depends on it.
3. **Parallelogram by decompose-and-rearrange** — makes base × *perpendicular* height necessary rather than arbitrary, and kills the slant-side grab at the source.
4. **Triangle as half a parallelogram** — the ½ arrives as a consequence of a picture, which is what keeps it from being dropped under load.
5. **Composite polygons with no direct formula** — forces strategy choice; without these, students never have to decompose at all.
6. **Volume by layers** — "how many in one layer, how many layers" *before* V = lwh, so the formula compresses a structure students already see.
7. **Fractional edge lengths** — subdivide the unit cube. This is where the whole-number bias breaks and where the fraction arithmetic bites.
8. **Nets and surface area** — as an application of steps 1–5, not a new topic.

### The three misconceptions to plan for

- **Height = the slanted side** (triangle base 7, slant 8, height 6 → student writes 7×8÷2 = 28) → surface it with an **obtuse triangle drawn on a slanted base, height falling outside the figure**. A student running "height is vertical and inside" can't even locate a height, so the rule fails visibly instead of quietly producing a wrong number.
- **Counting only visible cubes** (4×5×2 prism → student sums the three visible faces, 8+20+10 = 38) → surface it with a **5×5×5 cube**. Visible-face method gives 75; correct is 125. The 50-cube gap is all hidden interior and it's impossible to argue with.
- **Volume computed as surface area** (asked for volume of a prism, student adds face areas: 2×9 + 4×12 = 66) → surface it with a **1×1×20 prism**, asking for both quantities side by side. Volume is 20 cm³; surface area is 82 cm². The magnitudes reverse from what students expect, so the swap can't hide.

### The progression at a glance

Not classic C→R→A. The stages below come from the spatial-structuring progression, which is what the evidence for *this topic* actually describes. The middle stage is the load-bearing one, and the fade trigger is a drawing, not a date.

| Stage | Students do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure with material** | Tile a rectangle, stack cubes into a prism, cut and rearrange a parallelogram. Always narrate: "how many in a row, how many rows" / "how many in a layer, how many layers." | The student **predicts the count before manipulating** and is right. Predicting, not counting, is the signal. |
| **Structure in a drawing** | Draw the covered rectangle **with lines, not individual squares**. Draw one layer of the prism and label how many layers. Mark the perpendicular height with a right-angle symbol. | The drawing shows **two perpendicular sets of lines** rather than individually drawn units. Outhred & Mitchelmore found that correct counts almost never occur without this — the drawing *is* the diagnostic. |
| **Formula as compression** | Write A = bh, A = ½bh, V = Bh, and say what each letter is doing in the picture. | The student can go **backwards**: given the area, find the missing height. |

**Return signal** (fade is not one-way): the student writes a formula and can't say which number is the height, or produces an answer whose magnitude is absurd. That's a drop back to drawing, not to material — the drawing stage catches most of it, and framing it as "let's see your picture" keeps it from reading as demotion.

### If they're two years behind

Four prerequisites actually block this unit, and each has a one-problem diagnostic you can run as warm-up review:

- **3.MD.C.7 — area is additive/decomposable.** *Find the area of this L-shape on grid paper.* Adding the outer edge lengths means area/perimeter confusion; counting only part of it means no decomposition.
- **5.MD.C.4/5 — volume by counting unit cubes.** *A drawing of a 5×5×5 cube: how many unit cubes?* An answer of 75 is the visible-face error; 25 means they collapsed to 2-D.
- **5.NF.B.4 — multiply fractions.** *What is ¾ × ⅔?* This is the **fluency bottleneck** that silently sinks 6.G.A.2. If fraction multiplication consumes working memory, the volume idea can't land — give a fraction-multiplication reference card during the geometry lesson so the load goes where you want it. That's a cognitive-load argument, not lowered expectations.
- **3.MD.D.8 — same perimeter, different area.** *Which has more area, a 1×20 rectangle or a 6×6 square?* Their perimeters are 42 and 24. The "bigger perimeter means bigger area" rule predicts wrong, loudly.

**Just-in-time, not re-teach-first.** Re-anchor the specific prerequisite in five minutes immediately before the lesson that needs it. The support for this is Tier 3 practitioner analytics — TNTP's data showed acceleration classrooms completing 27% more grade-level lessons than remediation classrooms (28% more in Title 1 schools), but that's a non-randomized natural experiment on one platform's usage data, not a trial. Weigh it as such.

### Watch out for

- **Manipulatives can make it worse.** Outhred & Mitchelmore found that materials sometimes obscure array structure and encourage counting over multiplicative thinking. Cubes without the layer narration are just a fun way to count.
- **Your quiz may not show the difference.** In the one quasi-experiment that isolated it, structure-first instruction beat formula-first on *conceptual understanding* (p < .01) but showed **no significant advantage on numerical calculation** (p = .08). If you only measure computation, you will conclude the structure work didn't matter. Put "explain / draw / find the missing dimension" items on the assessment or you're flying blind.
- **CRA has never been tested on this content.** The most current CRA meta-analysis contains zero geometry studies, and the IES practice guide's own evidence table marks "no qualifying studies" in the geometry column for all six of its recommendations. Section 6 explains what to do about that. Short version: keep the structure, change the justification, and gate the fade on what students draw rather than on the calendar.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. What the research supports

**Spatial structuring is the construct that matters, and it is not automatic.** Battista and Clements define spatial structuring as "establishing units, establishing relationships between units... and recognizing that a subset of the units, if repeated properly, can generate the whole set" [T2]. Their 1998 JRME study of students covering rectangles with unit squares documents a hierarchy running from disorganized one-by-one counting, through row-based counting where the student is unsure how many rows there are, to immediate use of rows × columns [T2]. The 1996 companion study on 3-D cube arrays finds the same thing in three dimensions: without structuring, students cannot build a coherent mental model of a prism and miscount hidden cubes [T2]. Battista's 2004 cognition-based assessment work frames these as an integrated set of levels across area *and* volume with identifiable cognitive plateaus [T2, abstract-level retrieval only].

The instructional consequence is blunt: **do not assume that showing a grid means students see rows and columns.** Ask "how many in a row, how many rows" as a taught move, not as a rhetorical question.

**Unitizing is one competency across length, area, and volume — not three.** Barrett, Cullen, Sarama, Clements, Klanderman, Miller & Rumsey ran a six-semester longitudinal teaching experiment spanning grades 2–5 and found that task sequences helping children integrate representations of "unit," group units into higher-order composite units, and recognize the arbitrary nature of the unit produced more fully developed unit concepts across all three measures [T2]. Reflection on the *multiplicative relations* among quantities was the active ingredient. So a 6th grader shaky on "what counts as a unit" in linear measurement carries that gap straight into volume, and ten minutes of unit-concept diagnostic before the unit is worth it.

**Structure-first instruction beats formula-first on understanding — but not on computation.** This is the closest thing to a controlled test on this exact content. Huang & Wu (2019) ran a two-phase quasi-experiment with 53 fifth graders in Taipei comparing a curriculum that explicitly connected the layer structure of 3-D cube arrays to the volume formula against one emphasizing formula and procedure. ANCOVA on the post-test total favored the structure curriculum, F(1,50) = 6.45, p < .01, partial η² = .11; on the conceptual-understanding subscale, F(1,50) = 6.90, p < .01, partial η² = .12. **On the numerical-calculation subscale the difference was not significant (p = .08)** [T2]. The same paper documents fifth graders using V = l×w×h with no understanding of what it meant — "rote formula" as a named failure mode among students producing correct answers.

Huang (2016) found a parallel result for area: with 131 fourth graders across three curriculum conditions taught by one instructor, an enriched curriculum integrating 2-D geometry knowledge outperformed curricula stressing only 2-D geometry or only numerical calculation on problems requiring mathematical judgment and explanation, and improved students' reasoning distinguishing perimeter from area [T2].

Two honest caveats: both are quasi-experiments outside the US, neither is large, and the null on computation in Huang & Wu is a real finding, not a rounding error. Report it to anyone who asks.

**Manipulatives help, modestly, and mostly with retention.** Carbonneau, Marley & Selig's meta-analysis (55 studies, N = 7,237, K–college) found a small-to-moderate overall effect favoring manipulatives over abstract-symbols-only instruction, with the effect **moderate-to-large on retention** (k = 53) but **small on problem solving** (k = 9), **transfer** (k = 13), and **justification** (k = 2). Effects were moderated by instructional and methodological characteristics [T1]. Peltier et al.'s meta-analysis of 53 single-case studies with at-risk and disabled students found an omnibus Tau-U of 0.91 (95% CI 0.87–0.95), and of nine moderators tested — including manipulative type, perceptual richness, and math concept — **only disability category was significant** [T1].

Read together: cubes and tiles are worth using and they generalize across topics, but they buy retention more than they buy transfer, and transfer is exactly what an open-ended composite-area task demands. Don't expect the material to do the abstraction for you.

**Materials can actively obscure structure.** This is the most counterintuitive finding in the retrieval and it deserves its own paragraph. Outhred & Mitchelmore (n = 115, grades 1–4, individual interviews, full text retrieved) documented a five-level progression in how students *draw* a covered rectangle: unorganized units → primitive covering → individually drawn but aligned units → some lines → two full perpendicular sets of lines. Numerically correct counts rarely occurred without the lines representation. They note explicitly that materials can obscure structure and encourage counting rather than multiplicative thinking [T2].

That gives you two things at once: a warning, and the best fade trigger available for this topic (see §6).

**Explicit instruction is the strongest general lever for students who are behind.** Gersten, Chard, Jayanthi, Baker, Morphy & Flojo's meta-analysis of 42 interventions for students with LD found significant mean effects for all instructional components except student feedback with goal setting and within-class peer-assisted learning, effects ranging 0.21–1.56; in regression, the two components with the largest and most robust boosts were **teaching students to use heuristics** and **explicit instruction** [T1]. The IES/WWC 2021 practice guide *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics* rates all six of its recommendations — systematic instruction, explicit mathematical language, concrete and semi-concrete representations, number lines, deliberate word-problem instruction, timed activities — at the **Strong** evidence level [T1].

But see §6 for the geometry-shaped hole in that last one.

**Spatial skill is trainable and transfers to math modestly.** Uttal et al.'s meta-analysis of 217 training studies found an average training effect of Hedges' g = 0.47, stable over delay and transferring to untrained spatial tasks, rising to g = 0.61 for children under 13 [T1]. Hawes, Gilligan-Lee & Mix's meta-analysis (29 controlled pre-post studies, N = 3,765, k = 89) found spatial training improved math performance at g = 0.28 (SE .07) and spatial thinking itself at g = 0.49, with effects moderated by age (larger for older children) and training duration [T1]. Gilligan, Thomas & Farran's training RCT with 8-year-olds found far transfer specifically to geometry-shape problems from both mental-rotation and spatial-scaling training [T1, retrieved via search summary].

Practical read: a short spatial warm-up is cheap and defensible, but the transfer effect is modest and duration-dependent. Don't sell it as the intervention.

### 2. Emphasis — where the leverage is

**First, the pacing fact that frames everything.** 6.G.A — "Solve real-world and mathematical problems involving area, surface area, and volume" — is a **Supporting cluster** in grade 6, not major work [T3, confirmed independently in three documents: Student Achievement Partners' *Where to Focus: Grade 6 Mathematics*, the Florida DOE grade 6–8 cluster designation table, and CPALMS' own standard page]. Grade 6's major work is 6.RP.A, 6.NS.A/C, and 6.EE. This is a standards-weighting argument, which the analysis framework rates as the weakest kind — but it's real, and it means a four-week isolated geometry unit is a pacing mistake. Achieve the Core's own framing gives you the move: "supporting work... can engage students in the major work of the grade." Fractional edge lengths in 6.G.A.2 are a fraction-multiplication vehicle. Use them that way.

**Highest leverage #1 — spatial structuring of arrays and layers.** *Prerequisite fan-out + misconception density.* Every formula in the unit is a compression of this structure, and both the area strand and the volume strand depend on it. The misconception evidence is dense and cross-national: the visible-cube-counting error was documented by Ben-Chaim, Lappan & Houang in 1985 and was still being observed by teachers in Turkish classrooms in 2018 [T3 for the 2018 observation; the 1985 categories were retrieved as quoted within it]. Battista's levels frame the plateaus [T2]. Huang & Wu's quasi-experiment is the direct instructional evidence that teaching the layer structure explicitly moves conceptual understanding [T2].

**Highest leverage #2 — base × perpendicular height via decomposition.** *Misconception density, with the hardest prevalence data in this brief.* Cutugno & Spagnolo's questionnaire study with 77 students aged 11–12 found 91% drew triangles with a horizontal base "as if it were a heavy body," 39% always marked the height as a strictly vertical line, and 56% always marked it as falling inside the triangle [T2, research-group technical report rather than a refereed journal]. Those three stereotypes all fail on an obtuse triangle. Craig Barton's aggregated diagnostic-question data (471 responses) shows 76% correct on a triangle-area item with each of three wrong options — slant side, no halving, both — taken by 8% of respondents [T4, practitioner site with real response data; use it for prevalence signal only, never for an effectiveness claim].

Huang's quasi-experiment supports the instructional response: geometry-integrated instruction outperformed calculation-only on judgment and explanation problems, and specifically improved perimeter/area discrimination [T2]. Lehmann's classroom design study with two Year 8 classes (n = 31) found students developing a *repertoire* of decomposition strategies used flexibly depending on the shape's features, with self-generated justifications [T2].

**Highest leverage #3 — fraction multiplication as the gate on 6.G.A.2.** *Prerequisite fan-out, and it double-dips into major work.* 5.NF.B.4 explicitly includes finding the area of a rectangle with fractional side lengths by tiling with fractional unit squares — that is the numerical prerequisite for 6.G.A.2, and it's easy to miss because it lives in the fractions domain rather than geometry. A student can hold the packing idea perfectly and still fail on the arithmetic. Zazkis (2025) studied *prospective teachers* justifying A = l×w for rational side lengths and found the formula survives the jump to fractions while the justification for it does not — adults either counted fractional units, invented a common unit, or expanded to clear denominators, and the first two quietly assumed the thing they were meant to justify [T2, population is adults, flagged].

**What gets compressed, and why that's defensible.** Nets and surface area. Wright & Smith (n = 34, Year 6, two interview rounds) found that successful net-folding schemes depend on strategic base selection, mental rotation rules, and anticipating how polygon composites form faces — and that **inferences generalizing a strategy to a new net were rare**, requiring explicit teacher support to transfer [T2]. Knight & Wright (n = 9 interviewed from a class of 23, Year 5) found success rates ranging from 100% on the prototypical cube T-net down to 26–33% on nets requiring coordination of an L-shaped three-square composite [T2]. Both find students reasoning by *imagined folding supported by gesture*, not by formal properties.

So the mental-folding skill is real, separate, poorly transferring, and expensive to build. It is also not what 6.G.A.4 is assessing. The standard is "use nets to find surface area" — additive combination of face areas you already know how to compute. Teach it that way: unfold real boxes physically, compute and sum face areas, and do not invest days in making students fluent at mentally folding unfamiliar nets. If you want the spatial work, it's better spent on 3-D array structuring, where it's load-bearing for volume.

**Also compressed:** area/perimeter comparison investigations. They matter (see the misconception in §4), but 3.MD.D.8 already names "rectangles with the same perimeter and different areas, or with the same area and different perimeters" as a *third grade* expectation. That makes it a warm-up diagnostic and an on-ramp for students who are behind, not a multi-day 6th grade investigation.

### 3. Sequence and why

1. **Structure the rectangle** — makes area multiplicative rather than a count. Nothing after this works without it (Battista et al. 1998; Outhred & Mitchelmore).
2. **Conserve area under decomposition and rearrangement** — makes cut-and-rearrange arguments *legitimate* in students' eyes. Funny's design research with third graders found students still divided on whether a cloth cut and re-sewn to fit a table was "the same size" as the table, some reverting to "the table is bigger because it's wider" — after physically doing the rearrangement themselves [T2]. Skipping this step means step 3 is a magic trick.
3. **Parallelogram by decompose-and-rearrange** — makes the *perpendicular* height necessary. When students physically cut the triangle off one end and slide it to the other, the height they used is visibly the one that made the rectangle work. The slant side does nothing.
4. **Triangle as half a parallelogram** — makes the ½ a consequence rather than a symbol. Barton's student explanations are instructive here: students verbalized the correct rectangle-decomposition reasoning and *still* wrote the wrong operation ("split the triangles into 2 so there is two right angled triangles... but these cancel out to make 6×7"), which suggests the image and the procedure hadn't been connected [T4].
5. **Composite polygons with no direct formula** — makes decomposition *necessary*. Ekawati et al.'s case studies of two seventh graders found both defaulted to formula-first and used cut-rotate-paste reasoning **only when no formula existed**; the low achiever couldn't generate an alternative strategy at all when formulas failed [T2]. If you only assign triangles and parallelograms, students never practice the reasoning the standard is actually about.
6. **Volume by layers before V = lwh** — makes the formula a compression of a seen structure (Huang & Wu; Battista & Clements 1996). CMP's own design rationale frames volume as "a stack of several layers of unit cubes" where the layer count is the base area, explicitly building area → volume [T3].
7. **Fractional edge lengths by subdividing the unit cube** — makes the whole-number bias break where you can see it, and forces the 5.NF.B.4 arithmetic into the open rather than letting it hide.
8. **Nets and surface area** — as an application of steps 1–5.

#### Where the research disagrees

**Structure-first vs. explicit-formula-first.** This is a live tension. On one side, Huang & Wu and Huang both found structure- and geometry-integrated instruction beating calculation-focused instruction on conceptual outcomes [T2, T2]. On the other, Gersten et al.'s meta-analysis found explicit instruction and heuristics to be the two most robust levers for students with LD [T1], and the IES practice guide rates systematic instruction at Strong [T1] — and a third of your room is the population those findings are about.

**What the disagreement hinges on:** the outcome measured. Huang & Wu's structure advantage appeared on conceptual understanding and *disappeared on numerical calculation* (p = .08). The explicit-instruction meta-analyses are dominated by computation and word-problem outcomes. These aren't contradicting each other; they're measuring different things.

**Recommendation:** structure-first, taught explicitly. That's not a dodge — it means the *content* of the explicit instruction is the structure ("here is how you see rows and columns; watch me do it; now you do it") rather than the procedure. Explicit teaching of a conceptual move is not the same as discovery learning, and Gersten's finding about heuristics points the same direction.

**What would flip me:** if a student population is failing computation outright rather than failing to explain, lead with the procedure and build the structure underneath it afterward. Huang & Wu give you permission — formula-first was not *worse* on calculation. If your 6-week data shows kids can explain and can't compute, invert the emphasis without guilt.

**A second, smaller disagreement: how much to lean on manipulatives.** Carbonneau et al. support them modestly [T1]; Outhred & Mitchelmore document them obscuring the very structure they're meant to reveal [T2]. The resolution is in §6: the material is scaffolding for the drawing, and the drawing is the target.

### 4. Misconceptions and how to prepare

**Misconception:** Height is the slanted side / height is vertical / height must be inside the figure
- **Looks like:** Triangle with base 7, perpendicular height 6, slanted third side 8 → student writes 7 × 8 ÷ 2 = 28. Or, given an obtuse triangle, the student draws a "height" that isn't perpendicular to the base, or refuses to mark one at all.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** Two previously-true rules collide. First, "use all the numbers you're given" is a reliable heuristic in elementary word problems. Second, every triangle they've ever been shown sat on a horizontal base with a vertical height inside it — so "vertical" and "height" fused into one idea before anyone told them otherwise.
- **Evidence:** Cutugno & Spagnolo, GRIM, University of Palermo — 77 students aged 11–12, open-question questionnaire: 91% drew triangles with a horizontal base, 39% always marked the height as a vertical line, 56% always marked it as inside the triangle [T2, research-group report]. Barton / diagnosticquestions.com, 471 responses: 8% chose the slant-side answer, with student explanations quoted verbatim [T4, prevalence only].
- **Surface it early with:** An obtuse triangle drawn on a *slanted* base, with the height's foot falling outside the base segment. There is no vertical-and-inside line to grab, so the broken rule announces itself instead of producing a plausible-looking wrong number. Include the slant length as given data so the "use every number" heuristic also gets tested.
- **When it shows up:** Confusion is step one — this is a *good* moment, don't rescue it. Ask "what do you know?" (they know area came from a rectangle) and "try something" (hand them the parallelogram cut-out and ask them to make the rectangle). The height is whichever length made the rectangle work. Do not re-explain the formula; the formula was never the problem.

**Misconception:** Volume = count of visible cubes only
- **Looks like:** A 4×5×2 prism drawn in isometric view → student counts the three visible faces and reports 8 + 20 + 10 = 38.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** A drawing shows only what it shows. Without a 3-D mental model built from actual layer structure, a picture of a prism is a 2-D arrangement of squares, and counting the squares you can see is a perfectly rational response to that object. Students also don't recognize that cubes on an edge belong to two faces at once.
- **Evidence:** Ben-Chaim, Lappan & Houang (1985, *Educational Studies in Mathematics*) categorized four error types — counting visible faces, doubling that, counting visible cubes, doubling that — quoted and reconfirmed in Tekin-Sitrava & Işıksal-Bostan's 2018 case study of four middle school teachers, which includes the 38-for-a-4×5×2-prism example from a real classroom [T3 for the 2018 study; the 1985 categories were retrieved as quoted within it, not from the original]. Battista & Clements (1996) attribute the error to inadequate spatial structuring and failure to recognize shared cubes [T2].
- **Surface it early with:** A drawn **5×5×5 cube**. Visible-face counting gives 75; the answer is 125. Fifty missing cubes is not a rounding error and cannot be talked around. Follow with the build: predict, then fill, then count.
- **When it shows up:** Don't correct the count. Ask them to build the thing and then ask what's inside. Prummer, Amador & Wallin's task extends this beautifully — build a box, then scale all three dimensions ×3 and predict how many of the small boxes fit inside. Most students predicted 3 or 9; the answer is 27 [T3, NCTM practitioner journal].

**Misconception:** Volume computed as surface area (and vice versa)
- **Looks like:** Asked for the volume of a prism with 3×3 and 3×4 faces, a student writes 2 × 9 + 4 × 12 = 66 and reports 66 as the volume. Running the other direction: asked for surface area, a student computes l × w × h.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** Both are taught close together, both "use all the face dimensions," and if the mental model of a prism is still an uncoordinated set of faces rather than a filled region, the two operations aren't distinguishable — they're both "do something with the numbers on the faces."
- **Evidence:** Tekin-Sitrava & Işıksal-Bostan (2018) quote the 66-as-volume example verbatim from classroom observation and name the misconception "the volume of a prism is the total area of its faces" [T3]. Chiphambo & Mtsi (2021, *EJMSTE*), diagnostic test with 18 Grade 8 students in South Africa: **44% used the volume formula when asked for total surface area**; 22% simply added the edge dimensions (cube edge 3 → "6 cm"); 17% combined both shapes' dimensions into one sum; 11% applied a plain 2-D area formula. The same study found 61% could not correctly identify a cube or rectangular prism from a drawing and 83% could not state the number of dimensions of a 3-D solid [T2, tiny n — treat as a vivid signal, not a population estimate]. Tan Sisman & Aksu (2016), 445 sixth graders in Ankara, 16 constructed-response tasks, documents "using the volume formula for surface area" and "believing a box has more than one surface area" [T2].
- **Surface it early with:** A **1×1×20 cm prism**, asking for volume and surface area side by side. Volume is 20 cm³; surface area is 82 cm². Students expect volume to be the "big" number and it isn't. The reversal makes the swap impossible to miss. Second move: ask "which one would you use to figure out how much wrapping paper, and which for how much sand?"
- **When it shows up:** Physical objects, immediately. Fill it (volume) vs. wrap it (surface area). Keep the two colour-coded in your notes all unit — students conflate them by default, not by exception.

**Misconception:** Area and perimeter covary — bigger perimeter means bigger area
- **Looks like:** Given two rectangles, the student compares perimeters and concludes the larger-perimeter one must have more area. Asked to find a counterexample, they generate confirming examples instead.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** For similar figures — which is most of what they've seen — everything does grow together. Nothing in their experience has yet forced the two quantities apart.
- **Evidence:** Widjaja & Vale (2021), *Journal on Mathematics Education* 12(3), 487–506: 82 students in Years 4–6 across three schools in Victoria, Australia, given the conjecture "a rectangle with a larger perimeter will always have a larger area." Students readily generated confirming examples and **struggled to construct counterexamples** [T2].
- **Surface it early with:** A **1×20 rectangle (perimeter 42, area 20)** next to a **6×6 square (perimeter 24, area 36)**. Ask for both quantities before comparing. The rule predicts the skinny one wins on area and it loses by 16 square units.
- **When it shows up:** Don't announce that the rule is wrong. Ask them to make a rectangle with perimeter 24 that has the *smallest possible* area, then the largest. Constructing the counterexample themselves is the whole point — Widjaja & Vale's finding is precisely that students can't do this unprompted, which makes it worth explicit practice rather than a one-line correction.

**Misconception:** Dropping the ½ in the triangle formula
- **Looks like:** Base 6, height 4 → 6 × 4 = 24.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** If the triangle-as-half-a-parallelogram image was never built, the ½ is a free-floating symbol with nothing holding it in place, and it drops under working-memory load.
- **Evidence:** Barton / diagnosticquestions.com, 471 responses: the forgot-to-halve distractor was chosen by 8%, with student explanations showing correct decomposition reasoning attached to the wrong operation [T4, prevalence only].
- **Surface it early with:** A triangle drawn *inside* the parallelogram it came from, base 8 and height 5. Un-halved gives 40, which is visibly the whole parallelogram sitting right there on the page. The picture does the arguing.
- **When it shows up:** "Show me on your drawing where the 40 is." That's a one-question fix if the image exists and a diagnosis that it doesn't if the question lands blank.

**Misconception:** Unit errors — area or surface area reported in linear units
- **Looks like:** Surface area of a cube with edge 3 cm reported as "6 cm" (3 + 3).
- **Why it makes sense to them:** "Total" cues addition from years of sum problems, and units are copied from the given data rather than derived from what's being measured.
- **Evidence:** Chiphambo & Mtsi (2021): 22% added the given edge dimensions, producing 6 cm for the cube and 14 cm for the prism [T2, n = 18]. Tan Sisman & Aksu (2016) found students writing "surface area equals length plus width plus height" among 445 sixth graders [T2].
- **Surface it early with:** Surface area of a cube with edge 4 cm. "Add the edges" gives 12 cm — a linear measurement, absurdly small for something you're covering. The correct 96 cm² is eight times larger and carries the right unit. NAEP has a field-tested version of this: grade 8 item asking for the best estimate of a classroom floor's area with options "700 feet / 700 square feet / 700 cubic feet / 700 yards / 700 square yards" [released item retrieved; **percent-correct data was not retrievable this session** and is not reported].
- **When it shows up:** "What are you measuring — a length, a covering, or a filling?" Make the unit a consequence of the question rather than a suffix.

**Misconception:** Conservation of area fails under rearrangement
- **Looks like:** After cutting a shape and rearranging the pieces, the student argues the new shape is a different size — "it's bigger because it's wider."
- **Why it makes sense to them:** Size is judged perceptually before it's judged quantitatively, and rearranging disrupts the perceptual cue even when nothing was added or removed.
- **Evidence:** Funny (2014), *IndoMS-JME* 5(1), 57–65: design research with third graders in Surabaya using a cloth-rag-to-tablecloth recomposition task, deliberately set before students learn a formal area formula. Even after physically cutting and re-sewing, students remained divided on whether the rearranged cloth and the table were the same size [T2]. The paper cites Kordaki (2003) and Kospentaris, Spyrou & Lappas (2011) for the broader finding that conservation of area is a prerequisite often skipped in instruction [as cited in Funny; not independently retrieved].
- **Surface it early with:** Two figures side by side — one genuinely rearranged (area unchanged) and one where a sliver was actually trimmed off. "Same area or not, and how do you know?" A perceptual answer can't distinguish them.
- **When it shows up:** This is prerequisite work, not a 6th grade misconception to schedule around. If it appears, you cannot proceed to the parallelogram cut-and-rearrange derivation with that student until it's resolved — the derivation is meaningless to someone who doesn't believe area is conserved.

**Misconception:** Volume, capacity, and "how big" are different unrelated things
- **Looks like:** The student insists volume and capacity are different but cannot say how, and may expect different units or tools for each on the same hollow box.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** Everyday English uses "capacity" for things you fill and "volume" for solid objects or vague bigness. Nothing has forced the unification.
- **Evidence:** Ho & McMaster (2019), MERGA: semi-structured video-recorded interviews with 11–12 year olds found "students believed that 'capacity' was different to 'volume' but were confused about the difference," while the phrase "interior volume" was easily understood as the space inside a hollow container [T2].
- **Surface it early with:** A solid block 10×6×5 cm and an open box with the same outer dimensions and 1 cm walls. Ask for the volume and the capacity of each. A student using the terms interchangeably gives one answer to all four.
- **When it shows up:** Name it as a vocabulary problem, which is what the research says it is. "Interior volume" is the phrase students already understand — use it.

**Misconception:** Whole-number bias with fractional edge lengths
- **Looks like:** Given a prism with edges ½, ½, and 1 in., the student predicts a volume larger than 1 in³ because "multiplying makes it bigger," or refuses to accept a prism with edges under 1 unit can be filled at all.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** "Multiplication makes bigger" was true for every number they used for six years.
- **Evidence:** **The whole-number-bias mechanism is well documented in fraction magnitude and arithmetic, but I could not retrieve a study applying it specifically to 6.G.A.2 fractional-edge-length volume tasks.** The nearest verified evidence is Zazkis (2025, *Educational Studies in Mathematics*), which found that prospective teachers justifying A = l×w for rational side lengths produced justifications that quietly assumed the thing being justified [T2, adult population]. `[unverified — the volume-specific framing is an inference from the general mechanism, not a retrieved finding]`
- **Surface it early with:** Predict-before-computing, twice in a row. First: 1½ × 1½ × 1 vs. a unit cube (correct: larger, 2.25 in³). Then: ½ × ½ × 1 vs. the same unit cube (correct: smaller, 0.25 in³). Predicting "bigger" both times exposes that the rule is attached to the operation's name rather than to the numbers.
- **When it shows up:** Go to the physical subdivision — how many ½-inch cubes fit in a 1-inch cube? Eight. So each one is ⅛ of a cubic inch. That's the concrete answer to "how can multiplying make it smaller," and it's the same move the standard is asking for.

### 5. Concrete activities

**Cut-and-rearrange parallelogram → rectangle** (~15–20 min). Students cut a parallelogram drawn on grid paper and rearrange it into a rectangle, or enclose it in a rectangle and subtract the corner triangles. *Materials:* grid paper, scissors, index card as a right-angle checker. *Embodies:* conservation of area under decomposition; base × perpendicular height as the pair that makes the rectangle work. *Cannot show:* doesn't generalize symbolically on its own, and doesn't handle a parallelogram with no horizontal side without rotating it first. *Source:* Illustrative Mathematics Grade 6 Unit 1 Lesson 4 teacher edition, whose own rationale states the investigation "lays a foundation for upcoming work with area of triangles and other polygons" [T3].

**Triangle as half a parallelogram** (~15 min). Two congruent copies of a triangle; rotate one 180° and join. *Materials:* grid or dot paper, scissors, tracing paper. *Embodies:* the ½ as a consequence of the picture. *Cannot show:* doesn't resolve height identification for an obtuse triangle where the height falls outside — that needs a deliberate follow-up example [T3].

**Comparison set of parallelograms** (~15–20 min). Three parallelograms designed so each rewards a different strategy: one clean decompose-and-rearrange, one that resists it and rewards enclose-and-subtract, one off-grid with a distractor measurement. *Materials:* printed shape cards. *Embodies:* strategy selection instead of formula plug-in; deliberately blocks the grade-3 default of counting squares. *Cannot show:* nothing curved or composite [T3, IM design]. The research backing for this activity *type* is Ekawati et al.'s finding that students only reason by decomposition when no formula is available [T2].

**Fill-a-box, predict before counting** (~30–45 min with the net; ~15 with a pre-made box). Build or borrow a small box, **predict the cube count, then fill and verify.** Extend to the scaled version: build a box, then one with all three dimensions ×3, and predict how many small boxes fit inside before testing. *Materials:* centimeter cubes if the school owns them; **free substitutes:** sugar cubes, dice, folded-paper unit cubes; cereal-box cardboard for the box. *Embodies:* volume as stacked layers where the layer count is the base area — CMP's explicit design rationale [T3]. *Cannot show:* doesn't reach fractional edge lengths without a follow-up. *Source:* Prummer, Amador & Wallin (2016), *Mathematics Teaching in the Middle School* 21(8), 472–479 — documents that most students predicted 3 or 9 rather than 27 on the scaled-box task [T3].

**Unfold a real cereal box into its net** (~20–25 min). Open a box flat, trace and label each face, find each area, sum. *Materials:* real boxes (free — have families save them), scissors, ruler. *Embodies:* net as the 2-D record of a solid's faces; surface area as additive combination of 6.G.A.1 skills. *Cannot show:* real boxes have glue flaps that break the clean six-rectangle idealization — name them and set them aside explicitly; doesn't generalize to non-rectangular faces without a second example. *Caution from the research:* Knight & Wright found difficulty varies enormously by net shape, and Wright & Smith found students rarely transfer a folding strategy to a new solid unprompted — so ask "how is this like the one you just folded?" out loud rather than assuming transfer [T2, T2].

**Critique a flawed net** (~15 min). Students receive a net with a deliberate error — mismatched triangle faces, a doubled rectangle — and must find and fix it before computing surface area. *Materials:* printed net, grid paper. *Embodies:* verification as a habit rather than execution. *Cannot show:* doesn't build the physical fold/unfold skill; pair with the cereal-box activity [T3, IM family materials].

**Fixed-perimeter / fixed-area investigation** (~20–30 min, or a recurring warm-up). Using a fixed length of string or a fixed count of grid squares, build every whole-number rectangle and compare. *Materials:* string or yarn cut to length, grid paper; **free substitutes:** toothpicks or pretzel sticks on grid paper. *Embodies:* separating "surrounding" from "covering" — literally CMP's naming logic for *Covering and Surrounding* [T3], and the direct counter to the Widjaja & Vale covariation misconception [T2]. *Cannot show:* doesn't extend past rectangles. *Note:* this is already a 3.MD.D.8 expectation, which makes it a strong accessible on-ramp for students who are behind.

**Estimate-then-measure an irregular shape** (~10–15 min, good as a recurring warm-up). Trace a shoe, hand, or leaf on grid paper; estimate the area before counting squares. *Materials:* grid paper, any object. *Embodies:* estimation-first — the NSW Department of Education measurement guidance states that "if students predict before they measure, they will learn to judge the relative size of the quantity and the units," and notes that estimating area and volume is harder than estimating length [T3]. *Cannot show:* curved regions can only be approximated by grid counting, not measured exactly — keep this as a routine, not a substitute for exact decomposition.

**Draw-the-array diagnostic** (~5 min, use repeatedly). Show a rectangle partially covered by unit squares and ask students to complete the drawing. *Materials:* paper. *Embodies:* nothing new — it's a diagnostic, and it's the single cheapest one in this brief. *What it reveals:* students who draw individual squares one at a time have not structured the array, even when the final count is right; students who draw two perpendicular sets of lines have [T2, Outhred & Mitchelmore].

### 6. The progression to abstract

**Start with the honest finding: CRA has never been tested on this content.**

Three independent, current sources converge:

- Ebner, MacDonald, Grekov & Aspiranti's 2025 meta-analysis of CRA (30 single-case studies, 116 students, PK–college) reports an overall Tau-BC of 0.9965 (95% CI [0.9947, 0.9983]) — but **every study in its coded table addresses addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, fractions, place value, change making, rate of change, or quadratic expressions. Not one addresses area, volume, perimeter, or geometry** [T1, study table read in full].
- Bouck, Satsangi & Park's 2018 evidence-based-practice synthesis states in its own abstract that CRA earned evidence-based status "relative to computational problems, such as addition, subtraction, and multiplication, largely with regrouping." Effect sizes across included studies ranged 0.08–4.78 with no omnibus. Their search located geometry studies and screened them out of the final evidence base [T1/T2].
- The IES/WWC 2021 practice guide *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics* rates all six recommendations Strong — **but its outcome-domain table marks "X" (no studies meeting WWC standards) in the Geometry column for all six, including Recommendation 3, the concrete-and-semi-concrete-representations one.** Rational Numbers and Whole Numbers columns are mostly positive [T1, evidence table read in full].

What *has* been tested on geometric measurement specifically: two small single-case studies. Cass, Cates, Smith & Jackson (2003), three students in grades 7–10 with LD, geoboard instruction on area and perimeter, multiple-baseline — students who couldn't solve the problems at baseline gained accuracy [T2, n = 3, retrieved via search summary]. Hord & Xin (2015), sixth graders with mild intellectual disability, concrete–semiconcrete–abstract plus model-based problem solving on area and volume, multiple-probe design — students solved a high percentage of grade-6-level problems on criterion tests, with the authors noting that "success with more complex problems will be necessary for proficiency at grade level" [T2, very small n, abstract-level retrieval]. Neither made it into the field's own synthesized evidence base.

**So what should replace it? Structurally, almost the same thing — but justified from the right literature and gated on the right trigger.**

The progression the evidence for *this topic* actually describes is the spatial-structuring progression (Battista & Clements; Outhred & Mitchelmore; Barrett et al.; Huang & Wu), and it happens to run material → drawing → symbol. The difference from generic CRA is what each stage is *for*:

- **The material's job is not to be concrete. It's to make layer and row structure available for narration.** Cubes with no "how many in a layer, how many layers" talk are just counting practice. Huang & Wu's advantage came from the curriculum that *connected* the layer structure to the formula, not from the cubes [T2].
- **The drawing's job is to strip the structure out of the material.** This is where the abstraction actually happens, and it's the stage most likely to get skipped as decorative.
- **The symbol's job is compression.** The check that it worked is reversibility: given the area, find the missing height.

**What students draw, precisely.** For area: the covered rectangle drawn with **two perpendicular sets of lines**, not individually drawn squares. For the parallelogram: the rectangle it becomes, with the height marked by a right-angle symbol. For volume: **one layer, drawn as a structured array, plus a label for how many layers** — not a picture of a whole prism.

**The fade trigger.** Outhred & Mitchelmore gives you the best one available on this topic, and it's observable in ten seconds: **a student who draws individual units one at a time has not structured the array; a student who draws lines has.** They found numerically correct counts rarely occurred without the lines representation [T2]. Pair it with a prediction check — the student **predicts the count before manipulating and is right** — and you have a trigger that is about the student's thinking rather than about the calendar.

**The fade schedule.** Ebner et al. found non-integrated CRA — mastery-gated, sequential phase-out — slightly outperforming integrated/faster fading (b = 0.011, p = .045) [T1]. That's a tiny effect and I wouldn't build a unit on it, but it points the same direction as the trigger above: fade on an observed criterion, not on a date.

**How strong is the case for fading at all?** Weaker than usually claimed, and worth knowing. Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas (2015) is the flagship study isolating a faded condition against both concrete-only and abstract-only, and it found fading beat both, with concrete→abstract beating the reverse — **but the topic was math equivalence with balance-scale problems in 2nd/3rd grade, not geometry, and I could not retrieve the effect size this session** [T1, retrieved via search summary]. Fyfe et al.'s 2014 systematic review is an argument piece proposing four theoretical mechanisms, not a pooled meta-analysis [T2]. Carbonneau et al. measures manipulatives against symbols-only; it does not isolate fading as a variable [T1].

Net: the evidence supports **using concrete representations** (strong, general, not geometry-specific) and **gating their withdrawal on mastery** (weak, indirect). It does not establish that fading is the active ingredient for area and volume. Say that plainly if anyone asks.

**The one place the "manipulative that never leaves" warning has real teeth for this topic:** Outhred & Mitchelmore's finding that materials can obscure structure and reinforce counting over multiplicative thinking [T2]. That's not a generic caution about dependence — it's a specific, documented mechanism by which tiles make area *worse*. Combined with Carbonneau's finding that manipulatives buy retention far more than transfer, the practical rule is: material for a day, drawing for the week, and the drawing is what you grade.

**The return signal.** A student writes a formula and can't say which number is the height, or produces an answer whose magnitude is absurd (surface area smaller than one face; volume smaller than the base area). Drop to the drawing stage, not to the material — the drawing catches most of it, and "show me your picture" is a lower-status move for a 6th grader than "go get the cubes."

**Can one teacher run this in one period with a mixed-readiness room?** Yes, and that's not a compromise. The whole approach uses one task for the whole class with the *representation* varying by student rather than the task — the below-grade-level student is still cutting the same parallelogram, just spending longer at the material stage while a classmate is already at the drawing. What it costs you is that the material stage takes more class time than a formula-first unit would, and 6.G.A is supporting work. That's the real trade-off: buy the structure by spending less on nets, not by spending more total days.

### 7. Scaffolding and differentiation

**Prerequisite chain with diagnostic items.** All prerequisite standards below are verbatim from the CCSS text.

| Prerequisite | Diagnostic item | What the wrong answers tell you |
|---|---|---|
| **3.MD.C.5–7** — area as unit squares; area of a rectangle by multiplying side lengths; area is additive and decomposable into non-overlapping rectangles | Find the area of an L-shape drawn on grid paper (e.g. a 6×4 rectangle with a 2×2 notch removed) | Adds the boundary lengths → area/perimeter confusion. Counts only part → hasn't decomposed. Counts one by one and gets it right → **conceptual gap in structuring**, which is the one that can't be scaffolded around |
| **3.MD.D.8** — same perimeter/different area | 1×20 rectangle vs. 6×6 square: which has more area? | Chooses the 1×20 → the covariation misconception; use the fixed-perimeter investigation as an on-ramp |
| **4.MD.A.3** — apply area and perimeter formulas for rectangles | Rectangle, area 24, one side 6 — find the other side | Can't reverse it → procedural gap; responds well to worked-example pairs |
| **5.MD.C.3–5** — unit cube; volume by counting unit cubes; V = lwh; additive volume | Drawing of a 5×5×5 cube: how many unit cubes? | 75 → visible-face counting. 25 → collapsed to 2-D. 125 without hesitation → move them on |
| **5.NF.B.4** — multiply fractions, **including area of a rectangle with fractional side lengths tiled with fractional unit squares** | ¾ × ⅔ = ? | Wrong or very slow → **fluency bottleneck** on 6.G.A.2. This one is easy to miss because it lives in the fractions domain, not geometry |

**Gap types get different responses.**

- **Conceptual gap** — spatial structuring was never built. This requires the material stage and cannot be scaffolded around. It is also the most common gap in this unit and the reason a formula-first approach fails these students specifically: they can execute V = lwh and cannot tell you what they computed.
- **Procedural gap** — the structure is there but identifying the perpendicular height is slow and error-prone. Responds to worked-example pairs and to marked right-angle symbols on every figure for a week.
- **Fluency bottleneck** — fraction multiplication consumes working memory, so the volume idea can't land on top of it. **Give the tool.** A fraction-multiplication reference card, or letting the student use the area model for the sub-step, puts the cognitive load on the new idea. This is a load argument, and it's worth framing that way explicitly for anyone reviewing the plan — it's not lowering the bar on 6.G.A.2, it's refusing to assess 5.NF.B.4 while pretending to assess geometry. If the fraction gap is broad across the class, sequence whole-number volume to mastery *first* and bring fractional edges in only after.

**Acceleration over remediation, with the evidence labeled honestly.** Re-anchor the specific prerequisite for five minutes immediately before the lesson that needs it, rather than running a two-week 5th grade review before anyone touches 6th grade content. TNTP's *Accelerate, Don't Remediate* analysis of >2M students across >100,000 Zearn classrooms found acceleration classrooms completing 27% more grade-level lessons, 28% more in Title 1 schools and 49% more in majority-students-of-color schools, with remediation classrooms showing *more* struggle on grade-level content [T3 — explicitly a non-randomized natural experiment on one platform's usage data with teacher self-selection into approach; useful, not dispositive]. *The Opportunity Myth* found students met grade-level standards on assignments only 17% of the time despite mostly A/B grades, with ≥50%-grade-level-work classrooms gaining 7 more months of learning [T3].

**Differentiation without three lessons.** Everything in §5 is one task with a varying floor and ceiling:

- **Number choice within one problem set.** The same L-shape task with whole-number dimensions for some students and fractional dimensions for others. Same structure, different arithmetic load.
- **The grid is the floor.** A student who can't decompose can count squares and get there. A student who can decompose does it in two moves. Same task, same answer, no separate worksheet.
- **Worked-example pairs** for students who need a model before attempting — Gersten's meta-analysis puts explicit instruction and heuristics as the two strongest levers for this population [T1], and a worked example beside a blank parallel problem is the cheapest version of that.
- **Strategic partnering** at the material stage, so the narration ("how many in a layer, how many layers") gets said out loud by a student rather than only by you.

**Extension that deepens rather than accelerates.**

- **Reversibility.** "The area is 24 and the base is 6. What could the height be? Is there more than one answer?" Then: "The triangle's area is 24. What are three different base-height pairs that work?"
- **Generalization.** "You found the area of that trapezoid by decomposing. Will your method work on *every* trapezoid? Show me one where it doesn't."
- **Error-finding.** The flawed-net critique task, and its area analogue — a worked solution with a slant side used as the height, to be found and diagnosed. Explaining *why* someone else's error is an error is harder than avoiding it.
- **Do not** send them into 7th grade circles. Same idea, more depth.

### 8. Open questions and weak spots

**The biggest hole: there is no strong instructional-effectiveness evidence for this content.** No RCT on area/volume instruction in grades 5–8 turned up. No WWC-qualifying study in geometry exists — the practice guide's own table says so. The two quasi-experiments I'm leaning on (Huang & Wu, n = 53; Huang, n = 131) are small, non-US, and one has a null on computation. Everything in §1–§3 that reads as an instructional recommendation is built on (a) strong developmental/misconception evidence about *what students do*, plus (b) general instructional principles that were not tested on this content. That's a real limitation and it's the honest answer if someone asks "what's the effect size for this approach?"

**6.G.A.2's fractional edge lengths are the least-studied piece of the standard.** I could not retrieve any peer-reviewed empirical study of 6th graders learning volume with fractional edge lengths by subdividing unit cubes. The nearest verified work is Zazkis (2025) on prospective teachers and rational-side-length area justification, which is an adult population. The whole-number-bias framing for fractional-edge volume in §4 is marked `[unverified]` and is my inference from the general whole-number-bias mechanism, not a retrieved finding.

**NAEP and TIMSS percent-correct data could not be verified.** I retrieved genuine released NAEP grade 8 measurement and geometry items (irregular area on a grid; recognize a unit of volume; the linear-vs-square-vs-cubic-unit classroom-floor item; identify the net that folds into a box) from a state DOE compilation, but the percent-correct figures live in the NAEP Questions Tool, which didn't render. **No NAEP or TIMSS percentages appear in this brief.** The items themselves are usable as diagnostics regardless.

**Several prevalence figures come from very small samples.** Chiphambo & Mtsi's striking numbers (44% used the volume formula for surface area; 22% added edge lengths) come from n = 18 in South Africa. Treat them as vivid illustrations of the error's existence, not as estimates of how many of your students will make it. Tan Sisman & Aksu's n = 445 is the strongest sample in the misconception set, but its full text is paywalled and only the abstract-level misconception list was verified — a secondary summary attributed specific percentages to it that I could not confirm and have not reported.

**The Barton diagnostic-question data is Tier 4.** 471 responses is a real number from a real platform, but it's an unrefereed practitioner site with a self-selecting respondent pool. It's in here because the student explanations it quotes verbatim are unusually illuminating about the reasoning behind the ÷2 omission. It carries no effectiveness claim.

**Ben-Chaim, Lappan & Houang (1985) was retrieved as quoted within a 2018 paper, not from the original.** The four error categories are quoted directly and the finding is corroborated by Battista & Clements, so I'm comfortable with it, but the citation is second-hand.

**What I'd want and couldn't find:** a controlled comparison of a structure-first vs. formula-first area/volume unit in a US middle school with a demographically similar population, measuring both conceptual and computational outcomes at delay. That study would resolve most of §3's disagreement. It does not appear to exist.

## Appendix — Classroom OS handoff

Mapped to the warm-up format (2 review + 3 current, with the misconception the third current question targets named explicitly). The day-by-day mapping onto the 11-state CRA spine isn't in here — that needs `classroom-os-context` loaded and a pacing decision from you; say the word and I'll build it.

Every "review" slot below is a prerequisite diagnostic doing double duty. That's the cheapest possible screening you have.

| Day | 2 review (= prerequisite diagnostic) | 3 current | Misconception the 3rd targets |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Structure the rectangle** | (1) Area of an L-shape on grid paper [3.MD.C.7 — decomposition]. (2) Which has more area, 1×20 or 6×6? [3.MD.D.8] | Complete a partial array; find area of a 7×9 rectangle two ways; **a rectangle where only two adjacent side lengths are labeled and the grid is removed** | Counting-instead-of-structuring: with no squares to count, the student must use the row × column relationship |
| **2 — Conserve, then parallelogram** | (1) Same L-shape, different decomposition — same answer? (2) Rectangle area 24, one side 6 — find the other [4.MD.A.3, reversibility] | Cut-and-rearrange a parallelogram; find area of a parallelogram on grid; **a parallelogram with base 6, height 4, and slant side 5 all labeled** | Slant-side grab — the extra number is bait, and "use every number given" fails |
| **3 — Triangle** | (1) Parallelogram area with an unlabeled slant side. (2) ¾ × ⅔ [5.NF.B.4 — early warning for Day 6] | Triangle inside its parallelogram; triangle on grid; **an obtuse triangle on a slanted base with the height falling outside** | Height is vertical / height is inside — the rule can't even be applied here |
| **4 — Composite polygons** | (1) Triangle area, base and height only. (2) Same L-shape, now with a fractional dimension | Trapezoid two ways; irregular polygon; **a composite shape with no applicable formula at all** | Formula-dependence — Ekawati's finding that decomposition only appears when no formula exists |
| **5 — Volume by layers** | (1) Area of a 6×4 rectangle. (2) Drawing of a 5×5×5 cube: how many unit cubes? | Predict-then-fill a box; one layer × number of layers; **a prism drawn in isometric view with hidden cubes** | Visible-cube counting — the 5×5×5 review question already primed it; now make them own it |
| **6 — Fractional edges** | (1) Volume of a 4×3×2 prism. (2) ½ × ½ [the bottleneck, now load-bearing] | How many ½-inch cubes fill a 1-inch cube?; volume of a 1½ × 1 × 2 prism; **predict whether a ½ × ½ × 1 prism has more or less volume than a unit cube, before computing** | Whole-number bias — "multiplying makes bigger" produces a visibly wrong prediction |
| **7 — Nets and surface area** | (1) Area of a 3×5 rectangle. (2) Area of a triangle, base 4, height 6 | Unfold a real box and sum the face areas; a net with a deliberate error to find; **the same prism, asked for volume and surface area side by side (use 1×1×20)** | Volume/surface-area conflation — the magnitude reversal makes the swap impossible to hide |

**One note on Day 3's second review question.** Putting ¾ × ⅔ in the warm-up three days before you need it is deliberate. If a chunk of the class misses it, you have three days to decide whether to give the reference card, reorder so whole-number volume gets fully mastered first, or run a five-minute re-anchor on Day 6. That's the just-in-time move with enough lead time to actually be just in time.
