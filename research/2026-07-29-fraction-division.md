# Dividing Fractions and Mixed Numbers — Research Brief

**Grade band:** 6th grade, Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind
**Standard(s):** CCSS 6.NS.A.1 — "Interpret and compute quotients of fractions, and solve word problems involving division of fractions by fractions, e.g., by using visual fraction models and equations to represent the problem." Prerequisites: 5.NF.B.7 (unit fraction ÷ whole number, whole number ÷ unit fraction), 5.NF.B.3 (a/b = a ÷ b), 5.NF.B.4 (fraction multiplication), 5.NF.A.1 (unlike denominators, including mixed numbers), 4.NF (equivalence and comparison), 3.NF (fraction as a number).
**Compiled:** 2026-07-29

**Scope:** Covers the four cases inside 6.NS.A.1 — fraction ÷ whole number, whole number ÷ fraction, fraction ÷ fraction, and division involving mixed numbers — plus the measurement/partitive distinction underneath them and mixed number ↔ improper fraction conversion as a sub-routine. Deliberately left out: decimal division (6.NS.B.3), ratio and unit-rate reasoning as its own topic (6.RP), and negative rational numbers (7.NS), except where they bear on sequencing.

**Evidence at a glance:** 22 Tier 1 · 21 Tier 2 · 7 Tier 3 · 2 Tier 4 sources retrieved. The evidence is unusually strong for a middle-grades topic in three places — that fraction knowledge predicts later algebra, that explicit instruction works for struggling learners, and that fraction division is the single worst-performed arithmetic operation with documented, countable error patterns. It is genuinely thin in two places: **mixed number ↔ improper fraction conversion has almost no dedicated research base** (a targeted ERIC search returned one practitioner book), and **concreteness fading is less settled than the CRA slogan implies** — its own systematic review calls the supporting evidence "admittedly indirect." Both are handled honestly below rather than papered over.

## Part 1 — Action Summary

*One page. Read this before you teach.*

### The short version

Fraction division is the hardest arithmetic operation students meet, and it is not close. In a study of 120 sixth and eighth graders in low-income Pittsburgh-area districts, division accuracy was **20%** overall — versus 60% for addition and 48% for multiplication — and the low-achieving sixth graders got **6–12%** correct (Siegler & Pyke, 2013). The single most important instructional decision is this: **build "how many ___ fit inside ___?" as the meaning of division before you teach any procedure, and teach students to predict whether the answer will be bigger or smaller than the dividend before they compute.** The thing most likely to go wrong is starting with keep-change-flip. When 34 preservice teachers were handed a fraction-division word problem, 68% reached for invert-and-multiply and **only 26% of those got it right** (Sahin et al., 2020). A procedure with no meaning underneath it degrades into a coin flip about which fraction to invert.

### Where to spend the time

- **Fraction magnitude on a number line, including mixed numbers.** Elementary fraction and division knowledge uniquely predicts algebra achievement five to six years later, controlling for IQ, working memory, and family income (Siegler et al., 2012, n=3,677 UK + 599 US). In the strongest fraction RCT for at-risk students, improvement in measurement/magnitude understanding *statistically mediated* the whole intervention effect (Fuchs et al., 2013).
- **The measurement meaning of division ("how many groups of size b are in a?").** This is the interpretation that keeps working when the divisor is less than 1 — and it is the one that does *not* develop on its own. Only 25% of 92 teachers could correctly represent quotitive division, vs. 51% for partitive (Roche & Clarke, 2009). Fifth graders inventing their own strategies produced the common-denominator method, never invert-and-multiply (Sharp & Adams, 2002).
- **Predicting the size of the quotient before computing.** Preservice teachers judged the *direction* of a fraction-division answer with a divisor below 1 correctly only **29% of the time — below chance** — while executing the arithmetic correctly (Siegler & Lortie-Forgues, 2015). Procedural fluency does not produce this intuition. It has to be taught separately, and it is your students' only built-in error detector.

**What gets compressed to pay for it:** mixed number ↔ improper fraction conversion is taught as a two-minute procedural sub-routine with a magnitude sanity check attached, not as a conceptual mini-unit — the documented error is a slip, not a broken model. Fraction ÷ whole number lives in warm-up review (it's 5.NF.B.7a, not new). Formally deriving (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = ad/bc gets one lesson at the end, not the spine of the unit.

### The sequence

1. **What division means, with whole numbers** — establish both meanings ("how many groups?" and "how much in each group?") before any fraction divisor appears, so the fraction case has something to extend.
2. **Will the answer be bigger or smaller?** — estimation-only day; installs the error detector before there is anything to get wrong.
3. **How many unit fractions fit in a whole number?** — concrete stage; the easiest true case, and 5.NF.B.7b, so it doubles as a diagnostic.
4. **How many non-unit fractions fit?** — the diagram now has to be read carefully; introduces the leftover-piece problem.
5. **Same denominators** — students discover you can just divide the numerators; this is the bridge that makes the general case feel reachable.
6. **The algorithm(s), explicitly taught** — common-denominator method and invert-and-multiply, compared side by side against the diagrams that produced them.
7. **"How much is in one group?"** — the partitive meaning, which 6.NS.A.1's own examples require and which feeds 6.RP unit rates.
8. **Mixed numbers** — conversion as a sub-routine, immediately applied to division, with a magnitude check every time.
9. **Remainders and what they're a fraction *of*** — the referent-unit trap, isolated on purpose.
10. **Which operation is this?** — sorting word problems; the misconception from Day 2 comes back to be killed.
11. **Application** — area, volume, and rate contexts.
12. **Assessment + reversibility extension.**

### The three misconceptions to plan for

| The wrong answer you will actually see | The task that surfaces it in week one |
|---|---|
| Student says 5 ÷ ⅔ must be less than 5 (or picks multiplication for a word problem where the divisor is under 1) | Day 2, no computing allowed: "Is 5 ÷ ⅔ bigger or smaller than 5? Bet on it before you solve." |
| ⅔ ÷ ⅙ = 2/18 (multiplied straight across), or flipped the *first* fraction | Give ⅔ ÷ ⅙ right after they've drawn it. The diagram says 4. The straight-across answer says 1/9. The gap is the lesson. |
| Correct answer, wrong unit: "8 servings with 1 leftover" instead of 8⅓ servings | 5 glasses of juice, ⅓-glass servings. Ask only: "the leftover piece — is it ⅓ of a *glass* or ⅓ of a *serving*?" |

### Concrete → abstract at a glance

| Stage | Students do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| **Concrete** (2–3 days, not more) | Fold and cut paper fraction strips; lay pattern blocks (or paper hexagon/trapezoid/rhombus/triangle cutouts) inside each other to answer "how many ½s in 4?"; cut string into ¾-foot lengths | Student can **predict the count before manipulating** and is right most of the time. Not "after two days." |
| **Representational** | Draw a tape diagram: bar for the dividend, tick marks at the divisor's size, count the groups, label the leftover *in units of the divisor*. Then write the matching equation next to the drawing, every time. | Student draws the diagram **and** writes the equation without being told, and the two agree. |
| **Abstract** | Symbolic division, both methods available; estimate first, compute, check against the estimate | Student computes fluently *and* catches their own reversed-fraction errors via the estimate. |

**Return signal:** any answer that is off by a reciprocal (student gets 1/9 where 4 was right, or 6 where 2/3 was right). That's a "draw it" signal, not a re-explain-the-rule signal. Frame dropping back a stage as *checking your work with a picture* — sixth graders will use a checking tool long after they've stopped using a crutch.

### If they're two years behind

Three prerequisites actually block this unit, and each has a one-question diagnostic you can run as warm-up review: **(1) Fraction as a magnitude** — "Place ¾, 5/4, and 1½ on a number line from 0 to 2." If 5/4 lands past 1½ or 1½ lands between 1 and 5/4, the student is reading numerals, not sizes; this is a conceptual gap and it needs the concrete stage. **(2) Measurement division with whole numbers** — "How many groups of 4 are in 20?" If they can only produce "20 shared among 4," the schema fraction division extends doesn't exist yet; five minutes of whole-number measurement division immediately before Day 3 fixes it (Sidney, Thompson & Opfer, 2019, report that reminding students of whole-number division improves fraction division understanding — with better transfer when the analogy is *not* over-mapped). **(3) Fraction multiplication** — "½ × ⅓ = ?" Needed for the algorithm, not for the meaning, so it can be scaffolded around for two weeks. **Just-in-time move:** hand out a multiplication chart on Day 6 without ceremony. Fuchs et al. (2013) found intervention effects were moderated by working memory, attentive behavior, and processing speed but **not** by whole-number calculation skill — a fact-fluency bottleneck is a working-memory problem, and removing it is a load argument, not lowered expectations.

### Watch out for

- **Your textbook probably under-supplies division practice.** Lortie-Forgues, Tian & Siegler (2015) counted 250 fraction-multiplication problems vs. **54** fraction-division problems in one U.S. grade 5–6 series, and 122 vs. 56 in another; a Korean series had 239 vs. **440**. Plan to supplement, and spiral division into warm-ups long after the unit ends.
- **Area models break on fraction ÷ fraction.** In-service teachers working ¾ ÷ ⅔ with an area model argued the leftover was 1/9 (counting pieces of the original whole) when it is 1/8 (pieces of the *divisor*) — Lamberg & Wiest (2015). Use tape diagrams and number lines as the primary representation; keep area models for the multiplication connection.
- **Manipulatives don't teach the abstraction by themselves.** The dual-representation line of work (Uttal, Scudder & DeLoache, 1997) shows children's use of an object as a symbol collapses to chance when the explicit orienting instruction is removed. The teacher narration linking block to symbol is the active ingredient, not the block.
- **Skip the motivational add-on.** Fuchs et al. (2021) randomized at-risk students to fraction intervention with vs. without embedded self-regulation and growth-mindset instruction. The add-on produced **no significant difference**. Spend the minutes on content.

## Part 2 — The Evidence

### 1. What the research supports

**Fraction division is where students break, and it is measurable.** Siegler & Pyke (2013) [T1] gave 16 fraction arithmetic problems to 120 students (60 sixth graders, 60 eighth graders) from three low-income public school districts near Pittsburgh, split into low-achieving (bottom 35% on the state test) and higher-achieving groups. Overall accuracy: addition 60%, subtraction 68%, multiplication 48%, **division 20%**. Broken out, low-achieving students were **12%** correct on equal-denominator division and **6%** on unequal-denominator division; higher-achieving students were 36% and 27%. From sixth to eighth grade the higher-achieving group gained 36–40 percentage points on division; the low-achieving group gained **11**. This is a widening-gap finding in a population that resembles Steele's, and it is the empirical reason this unit deserves more days than the pacing guide gives it.

**The gap is not a fluency problem — it's an operation-meaning problem.** The dominant error was importing a procedure from a different fraction operation: **55% of division answers** involved this (Siegler & Pyke, 2013) [T1]. Independently treating numerator and denominator as separate whole numbers accounted for another 12–21% of division trials. Students were also *least* confident on division (mean 3.1/5 vs. 4.2/5 for addition) — they can feel the shakiness, which is a usable opening in a classroom that treats confusion as step one.

**Fraction knowledge is the highest-leverage content in the K–8 sequence, and there is longitudinal evidence for that claim specifically.** Siegler et al. (2012) [T1], *Psychological Science*, used two nationally representative longitudinal datasets — the British Cohort Study (n=3,677, tested at age 10 and 16) and the U.S. Panel Study of Income Dynamics–Child Development Supplement (n=599, ages 10–12 then 15–17). Their finding, verbatim: "Elementary school students' knowledge of fractions and division uniquely predict those students' knowledge of algebra and overall mathematics achievement in high school, five or six years later, even after statistically controlling for other types of mathematical knowledge, general intellectual ability, working memory, and family income and education." In the UK data, a one-SD increase in early fractions knowledge was associated with a .15 SD increase in later algebra knowledge and .16 SD in total math achievement (p<.001). The paper explicitly tested fractions-and-division against addition/subtraction/multiplication as competing predictors, and fractions-and-division won in both datasets. **This is the strongest single argument available for spending extra days here**, and it is the one to hand a department chair.

**Explicit, systematic instruction is the best-evidenced delivery mode for the below-grade-level half of the room.** The IES/WWC practice guide *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: RtI for Elementary and Middle Schools* (2009) [T1] rates exactly two recommendations **Strong**: "Instruction during the intervention should be explicit and systematic" — which the guide specifies as "providing models of proficient problem solving, verbalization of thought processes, guided practice, corrective feedback, and frequent cumulative review" — and schema-based word-problem instruction. Its recommendation on visual representations is rated **Moderate**. Independently, Gersten et al. (2009) [T1], a meta-analysis of 42 RCTs and quasi-experiments on math instruction for students with learning disabilities, found "all instructional components except for student feedback with goal-setting and peer-assisted learning within a class resulted in significant mean effects ranging from 0.21 to 1.56," and that "two instructional components provided practically and statistically important increases in effect size — teaching students to use heuristics and explicit instruction." Two independent evidence streams converge. This is not the fashionable answer, but it is the defensible one.

**The number line, not the pie chart, is the representation with RCT support for this population.** Fuchs et al. (2013) [T1], *Journal of Educational Psychology*: 259 at-risk fourth graders randomized within classroom (129 intervention, 130 control), stratified by risk severity, with 292 low-risk classmates as a benchmark. Intervention was 12 weeks, 3×/week, 30 minutes, 3:1 small group, centered on the **measurement interpretation** — number-line placement, comparing and ordering, the inversion property, density — with procedural instruction deliberately delayed until lesson 22 of 36 and then integrated. Control was standard classroom instruction emphasizing part-whole/area models, with number lines "given considerably less emphasis." Verbatim: "On each conceptual and procedural fraction outcome, effects favored intervention over control (effect sizes 0.29 to 2.50), and the gap between at-risk and low-risk students narrowed for the intervention group but not the control group. **Improvement in the accuracy of children's measurement interpretation of fractions mediated intervention effects.**" That mediation result is the load-bearing part: the number-line work wasn't decoration, it was the mechanism.

Two replications in the right grade band. Barbieri et al. (2020) [T1], *Journal of Educational Psychology*: **51 sixth graders** struggling with fraction concepts, randomized to a 6-week/27-lesson number-line-plus-cognitive-strategies intervention (n=28) or business-as-usual school intervention (n=23). Results: fraction concepts g=1.09, number line estimation g=−.85 (percent absolute error), magnitude comparisons g=.82, and "these improvements held at delayed posttest 7 weeks later." An exploratory interaction suggested the intervention **buffered the usual negative effect of low attentive behavior** on learning — directly relevant to a Title 1 room. Jayanthi et al. (2021) [T1], *Exceptional Children*: 205 fifth graders with math difficulties, RCT, systematic explicit instruction with linear representations (Cuisenaire rods, number lines) and required mathematical explanation; g=0.66–0.78 on proficiency, 0.80–1.08 on number line estimation, 1.07 on procedures, 0.68–1.23 on explanation tasks.

**The IES fractions guide backs the conceptual-before-procedural move, at Moderate strength — and says so about itself.** The WWC practice guide *Developing Effective Fractions Instruction for Kindergarten Through 8th Grade* (2010, panel chaired by Siegler) [T1] rates Recommendation 2 (fractions are numbers; use number lines as a central representational tool) **Moderate**, Recommendation 3 (help students understand *why* computation procedures make sense) **Moderate**, and Recommendations 1, 4, and 5 **Minimal**. Its own division example is a measurement one: cut two equal ribbons, "have students find out how many fourths of a ribbon fit onto one-half of a ribbon," then "show students how to solve the problem computationally. ½ ÷ ¼ = ½ × 4/1 = 4 ÷ 2 = 2." The guide is candid that the number-line recommendation extrapolates: five qualifying studies examined number lines with whole numbers in preschool–grade 2, and "none of the studies examined using number lines in fractions instruction... the panel believes that given the clear applicability of number lines to fractions and whole numbers, these findings indicate that number lines can improve fractions learning." Cite the guide for the *practice*; cite Fuchs and Barbieri for the *fraction-specific evidence*.

**Comparing two solution methods beats drilling one.** The Rittle-Johnson & Star line of classroom experiments [T2 — retrieved via search summary and a fetched book chapter, not full-text of the primary trials] finds that comparing multiple solution methods for the *same* problem produces the strongest gains in procedural flexibility while supporting both conceptual and procedural knowledge, that general math achievement does not moderate the benefit, but that **prior knowledge of one of the compared strategies does**, and that slowing the pace helps novices learn from comparison. Practical read: the common-denominator vs. invert-and-multiply comparison on Day 6 is evidence-supported — but only *after* students own one of them, and taken slowly.

**Incorrect worked examples help, with a scaffolding condition attached.** Booth, Lange, Koedinger & Newton (2013) [T1], *Learning and Instruction*, two in-vivo experiments in Algebra I Cognitive Tutor: "explaining worked examples during guided practice leads to improved conceptual understanding compared with guided practice alone... incorrect examples, either alone or in combination with correct examples, may be especially beneficial for fostering conceptual understanding." Mechanism, verbatim: explaining an incorrect example can "draw students' attention to the particular features in a problem that make the procedure inappropriate." The caveat is in the same paper: Große & Renkl (2007) found relatively novice learners could **not** benefit from incorrect examples when required to locate the error themselves. So: show the error, name it, and ask *why* it's wrong — don't hand a struggling sixth grader a page of unlabeled error-hunting.

### 2. Emphasis — where the leverage is

**(a) Fraction magnitude, including mixed numbers — downstream predictive evidence.** This is the strongest form of emphasis argument available and Siegler et al. (2012) [T1] supplies it directly: fractions and division at age 10 uniquely predict algebra at 16 across two national datasets with heavy controls. It is reinforced by mechanism evidence — magnitude improvement mediated the Fuchs et al. (2013) [T1] effect, and magnitude was the primary target in both Barbieri et al. (2020) [T1] and Jayanthi et al. (2021) [T1], the two closest-matched studies to Steele's room. Budget: magnitude/number-line work is not a pre-unit warm-up week, it is a thread that runs through every day, including inside the mixed-number lesson.

**(b) The measurement interpretation of division — misconception density plus prerequisite fan-out.** Three independent lines converge. First, the partitive/sharing model is the default and it *fails* below 1: Fischbein, Deri, Nello & Marino (1985) [T1] established that primitive models "remain attached to arithmetic operations and influence tacitly the choice of an operation even after the learner has had solid formal-algorithmic training," and reported that Italian ninth graders identified the correct operation for a fabric-price problem **40%** of the time when the multiplier was 0.65 versus **98%** on an otherwise identical whole-number version (figures verified inside the fetched Lortie-Forgues et al., 2015 review). Roche & Clarke (2009) [T2] found 92 teachers were roughly twice as likely to correctly represent partitive division (51%) as quotitive (25%), and only 25% used the quotitive model successfully on 8 ÷ 0.5. Second, measurement thinking is what the CCSS Progressions use to reach the algorithm: "they might use the measurement interpretation of division to see that 8/3 ÷ 2/3 = 4, because 4 is how many 2/3's there are in 8/3. At the same time they can see that this latter statement also says that 4 × 2/3 = 8/3" [T2, official progressions document]. Third, it does not arise on its own — Sharp & Adams (2002) [T2] found fifth graders inventing strategies produced the common-denominator method and pictorial/repeated-subtraction methods, and **none** spontaneously invented invert-and-multiply. Fan-out: measurement division is also the reasoning that carries into 6.RP unit rates, 7.RP proportional relationships, and dimensional analysis.

**(c) Predicting the direction and size of the quotient — misconception density, and it's cheap.** Siegler & Lortie-Forgues (2015) [T1] gave 41 academically above-average preservice teachers 16 "direction of effects" true/false items. Percent correct, from the table reproduced in Lortie-Forgues et al. (2015):

| Operation | Fractions below 1 | Fractions above 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | 92 | 92 |
| Subtraction | 89 | 92 |
| Multiplication | **33** | 79 |
| Division | **29** | 77 |

29% is below the 50% you'd get by guessing — a *systematically inverted* intuition, in adults who compute correctly, and the same paper reported sixth and eighth graders performing similarly poorly. Math and science majors at a selective university scored 100% on the parallel multiplication items, so the skill is learnable; it just isn't a byproduct of procedural practice. This costs five minutes a day as a warm-up routine and returns an error detector students carry into every future rational-number topic.

**What gets less time, and why that's defensible.**

- **Mixed number ↔ improper fraction conversion: compress to a sub-routine.** A targeted ERIC search for research on mixed-number/improper-fraction misconceptions returned **zero peer-reviewed results**; a broader query returned one practitioner book (Spangler, 2011) [T3]. The one place it appears in retrieved research is as a named *procedural* error category — "incorrectly changing the mixed number to an improper fraction" — in Sahin et al. (2020) [T2], alongside other execution slips. That's the signature of a procedural gap, not a missing model. Response: model it explicitly, drill it in warm-ups, and staple a magnitude check to it ("2¾ became 11/4 — is 11/4 a little under 3? Yes. Good."). Do not build a conceptual unit around it, and do **not** let students spend a class period converting without dividing anything.
- **Fraction ÷ whole number: fold into review.** It's 5.NF.B.7a. Use it as warm-up diagnostic material, not new instruction.
- **The general formula derivation: one lesson, at the end.** The Progressions [T2] show a ratio-reasoning path that "leads us directly to the invert-and-multiply rule," and it's worth doing once for the students who want to know why — but 6.NS.A.1 asks students to *interpret and compute* quotients and to use the multiplication-division relationship to explain, not to prove the general identity.

### 3. Sequence and why

**Day 1 — What does division mean?** Whole numbers only. Both meanings, named: "how many groups?" (24 bagels into bags of 3) and "how much in each group?" (24 bagels into 3 bags). *Makes possible:* everything after this is an *extension* of a schema students already have rather than a new topic. Sidney, Thompson & Opfer (2019) [T2] report that "children's conceptual understanding of fraction division is improved by instruction that draws on children's more familiar, whole number division concepts," with a nuance worth respecting: Sidney & Alibali found that *reminding* students of whole-number division without explicitly mapping the analogy produced "fewer misconceptions and better transfer to novel problems" than explicit mapping. Reactivate the schema; don't build an elaborate side-by-side chart.

**Day 2 — Bigger or smaller?** No computing. Estimation and betting. *Makes possible:* the error detector exists before there are errors to detect. This is where the Siegler & Lortie-Forgues 29% finding cashes out.

**Day 3 — How many unit fractions fit in a whole number?** Concrete: pattern blocks or paper fraction strips. "How many ½s in 4? How many 1/6s in 1½?" *Makes possible:* a physically verifiable answer that contradicts "division makes smaller" on day one of fraction division. This is also 5.NF.B.7b, so students two years behind are on familiar ground while doing grade-level work.

**Day 4 — Non-unit fraction divisors.** ⅔ ÷ ⅙, then 5 ÷ ⅔ (which has a remainder). *Makes possible:* the leftover-piece question, which is the referent-unit misconception's front door.

**Day 5 — Same denominators.** Illustrative Mathematics' "Dan's Division Strategy" [T3]: Dan claims 6/10 ÷ 2/10 = 6 ÷ 2; does it generalize? *Makes possible:* the common-denominator algorithm, arrived at rather than announced — and, per Sharp & Adams (2002) [T2], this is the method students actually invent.

**Day 6 — The algorithm(s), explicitly taught and compared.** Model, verbalize, guided practice, corrective feedback (WWC RtI Recommendation 1, Strong [T1]). Present common-denominator and invert-and-multiply against the diagrams that produced them. Include one incorrect worked example with the error named and explained, not hunted (Booth et al., 2013 [T1]; Große & Renkl caveat). Gregg & Gregg (2007) [T3] argue the two algorithms map naturally onto the two interpretations — measurement to one, fair-sharing to the other — which is a clean way to organize the comparison.

**Day 7 — "How much in one group?"** The partitive case. *Makes possible:* the standard's own examples ("How much chocolate will each person get if 3 people share ½ lb?") and the bridge to 6.RP. Note a caution: Wahyu et al. (2020) [T2], a three-cycle design study with 44 students, found that understanding of non-unit-rate partitive problems "did not support the students' understanding of unit rate problems" and that students "were incapable of determining symbolic representations from unit rate problems and linking the problems to fraction division problems." Build that symbolic bridge explicitly; don't assume it.

**Day 8 — Mixed numbers.** Convert, divide, check the magnitude. Every problem gets an estimate first.

**Day 9 — Remainders and referent units.** Isolated on purpose. See §4.

**Day 10 — Which operation is this?** Sorting word problems, half of which have divisors under 1. This is where Fischbein's finding gets confronted directly. The Pennsylvania SAS "Fractions and Ribbons" lesson [T4] has students build a "When to Multiply / When to Divide" chart themselves from sorted problem cards — good structure, weak source; use the structure.

**Day 11 — Application.** Area with fractional side lengths, volume, rate. **Day 12 — Assessment + reversibility extension.**

#### Where the research disagrees

**How long do you withhold the algorithm?** Two defensible positions, both with real backing.

*Position A — delay it substantially.* Open Up Resources / Illustrative Mathematics Grade 6 Unit 4 [T3] runs seventeen lessons and does not reach the algorithm until **Lesson 10**: three lessons on making sense of division, six on the two meanings with diagrams, then the algorithm. Eureka Grade 6 Module 2 [T3] similarly separates fraction÷whole-number and whole-number÷fraction into distinct visual-model lessons before combining. Fuchs et al. (2013) [T1] delayed procedural instruction to lesson **22 of 36** — and got effect sizes from 0.29 to 2.50 with at-risk students. The strongest RCT in this space used the longest delay.

*Position B — don't delay much.* The WWC RtI guide's Strong-rated recommendation is explicit and systematic instruction with modeling and guided practice (2009) [T1], and Gersten et al. (2009) [T1] found explicit instruction to be one of two components with practically important effect-size increases for students with LD. The procedural/conceptual literature increasingly treats the two as iterative rather than ordered. And a nine-day delay in a Title 1 room is nine days during which students who need a foothold don't have one.

**What the disagreement hinges on:** *dosage and grouping.* Fuchs's 36-lesson delay happened in 3:1 small groups, 30 minutes, three times a week, over twelve weeks — a structure that can absorb a long conceptual runway because every student is being monitored continuously. A 55-minute whole-class period with 28 students and a two-year spread cannot; a student who is lost on Day 4 of a nine-day conceptual arc has no procedural handhold and disengages.

**Recommendation:** meaning first, but the *symbolic equation* appears alongside the diagram from Day 3 — every diagram gets an equation written next to it — and the algorithm is explicitly taught on Day 6 of a 12-day arc. That's meaning-before-procedure without a nine-day gap. **What would flip me:** if the Day 0 diagnostic shows most of the class solid on fraction magnitude and 5.NF.B.7, the longer inquiry arc is affordable and probably produces better transfer — run the OUR sequence closer to as written. If the diagnostic shows the magnitude gap is broad, tighten further: algorithm on Day 5, and move the saved day into number-line work.

**Which meaning leads?** Measurement, per §2(b). The honest counterweight: the Progressions [T2] present both interpretations **side by side on the same problem**, and 6.NS.A.1's own example set includes a partitive item. So measurement leads, but partitive is a full lesson (Day 7), not a footnote.

### 4. Misconceptions and how to prepare

**Misconception: "Division makes smaller."**
- **Looks like:** Student estimates 5 ÷ ⅔ < 5. Or, on a word problem — "A serving is ⅔ cup, you have 5 cups, how many servings?" — the student multiplies, because dividing "would make it smaller and that doesn't make sense."
- **Why it makes sense to them:** For every division problem they saw from grade 3 to grade 5, it was true. The rule wasn't wrong; its domain just ended. That's the renegotiation — not "you were wrong," but "that rule had a boundary and we just crossed it."
- **Evidence:** Fischbein et al. (1985) [T1] — implicit models drive operation choice even after formal training; 40% vs. 98% correct operation identification depending on whether the multiplier was 0.65 or a whole number. Siegler & Lortie-Forgues (2015) [T1] — 29% correct on direction of effects for division below 1, below chance. Van Hoof, Verschaffel & Van Dooren (2015) [T1], n=1,343 students grades 4–12 — natural number bias on operations tasks weakens with grade but "no disappearance."
- **Surface it early with:** Day 2 estimation-only. Best number choice: **5 ÷ ⅔ next to 5 × ⅔.** Same numbers, opposite behavior, no arithmetic required. Then Day 3's pattern-block answer ("how many ½s in 4?" = 8) makes the contradiction physical.
- **When it shows up:** Don't correct. Ask "how many halves are in one whole?" then "so how many are in four?" Then: "so what happened to the number when we divided?" Let them say it. The naming — "dividing by a number smaller than 1 makes the answer *bigger*, because you're asking how many small things fit in a big thing" — should come out of a student's mouth.

**Misconception: Cross-operation import (the reciprocal roulette).**
- **Looks like:** ⅔ ÷ ⅙ = 2/18 (multiplied straight across). Or ⅔ ÷ ⅙ = (3/2)(1/6) = 3/12 (inverted the *dividend*). Or, after correctly flipping, adding instead of multiplying.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** Four fraction operations, four superficially similar symbol-manipulation rules, learned as rules. Nothing in the symbols says which one applies, so retrieval is a guess weighted by recency.
- **Evidence:** Siegler & Pyke (2013) [T1] — **55% of division answers** involved importing components from other fraction arithmetic procedures; "inverting the wrong fraction" is explicitly named. Sahin et al. (2020) [T2], n=34 preservice teachers, documents the taxonomy verbatim: "multiplying across without 'flipping' the second fraction, incorrectly changing the mixed number to an improper fraction, cross multiplying after flipping the second fraction, adding after flipping the second fraction, and neglecting to properly interpret the remainder." Of the 68% who used invert-and-multiply on the pretest, only 26% got the right answer. Ristiana et al. (2021) [T2], n=80 prospective teachers: "53.75% of prospective teachers use the keep-change-flip algorithm without understanding the concept of fraction division, and just 15% of prospective teachers understand fraction division."
- **Surface it early with:** Give ⅔ ÷ ⅙ *immediately after* the class has physically found the answer is 4. Then let them run the procedure. Anyone who gets 1/9 has a visible, undeniable contradiction sitting next to a diagram they built themselves. This is the single highest-value number choice in the unit — a divisor that's a unit fraction with a large denominator makes the straight-across answer absurdly small.
- **When it shows up:** "Your answer is 1/9. Your picture says 4. One of them is lying — which, and how do you know?" The estimate from Day 2 is the arbiter. Re-teaching the steps here wastes the moment; the student's problem is that they have no way to *tell* which rule is right, and that's what needs building.

**Misconception: Referent-unit confusion in the remainder.**
- **Looks like:** 5 ÷ ⅗ — student answers "8 with 1 left over" or "8 and ⅕," rather than 8⅓. Or on ¾ ÷ ⅔, calls the leftover 1/9 instead of 1/8.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** They're reporting the leftover as a fraction of the *original whole*, which is what a fraction has meant to them since third grade. In quotitive division the answer's unit silently switches to *the divisor*, and nothing on the page announces that.
- **Evidence:** Lamberg & Wiest (2015) [T2], 12 in-service teachers, grades 3–9: teachers argued the leftover in ¾ ÷ ⅔ was 1/9 (blocks of the original whole) rather than 1/8 (relative to the divisor's partition); the breakthrough came from treating "the divisor [as] the referent unit for the dividend." Sahin et al. (2020) [T2] documents "leaving the answer as 6 with 6 leftover" as a distinct error level. The IES/REL Midwest fractions toolkit preview (Griffin & Ibama-Johnson, 2024) [T2] works exactly this example — 5 glasses of juice, ⅗-glass servings, 8⅓ servings — as its tape-diagram exemplar.
- **Surface it early with:** Day 4, one question only, no computation: "You cut 5 feet of ribbon into ¾-foot pieces. There's a leftover piece. Is it ¾ of a foot? Is it a whole piece? What fraction of a *piece* is it?"
- **When it shows up:** Point at the drawing. "Show me the leftover. Now show me one whole piece. How much of that piece is the leftover?" Physical, not verbal. The tape diagram makes this answerable; the bare algorithm does not.

**Misconception: Independent whole numbers (whole-number bias).**
- **Looks like:** ⅓ + ¼ = 2/7. On the classic estimation item, 12/13 + 7/8 ≈ **19**. In division, treating numerator and denominator as separate division problems.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** A fraction *looks* like two whole numbers with a line. Every prior operation acted on whole numbers component-wise.
- **Evidence:** The 1978 NAEP item (Carpenter et al., 1980) [T1], n>20,000 eighth graders, asked which of 1, 2, 19, or 21 is closest to 12/13 + 7/8: **24% chose 2**; the most common answer was 19. Lortie-Forgues, Tian & Siegler (2015) [T1] re-administered it in 2014 to 48 eighth-grade algebra students at an affluent suburban school: **27% correct** — "essentially unchanged" after 36 years. Siegler & Pyke (2013) [T1] found the independent-whole-numbers strategy on 12–21% of division trials. Ni & Zhou (2005) [T2] named and framed the construct.
- **Surface it early with:** the NAEP item itself, as a Day 1 or Day 2 warm-up review question. It takes ninety seconds and tells you more about the room than a twenty-item pretest.
- **When it shows up:** Number line. "Where's 12/13? Where's 7/8? Both almost 1. So the sum is about...?" Magnitude beats correction.

**Misconception: Mixed-number conversion slips.**
- **Looks like:** 2¾ → 5/4 (added instead of multiply-then-add), or 2¾ → ¾ (dropped the whole), or converting only one of the two mixed numbers before dividing.
- **Why it makes sense to them:** It's a multi-step routine with no meaning attached to the steps as usually taught, executed under load in the middle of a harder problem.
- **Evidence:** Sahin et al. (2020) [T2] names "incorrectly changing the mixed number to an improper fraction" as one of five documented error types. Beyond that, **the direct research base here is essentially empty** — an ERIC search for mixed-number/improper-fraction misconceptions returned no peer-reviewed results, and a broader error-pattern search returned one practitioner book (Spangler, 2011) [T3]. Treat any confident claim about *why* students make this error, including mine, as inference from the general fraction-arithmetic literature.
- **Surface it early with:** a warm-up review item pairing conversion with magnitude: "Write 2¾ as a single fraction, then mark it on this number line." The number line catches 5/4 instantly — it lands nowhere near 2¾.
- **When it shows up:** "Estimate first" is the entire fix. A student who says "2¾ is a bit under 3, so the top should be a bit under 12/4" cannot produce 5/4 and keep a straight face.

### 5. Concrete activities

All materials chosen for a Title 1 budget; anything purchased has a free substitute named.

**1. Pattern-block "how many fit?" (25–30 min, Day 3).** Hexagon = 1 whole, trapezoid = ½, rhombus = ⅓, triangle = 1/6. Students physically compose and decompose to answer "how many ½s are in 4?" and "how many 1/6s are in 1½?" before any symbolic division. *Materials:* pattern blocks if the school has them; otherwise print hexagons/trapezoids/rhombi/triangles on cardstock and cut once — free, and reusable all unit. *Embodies:* quotitive division as literal fitting; the answer is countable, so "division makes smaller" dies on contact. *Cannot show:* any denominator outside the halves/thirds/sixths/twelfths family — no fifths, sevenths, or ninths. *Evidence:* this is the actual verified activity in Illustrative Mathematics Grade 6 Unit 4 Lesson 4 [T3]; the "physically fit the divisor into the dividend" structure matches the WWC fractions guide's own ribbon example [T1, Recommendation 3 rated Moderate].

**2. Student-made paper fraction strips (20 min to build, reused all unit).** Fold paper strips into halves, fourths, eighths by successive folding; separately fold thirds and sixths. Cut, label, then lay strips against each other to count "how many ¼s fit in ½?" *Materials:* copy paper cut into strips, scissors, marker — free. *Embodies:* unit-fraction equivalence and quotitive division through direct comparison; students *making* the strips is itself the partitioning experience. *Cannot show:* denominators without a clean fold relationship (fifths ÷ sevenths), and non-unit divisors get awkward. *Evidence:* the WWC fractions guide [T1] states "two studies that meet WWC standards demonstrate that using manipulatives and visual representations, such as fraction squares and fraction strips, improved students' computational skills with fractions." The paper-folding *technique* specifically is Tier 4 (blog-sourced) — the strips themselves are the evidenced part.

**3. String/ribbon cutting (15 min per problem, Day 4).** Cut a measured length into pieces of a given fractional length; count the pieces; deal with the leftover. *Materials:* yarn or string, ruler, scissors — free from a craft bin. *Embodies:* quotitive division **with remainder**, which is the referent-unit trap in physical form. *Cannot show:* partitive division naturally; gets fiddly with eighths of an inch. *Evidence:* mirrors the WWC fractions guide's ribbon example [T1].

**4. Measuring-cup stations (10 min/station, ~4 stations, Day 8 or 11).** "How many ¼-cup scoops make 1⅔ cups?" *Materials:* one dollar-store set of dry measuring cups; fill with dry rice or beans (reusable, no food waste) instead of actual ingredients. *Embodies:* both interpretations with a real capacity unit; especially good for students still shaky on magnitude, since the cups make size tangible. *Cannot show:* eighths and sixths (not on a standard cup set); doesn't generalize to symbols on its own. *Evidence:* Tier 3/4 — station-rotation structure adapted from the Pennsylvania SAS lesson [T4]. Use for engagement and magnitude, not to carry an effectiveness claim.

**5. Tape diagrams (10–15 min per problem, every day from Day 4).** Bar for the dividend, tick marks at the divisor's size, count groups, label the leftover **in units of the divisor**, write the equation beside the drawing. *Materials:* plain or grid paper, ruler — free. *Embodies:* both meanings and the referent-unit switch; bridges directly to the equation. *Cannot show:* the algorithm — several worked diagrams are needed before "multiply by the reciprocal" is abstractable. *Evidence:* IES/REL Midwest (Griffin & Ibama-Johnson, 2024) [T2] uses exactly this for 5 ÷ ⅗ = 8⅓, explicitly tied to WWC Recommendation 3.

**6. Double number lines (15 min, Day 11).** Two parallel lines built from a rate context — miles above, hours below. *Materials:* adding-machine tape or paper strips, ruler — free. *Embodies:* the rate/ratio connection, foreshadowing 6.RP and 7.RP. *Cannot show:* pure fraction ÷ fraction structure as cleanly as a tape diagram; reserve for rate-flavored problems. *Evidence:* IM's "Traffic Jam" task [T3] and the IES/REL Midwest worked example [T2].

**7. Grid-paper area models (15–20 min, Day 11 only).** Shade a fractional area, partition into divisor-sized groups. *Materials:* printable grid paper, colored pencils — free. *Embodies:* continuity with the fraction-multiplication area work students already did. *Cannot show:* fraction ÷ fraction without the referent-unit hazard — Lamberg & Wiest (2015) [T2] documented in-service teachers failing exactly here. **Recommendation: do not use area models as the primary division representation.** Keep them for the multiplication connection.

**8. Estimation warm-up routine (5 min daily, all unit).** "Is 5 ÷ ⅔ bigger or smaller than 5?" "Which is bigger, 3 ÷ ¼ or 3 × ¼?" No computing. *Materials:* none. *Embodies:* quotient magnitude reasoning — the error detector. *Cannot show:* anything procedural. *Evidence:* the *target* is Tier 1 (Siegler & Lortie-Forgues, 2015 direction-of-effects data); the *routine format* (number talks) is Tier 3 practitioner consensus, not a verified trial.

### 6. The fade to abstract

**Say the nuance out loud, because the CRA slogan overstates it.** Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014) [T1], the systematic review, is candid: "Although empirical support for concreteness fading is increasing, most evidence in favor of this method is admittedly indirect and restricted to the domains of mathematics and science," and "the number of studies that instantiate the complete three-step transition is admittedly small. More research is needed to confirm the effectiveness of concreteness fading across tasks, settings, and populations." The same review names cases where fading **lost**: Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008) found abstract-only beat concrete-then-abstract; Braithwaite & Goldstone (2013) found concrete-first no better than abstract-only in combinatorics; Tapola et al. (2013) found concrete-only beat fading; Johnson et al. (2014) found a *reverse* abstract-to-concrete sequence outperformed fading. Fyfe & Nathan (2019) [T2] wrote a follow-up paper specifically to fix loose definitions of "concrete," "abstract," and "fading" across the literature — which implies a fair number of published "concreteness fading" studies didn't implement the construct as intended.

**What survives that scrutiny, and what it means here.** Three things.

First, **the fade is the intervention, not the manipulative.** The one fraction-specific data point inside the Fyfe review — Butler et al. (2003), where a full CRA sequence beat a representational-abstract sequence for students with math disabilities — is independently described in Fuchs et al. (2013) [T1] as having no random assignment and no control group, with "conclusions are tentative." So the fraction-specific CRA evidence is weak. Meanwhile the fraction-specific evidence for **linear/number-line representations faded into symbols** is strong and replicated (Fuchs et al., 2013; Barbieri et al., 2020; Jayanthi et al., 2021, all [T1]). Practical read for Steele: **keep the C stage short and make the R stage a number line or tape diagram, not a shape.** That is a re-weighting of CRA for this topic, not an abandonment of it, and it follows the evidence rather than the label.

Second, **the link must be explicitly narrated or the manipulative teaches nothing.** Uttal, Scudder & DeLoache (1997) [T2]: "all symbolic objects have a dual nature; they are simultaneously objects in their own right and representations of something else," and children "must learn to focus on what a symbolic object represents, not just on the object's inherent properties" — in the DeLoache scale-model work, children's symbolic use collapsed to chance when the orienting instruction was removed. McNeil & Jarvin (2007) [T2] add that both the pro- and anti-manipulative camps converge on one recommendation: **highly familiar or highly perceptually interesting manipulatives should be used less**, because their salience competes with the abstract referent. Plain paper strips beat cute plastic.

Third, **fading matters most for exactly Steele's low group, and shows up on the delayed test.** Ching and colleague [T1 — RCT, n=140 kindergartners, *Learning and Instruction*; co-author and publication year not confirmed this session] randomized five conditions including true fading and a reversed abstract-to-concrete sequence: all four intervention groups beat control, concrete representations were more effective than abstract **for children with lower prior knowledge specifically**, and fading's advantage over the other sequences was clearest on the **8-week delayed posttest**. Population is young, so transfer to sixth grade is inference, not evidence — but the pattern (fading helps low-prior-knowledge learners, and it shows up in retention rather than immediate performance) is worth knowing before you judge the approach on Friday's quiz.

**What students draw, and what the drawing must preserve.** The tape diagram must keep three things from the concrete stage: (a) the whole is labeled and visible; (b) the divisor appears as a *repeated unit of measurement*, marked off along the bar, not as a shaded region; (c) the leftover is labeled as a fraction **of the divisor unit**, not of the whole. If a student's drawing loses (c), they'll get the remainder wrong every time, and the drawing is worse than useless because it looks right.

**Fade triggers (observable, not scheduled).**
- **Concrete → Representational:** the student can *predict the count before manipulating* and is right most of the time. As long as they have to move the blocks to know, the blocks are doing the thinking.
- **Representational → Abstract:** the student draws the diagram and writes the matching equation **without prompting**, and the two agree. Agreement matters more than either alone — a correct equation next to a wrong diagram means the equation is memorized.
- **Abstract → fluent:** the student estimates first, computes, and catches their own errors against the estimate.

**Return signal, and how to frame it.** Any answer off by a reciprocal — 1/9 where 4 was right, 6 where ⅔ was right — is a "draw it" signal. Call it *checking your work with a picture*, and do it yourself on the board when you make an error. Sixth graders will abandon a crutch instantly and keep a checking tool for years; the framing is the whole difference.

### 7. Scaffolding and differentiation

**Prerequisite chain with diagnostic items.** Run these as the 2 review questions in warm-ups the week *before* the unit — cheapest possible diagnostic delivery.

| Prerequisite | Diagnostic item | What each wrong answer tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **3.NF/4.NF — fraction as a magnitude** | "Place ¾, 5/4, and 1½ on a number line from 0 to 2." | 5/4 placed past 1½ → reading numerals, not sizes. 5/4 placed beyond 2 → "5 is big" whole-number bias. Anything not on the line at all → fraction isn't a number yet. **Conceptual gap.** |
| **4.NF.B — comparison/equivalence** | "Which is bigger, ⅗ or ⅔? How do you know?" | "⅗, because 5 > 3" → whole-number bias on denominators. Correct answer with no reasoning → possibly memorized. **Conceptual if the reasoning is absent.** |
| **5.NF.B.3 — a/b = a ÷ b** | "3 people share 2 pizzas equally. How much does each get? Write it as a division *and* as a fraction." | Gets ⅔ but can't write 2 ÷ 3 → the fraction-as-division link is missing, which is what makes "invert and multiply" meaningless later. **Conceptual.** |
| **5.NF.B.7 — the easy division cases** | "How many ⅓-cup servings are in 2 cups?" (verbatim from the standard) | Answers ⅔ → applied multiplication. Answers 2/3 or 6 with no reasoning → check with a follow-up. **Conceptual if they can't picture it.** |
| **Whole-number measurement division** | "How many groups of 4 are in 20?" | Can only produce "20 shared among 4 people" → the schema fraction division extends doesn't exist. **Conceptual, but cheap to fix — 5 minutes before Day 3.** |
| **5.NF.B.4 — fraction multiplication** | "½ × ⅓ = ?" and "⅔ × 12 = ?" | Errors here block the *algorithm*, not the *meaning*. **Procedural — scaffold around it for two weeks.** |
| **Mixed ↔ improper** | "Write 2¾ as a single fraction, then mark it on a number line 0–4." | 5/4 → added instead of multiplied. ¾ → dropped the whole. Correct fraction, wrong placement → conversion is a ritual with no magnitude attached. **Procedural.** |

**Three gap types, three different responses.**

- **Conceptual gap — the model was never built.** No magnitude sense, no measurement schema, fraction-as-division missing. This cannot be scaffolded around and it cannot be fixed with more practice problems. It needs the concrete stage and number-line work. The good news is that this is precisely what Barbieri et al. (2020) [T1] fixed in **51 struggling sixth graders in six weeks**, with gains holding at a 7-week delayed posttest.
- **Procedural gap — the model exists, the routine is broken.** Mixed-number conversion, equivalence, the algorithm steps. Responds to explicit modeling with think-aloud, worked examples, and *incorrect* worked examples with the error named and explained (Booth et al., 2013 [T1] — but not error-hunting, per the Große & Renkl caveat). WWC RtI Recommendation 1 [T1, **Strong**] is the delivery spec: model, verbalize thinking, guided practice, corrective feedback, frequent cumulative review.
- **Fluency bottleneck — can do it, too slowly to think.** Multiplication facts consuming working memory during a division problem. **Give the multiplication chart.** The argument is a cognitive-load argument, and it's evidenced: Fuchs et al. (2013) [T1] found intervention effects were moderated by working memory, attentive behavior, processing speed, and listening comprehension but **not** by whole-number calculation skill. If you have to defend this to an evaluator: the standard asks students to interpret and compute *quotients of fractions*, not to demonstrate multiplication recall, and offloading the sub-skill puts the cognitive load on the learning objective.

**Acceleration over remediation.** TNTP's *The Opportunity Myth* (2018) [T3 — observational, ~4,000 students across five systems, **not causal**] reported students spent "more than 500 hours per school year on assignments that weren't appropriate for their grade," with low-income students, students of color, students with mild-to-moderate disabilities, and English learners most likely to be denied grade-appropriate work; classrooms where below-level students got grade-level assignments even 50% of the time showed on average 7 months of additional learning gain. **Label this correlational when you cite it** — it is a strong argument and a weak design, and conflating it with the Fuchs or Gersten effect sizes is exactly the move that gets a brief dismissed. The practical read: don't hold students out of 6.NS.A.1 until they pass a 5.NF gate. Deliver prerequisites as five-minute re-anchors immediately before the lesson that needs them (whole-number measurement division before Day 3; equivalent fractions before Day 5; conversion before Day 8).

**Differentiation that isn't three lessons.** One teacher, 55 minutes:
- **Same task, different numbers.** "How many ___ fit in ___?" runs from 4 ÷ ½ to 3⅓ ÷ ⅚ without changing the question, the diagram, or the discussion.
- **Worked-example pairs.** Students who need a model before attempting get a fully worked example to study and explain; students who don't, attempt first. Same problem set, different entry.
- **Strategic partnering on the diagram, independent on the equation.** Keeps the reasoning social and the accountability individual.
- **Extension = reversibility, not acceleration.** "What would the divisor have to be for the answer to come out exactly 6?" "Write a word problem whose answer is 2⅔ servings." "Here's a student's work with an error in it — find it and explain what they were thinking." Ma (1999) [T2] found U.S. teachers largely unable to generate a valid story context for 1¾ ÷ ½ while Chinese teachers produced several — story-writing is a genuinely hard task that deepens the same idea rather than racing into 7.NS.

**High-cost structures to avoid:** a separate parallel lesson for below-grade-level students. Nothing in the retrieved evidence justifies its cost in a 55-minute period; Fuchs-style small-group intervention worked at 3:1 with a trained tutor and is an intervention-block design, not a core-instruction design. If Steele has an intervention block, that's where the Fuchs/Barbieri model belongs — not inside core instruction.

### 8. Open questions and weak spots

**Where the evidence is genuinely thin:**

1. **Mixed numbers have almost no dedicated research base.** A targeted ERIC search for mixed-number/improper-fraction misconceptions returned **zero** peer-reviewed results; a broader error-pattern query returned one practitioner book (Spangler, 2011). Everything in this brief about mixed numbers rests on (a) one named error category in a preservice-teacher study of 34 people (Sahin et al., 2020) and (b) inference from general fraction-arithmetic findings. The recommendation to compress it into a sub-routine is defensible as a resource-allocation call — you should spend days where the evidence points, and it points elsewhere — but it is *not* backed by a study showing that compressing it is harmless. Watch it in your own data.

2. **Concreteness fading is oversold in professional-development contexts.** See §6. The review's own authors call the evidence "admittedly indirect," the complete three-step fade has been tested in a small number of studies, and there are published reversals. CRA is a reasonable default; it is not a settled finding, and a brief that presents it as one would be misrepresenting the literature.

3. **The strongest fraction RCTs are not whole-class studies.** Fuchs et al. (2013) and Barbieri et al. (2020) delivered intervention in small groups with trained personnel over 6–13 weeks. Their effect sizes do not transfer to 28 students in 55 minutes, and this brief does not claim they do. What transfers is the *design principle* (measurement interpretation, number lines, explicit instruction, magnitude as the mediator), not the effect size.

4. **Two population mismatches worth naming.** Much of the sharpest error data comes from **preservice teachers and adults** (Sahin et al., 2020; Ristiana et al., 2021; Siegler & Lortie-Forgues, 2015; Ma, 1999; Roche & Clarke, 2009), not sixth graders. That's actually a *stronger* argument — if adults who chose to teach math get this wrong, sixth graders certainly will — but the specific percentages should not be quoted as student data. And the Ching et al. concreteness-fading RCT used kindergartners.

5. **A missing number I looked for and could not find.** No NAEP item specifically on *fraction division* surfaced in this session — the classic large-scale items are fraction estimation (12/13 + 7/8) and decimal multiplication. If you want a national-benchmark number for fraction division specifically, Siegler & Pyke (2013)'s 20% is the best retrieved substitute, but it's a three-district sample, not a national one.

**Citations I could not fully verify this session, flagged in place:** Fischbein et al. (1985) sample details (n=623, 13 Italian schools, grades 5/7/9) come from secondary sources, though the 40%/98% finding was confirmed inside a fetched primary source. Tirosh & Graeber (1989) n=136 is secondary. Newton, Willard & Teufel (2014) and Anisiobi, Dyson & Jordan (2026) — citations and DOIs confirmed, full error taxonomies not retrieved. Cramer, Post & delMas (2002) findings came from an abstract-level search result, not full text. Ma (1999)'s often-quoted "9 of 23 U.S. teachers" figure was *not* verified against the primary text; the qualitative characterization was verified inside a fetched source (Sidney, Thompson & Opfer, 2019). The Achieve the Core coherence map for 6.NS.A.1 is a JavaScript app that returned no fetchable text — the prerequisite chain in this brief was rebuilt from the **actual CCSS standard texts**, which were fetched and verified, rather than from the coherence map.

**Leads worth Steele's own reading, not used to support any claim here:** Siebert (2002), "Connecting Informal Thinking and Algorithms: The Case of Division of Fractions," in the NCTM yearbook *Making Sense of Fractions, Ratios, and Proportions* — frequently cited alongside Gregg & Gregg for bridging invented strategies to the standard algorithm; citation verified, content not retrieved. Empson & Levi (2011), *Extending Children's Mathematics* — the CGI problem-type framework (equal sharing / multiple groups) is a clean planning structure; content not retrieved this session.

Days 7–12 continue the same shape. Two notes where the research doesn't fit the spine cleanly:

- **Day 6 needs more Mini-Lesson time than a standard split allows.** Explicit instruction with modeling, verbalized thinking, *and* guided practice with corrective feedback (WWC RtI Rec 1, Strong) is roughly a 25-minute Mini-Lesson with Work Time compressed to 15. Either extend the state or split Day 6 across two days (algorithm introduction, then comparison). Compressing the modeling to fit a 12-minute Mini-Lesson would discard the part of the recommendation that carries the Strong rating.
- **The estimation routine is not a state.** It's a 3-minute habit that belongs inside every Warm Up for the rest of the year, not a block that ends when the unit does. The Siegler & Lortie-Forgues direction-of-effects data suggests this intuition doesn't consolidate from a two-week exposure.
