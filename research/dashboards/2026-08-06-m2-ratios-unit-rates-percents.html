Module 2 — Ratios, Unit Rates, Percents

Grade 6 · Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind · CCSS 6.RP.A.1–3 · compiled 2026-08-06

Before anything else — your module map is off. In the CCSD Grade 6 Acc clarity guides, Topic 1 is Ratios, Topic 2 is Percents, Unit Rates and Conversions together, and Topic 3 is The Statistical Process (6.SP), not percents. Two of your three math topics share one district topic carrying six learning progressions.

And here is the arithmetic none of the individual briefs can do. Their three day-by-day plans total 15 days (6 ratios, 4 unit rates, 5 percents). The ratios brief separately argues its own topic wants eight to ten, which puts the evidence-supported total nearer 17 to 19. And all three propose taking the slack from the same conversions strand. It cannot absorb all three. Decide which topic takes the hit before day one, not in week three.

The short version

A sixth grader's default move is to add. Measured on 755 students across grades 4–10, sixth graders used an additive method on 57% of proportional problems and 58.5% of additive problems — they are not choosing a model, they have one move, and it fires regardless of the situation. The cheapest lever you have against this is number choice, and it is not close.

The thing most likely to go wrong is subtler than teaching the wrong strategy: it is teaching "ratios are multiplicative, not additive" as a rule. In a small controlled teaching experiment (n = 18 experimental, 17 control) that successfully taught students to stop over-applying proportionality, accuracy on genuinely proportional problems fell from 83.3% to 58.3%. You cannot trade one over-generalization for another. What has to be taught is the decision, not the rule.

The three numbers that should change what you do

22.5% → 5.5%
Grade 6 proportional-method use, integer vs non-integer ratios
Change the numbers so the scale factor is not a whole number and correct reasoning collapses by a factor of four, while additive errors climb 52.5% → 61.5%. A worksheet built on doubling and halving shows you a class that looks competent and leaves the misconception fully intact.
91% vs 51%
Same recipe mathematics, easy multiplier within one measure vs between two
Finding a unit rate is structurally the 91% move. Applying it is the 51% move. That is why students find the rate and then stall — and why unit rate belongs late in the sequence, not at the front. The gap is measured; that it is the unit-rate bottleneck is an inference, not a finding — the study is about double number lines, not unit rate.
0 of 14
Students who drew any model on percent scrap paper they were told to use
"Arranging the relations between the given data was done in their heads, and often that went wrong." On the same test, 8 of 14 computed a 15% discount on €600 as 600 ÷ 15. The bar has to be a required routine, not an offered option.

Where to spend the time

Highest leverage, in order

RatiosDeciding whether a situation is proportional at all. The highest-consequence error, and the only sub-skill whose absence guarantees the other two. (The single highest error rate is the 60% absolute-comparison figure on tab 2 — which sits upstream of this one: a student comparing one column has not formed a ratio yet.) 57.0% (Spain, grade 6) and 56.5% (Australia, Year 5–6) — two instruments, two continents, half a percentage point apart.

Unit ratesApplying a known rate to a new quantity. The between-measure move is where the cliff is. Most curricula spend their time on finding the rate, which is the easy half.

PercentsPercent as parts of a bar, drawn before anything is computed — and finding the whole. Find-the-whole is documented as the hardest of the three problem types and it is where a positional template fails, because the unknown sits in the position the rule assumes is known.

What gets compressed to pay for it. The three-notations lesson (colon / "to" / fraction bar) folds into vocabulary — and part-to-part in fraction form should be dropped from grade 6 entirely. The ratio-vs-rate definitional distinction goes: the Standards deliberately collapse it. Fraction–decimal–percent conversion drilling keeps the benchmark subset and drops the exhaustive matching. Coordinate-graph work gets one day as a classifying tool, not a solving tool. Unit conversion rides on ratio tables you have already built.

The sequence across the module

  1. Composed unit, physically iterated and partitioned Until "3 grape to 2 peach" is a single thing that can be doubled and halved, there is nothing for a diagram to represent.
  2. Qualitative comparison before any computation Forces both quantities into view — the 60% absolute-comparison error is upstream of additive-vs-multiplicative and surfaces here on day one.
  3. Tape diagram for same-unit ratios, double number line for different-unit The unit test is the selection rule. A double number line on same-unit quantities makes two different lengths stand for the same amount.
  4. Ratio table as the bridge from build-up to multiplication Scales past what a diagram can physically hold — and you create that pressure by choosing the numbers.
  5. Partition down to one: "what if I only want one?" Unit rate arrives as an answer to a question, as a member of the family, not as a definition at the front.
  6. Apply the rate to a non-integer target The between-measure move, isolated. With whole-number targets students build up instead and you learn nothing.
  7. Percent as out of 100, on a bar, with benchmark decomposition 50% / 10% / 5% / 1% computes the answer and leaves a felt sense of its size, which is the only thing that catches a reversed operation.
  8. Non-integer ratios and non-proportional situations, interleaved throughout Not an extension. This is the assessment of everything above.

Three first moves you can run tomorrow

Everything else on this page is an argument. These are tasks, with timings and a materials line, one per topic. Full activity sets — with what each one cannot show — are in the briefs.

Ratios · about 15 minutes · no materials

The paint comparison. Abby's paint is 1 cup red to 3 yellow. Zack's is 3 red to 5 yellow. Same shade? Students must answer three ways: make the red the same, make the yellow the same, make the total the same. Surfaces the 60% absolute-comparison error on day one and needs no arithmetic to enter.

Deliberately abandon it after a day — it says nothing about non-integer ratios, which is where the real error lives.

Unit rates · about 15 minutes · no materials

Both ways. One rate situation. Students compute both unit rates — dollars per balloon and balloons per dollar — then answer two questions, one that each rate answers well. The mathematics is in the choosing, not the dividing, so a student two years behind participates on the actual decision.

Percents · about 25 minutes · paper strips

Bar-building with benchmarks. Given a total, students mark 50%, 25%, 10%, 5%, and 1% on a bar, each computed from the last. Then they are asked for 15%, 35%, and 11% and must build them. Establishes the routine that carries the whole topic: mark 0%, mark 100%, mark what you know, then decide.

Free. Paper strips or a printed bar template.

The one that costs nothing and is worth the most: run any proportional problem twice in the same period — once with an integer scale factor, once without. It teaches nothing on its own; it reveals. Pair it with instruction. About 15 minutes, and it is a number change.

The progression — and why it is not CRA

The 2025 CRA meta-analysis covers 30 studies, all single-case design, 116 students, researcher-implemented in 73% of cases — and its study table contains zero ratio, proportion, or percent studies. Not thin evidence. No evidence. What does have cluster RCTs on exactly this content is schema-based instruction. What has expert design support — Illustrative Mathematics sequences discrete diagram → double number line → table — is a representational fade, published as design rationale with no efficacy trial behind that sequence. That distinction will matter the first time an evaluator presses on it.

Watch out for

Friendly percentages are hiding your problem. "Problems with 25%, 75% and 20% can easily be solved by finding an equal proportion. That is not possible, however, with percentages like 15% and 35%." Teach the benchmarks as tools; assess with 15%, 35%, 11%.

Percent gains wash out. In the one RCT that measured percent separately, gains for students with mathematics difficulties were significant at posttest and not at nine weeks — while ratio and proportion gains held. Plan the spiral before the unit ends.

CCSD success criterion 3.2.b — "determine equivalent ratios in a table by adding or subtracting two equal ratios" — is mathematically correct and pedagogically loaded. Students read "add" in a ratio table as "add the same number to both." Teach it as adding one whole batch, or not at all in the week you are killing the additive error.

CCSD success criterion 3.4.a — "division of the part by the percent, to determine the whole" — points at the single hardest item in a 677-student study (accepting 145 ÷ 0.11). Three of six surveyed textbooks avoided this method entirely to dodge it. Teach it, but put the bar underneath it first.

Dose is a real constraint. The same research group's 9–10 session versions of their intervention produced nothing for students with mathematics difficulties; only the six-week daily version worked. The rational-number meta-analysis puts the significant duration threshold at 20+ hours. Plan against that, or commit to the spiral.

RatiosThe ratio as a difference (additive reasoning)
3 kg sugar for 6 kg strawberries → 15 kg sugar for 18 kg strawberries
Why it makes sense to them
The previously-true rule is build-up by adding, which is a genuinely correct proportional strategy. The student is running a legitimate method with the wrong scope. This is why "stop adding" is the wrong correction — adding is half the right method.
How common
57.0% of grade 6 students on proportional problems (n=755, Spain). Independently, 56.5% of Year 5–6 students (n=140, Australia). Still 21.2% in grade 10.
Surface it early with
The paired box-loading task, both versions, same class period. Integer: "started together but Tom loads faster; Peter 40 → Tom 160; Peter 80 → ?" Non-integer: "Peter 40 → Tom 100; Peter 60 → ?" The second version is where the additive answer appears, because build-up is no longer cheap.
What to say when it shows up
Do not correct the arithmetic. Ask what one batch is, and whether that batch is still the same in their answer. 15-per-18 is not 3-per-6. Put both rows on a table and ask which one describes the grandmother's recipe. Confusion is step one; what do you know is that the recipe did not change.

Fernández et al. (2010) [T2]; Hilton et al. (2012) [T3]; Van Dooren, Vamvakoussi & Verschaffel (2018) [T2]

RatiosProportional reasoning applied to an additive situation
"Equally fast, Ellen started later. Ellen 4 → Kim 8. Ellen 12 → Kim 24" (correct: 16)
Why it makes sense to them
The missing-value format is the trigger. Every problem in that shape for three weeks has been proportional, so the student is reading the format, not the situation.
How common
21.4% applied a multiplicative strategy to an additive situation; 78.6% missed two-dimensional area scaling. And this error rises monotonically into secondary school while the additive error falls — the error does not get fixed, it flips.
Surface it early with
The same problem stem with one clause changed. "Equally fast but Tom started earlier" versus "started together but Tom loads faster." Identical numbers. The clause is the whole problem.
What to say when it shows up
This is the moment for try something. Run the situation with small numbers. If they started together and Tom is faster, what happened at 1 box? If Tom started earlier, what happened at 1 box? The simulation settles it without you ruling.

Hilton et al. (2012) [T3]; Fernández et al. (2010) [T2]. Van Dooren et al. (2018): inappropriate proportional reasoning is "to a large extent, instruction-induced."

Ratios Unit ratesAbsolute comparison in place of relative comparison
"Washing powder A costs the least" / "B costs a bit more but you get 10 more loads"
Why it makes sense to them
For their entire schooling, "which is more?" was answered by looking at one number. Nothing before grade 6 required holding two quantities at once. Both wrong answers are complete sentences that sound like reasoning.
How common
60.0% — the highest single error rate in the retrieved data. A further 35.0% considered one value without a related value. This is upstream of additive-vs-multiplicative: a student comparing one column has not formed the ratio at all.
Surface it early with
Two products where the cheaper one is the worse buy. Design that deliberately — if the cheap one is also better value, the error produces the right answer and stays hidden.
What to say when it shows up
Do not correct the comparison. Ask a different question: "how much for one load?" That forces both quantities into the same sentence. Then re-ask the original. They know two things about each box and have used one.

Hilton et al. (2012) [T3], n=140, two-tier diagnostic. The four answer options are reproduced in the paper and are directly usable.

RatiosThe colon read as a fraction bar
"Can 10 people be split in a ratio of 1:2?" → treats 1:2 as one-half
Why it makes sense to them
Two years of "a number over a number is a fraction," and the colon is being taught as an alternative notation for the same thing. CCSD success criterion 1.3.a ("I can write a ratio as a fraction") states the equivalence without the part-to-part exception.
How common
26.7% success on that item pre-instruction (n=15 — tiny sample; use the taxonomy, not the rate). The WWC panel names it directly: "even though ratios are often expressed as fractions, ratios and fractions are not always the same thing. Students often struggle with this common misconception."
Surface it early with
"2 blue tiles, 3 red tiles. Write the ratio of blue to red as a fraction. Now: what fraction of the tiles are blue?" Both are legitimate questions with different answers. The collision is the lesson.
What to say when it shows up
Do not resolve it by rule. Ask what the 5 is in each case. Part-to-whole has a whole; part-to-part does not. Then drop part-to-part fraction notation from grade 6 — it is not worth the damage.

Cheung, Reid O'Connor & Zunica [T3]; WWC fractions practice guide [T1 guide, Rec 4 rated Minimal]

Unit ratesDividing in the wrong direction
"6 balloons for $24, cost of 5?" → 24 ÷ 5, or 6 ÷ 24 = "$0.25 per balloon"
Why it makes sense to them
Two previously-true rules colliding. "Divide the bigger by the smaller" has never failed them. And division has always produced something smaller, and usually whole.
How common
Honest note: I found no study measuring reversed division in unit-rate problems specifically. The evidence here is transferred from the percent literature, where 8 of 14 students computed a 15% discount on €600 as 600 ÷ 15. Label it as transfer, not as a unit-rate finding.
Surface it early with
Ask for both unit rates on the same item — "dollars per balloon" and "balloons per dollar" — then ask which one answers the question. Both are legitimate; the choice is the mathematics.
What to say when it shows up
Estimate first. "About how much should one balloon cost — more than a dollar or less?" A student who has said "about $4" cannot then accept $0.25 without noticing it themselves.

van Galen & van Eerde (2013) [T2], n=14 — illustrative, not evidential

Unit ratesFinds the rate, cannot apply it
Computes "$4.33 per pound," writes it down, and stops
Why it makes sense to them
They have completed a within-measure operation, which they are good at. The next move is between-measure, which they are not. From the student's side the problem feels finished — a number has been produced.
How common
91% vs 51% on identical mathematics, easy multiplier within one measure vs between two. Note: that study is about double number lines, not unit rate. The mapping onto unit rate is an inference — a sound one that explains a phenomenon you will recognize, but a bridge, not a finding.
Surface it early with
Any rate problem with a non-integer target quantity, so build-up is unavailable. With whole-number targets students bypass the rate entirely and this error is invisible.
What to say when it shows up
Put it on the double number line. The unit rate is the pair at 1; the answer is the pair at 7. Ask what is between them. This is the moment the double number line earns its place — it makes the second move a drawing rather than a decision about which operation to trust.

Küchemann, Hodgen & Brown (2014) [T2], ICCAMS project item data

PercentsDivide the bigger by the smaller ("percent means divide")
"€600 bike, 15% off" → 600 ÷ 15 = 40   |   "24 out of 500" → 500 ÷ 24
Why it makes sense to them
Sharper than it looks: the student expects a school problem to have a clean computation, sees that 100 does not divide 15 nicely, notices that 600 ÷ 15 does, and takes it. They are reasoning about the test, not about the mathematics — and they learned to do that from the problem sets.
How common
8 of 14 students on the bike problem; only 4 of 14 correct. The 500 ÷ 24 form is documented separately as "percent means divide."
Surface it early with
15%, 35%, and 11%. Never 25%, 50%, or 75% on an assessment. "Problems with 25%, 75% and 20% can easily be solved by finding an equal proportion. That is not possible, however, with percentages like 15% and 35%."
What to say when it shows up
Do not correct the operation. Ask for 10% first. A student who has said "10% is 60" cannot hold "15% is 40" in the same sentence. The contradiction is theirs to notice.

van Galen & van Eerde (2013) [T2]; Ryan & Williams (2007) via Baratta et al. (2010) [T2]

PercentsDivision makes smaller, so this cannot be right
Rejects 145 ÷ 0.11 as a way to find the whole
Why it makes sense to them
Every division they have done for six years produced something smaller. The rule was true until decimals under 1 arrived.
How common
The single hardest question in a 677-student study. Revealingly, three of six surveyed Year 8 textbooks avoided find-whole-by-division entirely, apparently to dodge it. By contrast (145 ÷ 11) × 100 — the unitary method in one calculation — was "relatively very well done."
Surface it early with
Estimation before computation: "11% of a number is 145. Is the number bigger or smaller than 145? How do you know?" The estimate makes the direction a decision the student defends, before any operation is chosen.
What to say when it shows up
Go to the bar. If 11% is 145, then 1% is about 13, so 100% is about 1,300. The bar makes the answer's size obvious and the division becomes a shortcut for something already known. This is also why the 1% unitary route should be your primary method — it is the one students can hold.

Baratta et al. (2010) [T2], n=677; MMBDMS attributed to Bell, Swan & Taylor (1981)

PercentsThe percent sign dropped
"60 books, 20 taken. What percent remains?" → "20"
Why it makes sense to them
The percent sign has been treated as notation rather than as a unit. Nothing in their experience makes "20" and "20%" different kinds of answer.
How common
38% of 14-year-olds could compute the percentage remaining; 31% answered "20" rather than "20%." Described as "common across contexts."
Surface it early with
Problems where a bare count and a percent are both plausible answers to the question as written. Ask what the number counts.
What to say when it shows up
"Twenty what?" One word. It works because the student can answer it.

Ryan & Williams (2007) via Baratta et al. (2010) [T2] — primary source not retrieved

PercentsThe additive error living inside the proportion
Sets up 12/32 = x/8 correctly, then reduces the wrong pairs → 32/6 = x/6
Why it makes sense to them
The notation now looks right, which makes the additive reasoning harder to see. A documented variant: the student "thinks that the value of a fraction does not change when the same number is added to its numerator or denominator."
How common
The scale finding is the one to hold: the Spanish curriculum introduces the rule of three in grades 5 and 6, and 57.0% of those sixth graders still solved proportional problems additively. The algorithm did not install the concept.
Surface it early with
A proportion whose numbers make the additive answer visibly absurd — where the additive result is wildly the wrong size.
What to say when it shows up
The bar. Ask them to mark their answer on it. An additive answer lands somewhere that does not match the percent, and the picture says so without you ruling.

Çalışıcı (2018) [T3/T4], n=34, weak design — use the error descriptions, not the intervention claim; Fernández et al. (2010) [T2] for the scale

Unit ratesReading a ratio statement as an equation
Writes "3 m = 2 sec"
Why it makes sense to them
The equals sign has meant "the answer is" for six years, and it is the only relational symbol they have been issued.
How common
Named directly in the CCSS Progressions as a documented error. No prevalence figure was retrieved.
Surface it early with
Ask students to read their own notation aloud. "Three meters equals two seconds" is a sentence a sixth grader hears as wrong once they say it.
What to say when it shows up
Give them the arrow: 3 m → 2 sec. A one-symbol fix that prevents an expensive grade 7 habit.

The spiral is not optional, and it is the cheapest thing in this brief. Percent gains washed out at nine weeks for students with mathematics difficulties in the one trial that measured it, while ratio and proportion gains held. Put ratio, rate, and percent problems in the warm-up spiral every week for the rest of the semester, mixed so no two consecutive problems take the same strategy. The interleaving evidence supports this independently — 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83, on an unannounced test a month later. Read that trial honestly: its interleaved topics were algebra and geometry, not ratio, and the sample was an honors cohort that excluded nearly all students who had failed the state test. So it is a general practice principle transferred to your content and your room, not ratio-specific evidence. It still costs zero instructional days. If you do one thing from this whole brief besides changing your numbers, do the spiral.

Three gap types, three different responses

Conceptual gap — the model was never built

Which ones: multiplicative comparison; division direction; "out of 100"; division making bigger.

Response: these cannot be scaffolded around. Give each a five-minute just-in-time re-anchor immediately before the lesson that needs it — not a week of pre-teaching before the unit. The exception is "out of 100": if that diagnostic comes back badly, spend the whole first percent day there, because nothing downstream holds without it.

Procedural gap — the model exists, the routine is unreliable

Which ones: equivalent fractions; scaling in a table; benchmark conversions.

Response: clean correct worked examples, paired with a matched blank. The meta-analysis found correct-only examples outperformed incorrect-only and correct-plus-incorrect — and that adding self-explanation prompts moderated the effect negatively. So skip the "explain your thinking" box on the worked example. Save error-finding for extension.

Fluency bottleneck — they can do it, but slowly enough to consume working memory

Which ones: multi-digit division; decimal division; multi-digit multiplication.

Response: hand out the calculator and the multiplication chart, consistently, including on the check. The meta-analysis of 54 studies found problem-solving improved with calculator involvement and that "in all cases, calculator use did not hinder the development of mathematical skills" — with results going mixed specifically when calculators were not part of assessment. Handing the tool out only on hard days is the condition where the benefit disappeared.

Frame it to any reviewer as a construct argument, not a lowered expectation: 6.RP assesses proportional reasoning. Long division is a confound. Honest caveat: I found no research at all on multiplication charts as a scaffold — that recommendation is reasoning from cognitive load theory, not a retrieved finding.

If a class set of calculators is not available: every Chromebook has one, and a photocopied multiplication chart plus a 1% / 10% reference strip covers most of what the calculator is doing in this module. This is the one materials assumption in the whole brief that could fail in a Title 1 room — decide which fallback you are using before day one, not mid-lesson.

Differentiation that is one task, not three lessons

Number choice inside a single problem set. Same context, scale factors of 2, 3, and 7/4. Same percentage question at 10%, 15%, 35%. Everyone works the same problem; the ceiling is in the numbers.

The two-problem structure. Pose it with a common factor, then re-pose the same context without one. Everyone gets into (a); (b) is where the range shows up, and you choose which group presents first.

Problem-type sorting before solving. A card sort requiring no computation, so a student two years behind participates on the actual mathematical decision. This has RCT evidence behind it and it is the one move the comparison literature specifically endorses for low-prior-knowledge students — who "learned best from either comparing problem types or studying examples sequentially," because side-by-side method comparison "may be too overwhelming without sufficient prior knowledge."

Accept-or-reject tasks for percent. Students were markedly better at rejecting wrong calculations (54%) than accepting right ones (36%). Evaluation is genuinely more accessible than generation for this population — and it targets the weaker skill directly.

Above grade level too

Extend by reversibility, generalization, and error-finding — not by racing into grade 7.

"What would the second quantity have to be for these two ratios to be equivalent?" · "The unit rate is $2.75 per pound. Write a ratio that produces it." · "Here are two rates from the same situation, per-dollar and per-item. Show they multiply to 1 and explain why." · "Here are three student bars for the same percent problem. Two are wrong. What was each student thinking?"

Error-finding is the highest-value extension because it recruits exactly the misconception analysis you want circulating in the room anyway.

The honest ceiling. For the 806 students with mathematics difficulties in the strongest trial available, the effect was 0.32 SD versus 0.46 SD for the full sample — and "growth rates did not vary significantly between students; treatment did not moderate student growth." The intervention raised the level without changing the rate of learning. Separately, effects were significantly larger for grades 3–6 than 7–9, and larger when delivered by research personnel than by school personnel. Both gradients point down for a sixth-grade teacher running this himself. Plan against the pessimistic numbers.
You named this: teaching percent by setting up ___ is ___% of ___ as a proportion with positional rules — first blank top left, second top right, third bottom left, percents with percents and numbers with numbers — then cross-multiplying and dividing. Here is what the evidence actually says, including the part that supports you.

The template is not wrong. The setup is where it breaks.

It is a real algorithm and it gets a right answer when it is set up correctly. The problem the research identifies is narrower and more specific than "don't use it": the placement decision is the failure point, and a positional mnemonic is a memory aid holding a decision that should be made by reading the situation. When the mnemonic is the only thing holding the placement, a slip produces a confidently wrong answer with nothing to catch it.

The documented setup failures

Writes 12/32 = x/8 correctly, then reduces the wrong number pairs → 32/6 = x/6

"The students do not understand which number pairs among the four number pairs in the numerator and denominator to reduce." — Çalışıcı (2018), n=34, weak design; use the error description, not the intervention claim.

Asked for 24 out of 500 → computes 500 ÷ 24

The bigger number went where the rule said the bigger number goes. — Ryan & Williams (2007) via Baratta et al. (2010).

"Thinks the value of a fraction does not change when the same number is added to its numerator or denominator"

The additive misconception living inside the proportion template, where the notation looks correct. — Çalışıcı (2018).

Position one: delay or omit it in grade 6

This is the position of every expert design document retrieved.

CCSS Progressions: "Although it is traditional to move students quickly to solving proportions by setting up an equation, the Standards do not require this method in Grade 6." And the reason, at grade 7: the constant of proportionality "is obscured by the traditional method of setting up proportions."

NCTM research brief: teachers should promote proportional reasoning by "delaying the instruction of the cross-multiplication algorithm until students have already gained experience with forming ratios and understanding proportions as an equivalence of ratios."

WWC fractions practice guide (Recommendation 4 — rated Minimal Evidence, the lowest of three levels; say so when you cite it): develop conceptual understanding of ratio, rate and proportion strategies before cross-multiplication. Two specific warnings from the companion: students "may attempt to apply the procedure without regard for whether a problem is set up correctly," and "by using cross-multiplication exclusively, students may fail to recognize more efficient ways to solve a proportion."

Van Dooren, Vamvakoussi & Verschaffel are the most direct. Scrupulously fair first — "All strategies that were described above lead in principle to a correct answer" — then: "research points out that students themselves rarely choose them, and mistakes are very common. One of the main causes seems to be that these algorithms consist of the blind manipulation of numbers according to formal rules that have no transparent relation whatsoever with the original problem context."

Battista & Van Auken Borrow (via a practitioner brief, Tier 4): students are "not ready to understand why cross multiplication works until they have studied properties of algebra," which is later than middle school.

Position two: teach it as one strategy on a menu

This is what the strongest experimental evidence actually did. Jitendra's schema-based instruction — 1,999 students, 82 classrooms, 0.46 SD, the only large RCT base on this content — explicitly had teachers "encourage students to deeply process information about when, how, and why to use a broad range of methods (e.g., equivalent fractions, unit rate, cross multiplication) for a given class of problems," with selection based on the numbers.

Cross-multiplication was in the room, and the results were the best in the literature. Anyone defending the template can point at this legitimately.

What the disagreement actually hinges on

Whether the algorithm is the organizing structure of the topic or an item on a menu the student chooses from. Position one objects to the first; Jitendra implemented the second. Read that way, they largely agree.

The recommendation

Keep the template if you want it. Move it to the end, and strip the positional mnemonic.

Replace "first blank goes top left" with "draw the bar, then write what you see." The bar is the "first consider how the quantities relate" step, made mandatory. That is the whole change — you are not abandoning a routine that works for you, you are putting one step in front of it.

Two reasons, and the second is the one to defend hardest

1. The empirical bridge. The Spanish curriculum introduces the rule of three in grades 5 and 6. Those sixth graders had already been taught the algorithm, and 57.0% still solved proportional problems additively. The algorithm did not install the concept. It is the closest thing to a direct test available and it points one way. It is also not a controlled comparison — the study did not manipulate whether students got the algorithm.

2. The failure is the setup, and the positional rule is the setup. Every documented error above is a placement error, not a computation error. As one practitioner brief states the mechanism: "Simple 'plugging in' numbers from a context into the a/b = c/d structure will often result in mistakes or uninterpretable calculations if the student does not first consider how the quantities relate."

The encouraging part

The alternative is already in your district guide. CCSD Topic 2 progression 3.2 and 3.3 already ask for double number lines to find the part and the whole. Progression 2.1 already asks for benchmark percents — 100%, 50%, 25%, 20%, 10% — and a rule for 1%. That is the research-supported approach, sitting in the clarity guide you already have.

The change is one of order and emphasis, not of content: lead with the bar, let the proportion arrive on day five.

One thing worth saying plainly

There is a real cost to changing this that the research does not weigh: you have a routine that works for you, that you can deliver consistently, and that students can execute. And the pull toward this method is systemic, not personal — the same synthesis reports that "in-service teachers strongly favor the cross-multiplication approach... Often, they did not acknowledge the value of any of the other strategies... and considered them as less sophisticated or even wrong."

The lowest-cost change is not abandoning the template. It is putting the bar in front of it and choosing harder percentages.

What would flip this recommendation. Evidence that an explicit positional setup routine reduces setup errors relative to a representation-first routine. I searched for it and found nothing either way — no trial has compared percent-bar-first against proportion-setup-first instruction in a middle-grades classroom. This is a bet on convergent design evidence plus a documented error taxonomy. A well-founded bet, and a bet. If someone challenges it, that is the honest answer to give them.
