6th Grade Decimals

6.NS.B.2 6.NS.B.3 6.NS.C.6c/7  ·  Title 1, grade level to ~2 years behind  ·  Compiled 2026-07-29

The short version

Sixth grade adds almost no new decimal concepts — the standards authors position 6.NS.B.3 as consolidating algorithms first met in 5th grade, and the genuinely new base-ten content this year is negative numbers. What 6th grade actually inherits is a room where roughly 70% of students do not reliably know which of two decimals is larger. This is a repair job wearing a fluency unit's clothes.

The thing most likely to go wrong: your item diet quietly manufactures the misconception. When every comparison problem has the same number of decimal places, treating decimals as whole numbers always works — so students learn that it works. Both fixes below are free.

31% expert

Correct on 4.6 + 2.3 vs 5.3 + 2.42. A single nameable alignment failure. Highest points-per-minute in the unit.

Direction of effect, × and ÷ below 1

Below chance on "is 0.77 × 0.63 bigger than 0.77?" And uncorrelated with comparison ability (r = 0.01) — nothing else you teach will fix it.

What gets compressed to pay for it. Decimal long division gets the algorithm taught efficiently, then moves into spiral warm-ups rather than owning multiple days — nothing in the literature marks it as a distinct conceptual hurdle beyond whole-number division fluency. Percent waits for the 6.RP unit; here it is only a third name for the same point on the line.

The sequence

  1. Diagnose before you teach anything10 min, day one. Ragged comparison test sorts the room into three piles and routes the next four days.
  2. Build magnitude on a length modelEvery later error traces back here, and length is the only attribute students can compare by eye without computing.
  3. Iterate concept and procedure — do not block themThe one on-point 6th grade decimal experiment found alternating beat teaching all the concepts first.
  4. Addition and subtraction, ragged from the first problem setSo alignment is the visible issue rather than an occasional exception.
  5. Multiplication via area, before any decimal-point ruleDerive the rule. Do not issue it.
  6. Direction of effect as its own named target"Bigger or smaller, and why" — taught as a sentence that names both the operation and the size of the numbers.
  7. Division last, after whole-number division fluencyThe coherence map makes 6.NS.B.2 a prerequisite of 6.NS.B.3, not a sibling.
  8. Interleave from the midpoint onMixed sets, not blocked. Expect in-class accuracy to drop — that drop is the intervention working.

The progression at a glance

The evidence does not support a clean C→R→A withdrawal schedule for decimals — there is not one concreteness-fading study on decimals, and the CRA evidence-based-practice designation explicitly covers only whole-number computation with regrouping. What it supports: a length model, notation shown alongside from day one, withdrawn per student on demonstrated accuracy.

Watch out for

MoneyA trap, not a shortcut. It permits complete success without any decimal understanding, caps at hundredths, and reading it aloud as "three dollars and seventy-six cents" actively teaches the two-separate-whole-numbers misconception. Application late in the unit, never the entry model.
Base-ten blocksStudents trained that the unit cube is one now have to hold that the flat is one. The one decimal-specific manipulative comparison found a length model markedly more accessible, partly because of that prior use.
"Count the decimal places"Both the rule and estimation have documented failure modes. Estimation cannot place the point in 0.023 × 0.0045; the rule without meaning produces the 73% of multiplication errors that are correct digits with a misplaced point. Teach the rule as a conclusion students derive; keep estimation as the plausibility check.
Read this first. The two common comparison errors need opposite instruction. Longer-is-larger students have broken place value. Shorter-is-larger students have good place value and a correct fraction idea they cannot coordinate with quantity. Reteaching place value to a shorter-is-larger student is wasted time — and that is the error most adults in the building do not know exists.
Longer is larger0.25 > 0.5
Why it makes sense to them
In whole numbers, more digits means bigger — 3 < 30 < 300. And comparing equal-length decimals as whole numbers always works, which is most of what they have practiced. A rule that used to be true, applied one domain too far.
Also looks like
4.7 < 4.08 ("the zero doesn't matter and 8 > 7") · 0.21 = 0.0021
Surface it early with
Ordering sets that mix digit lengths: 0.3, 0.4, 0.38, 0.312. Never 0.34, 0.35, 0.36 — they get those right and you learn nothing.
When it shows up
Don't reteach the rule. Send them to the length model, build both, ask which is longer before looking at the numerals. "You built the one with more digits and it came out shorter. So what do you know now?"
Shorter is larger (denominator-focused)0.4 > 0.83
Why it makes sense to them
When you cut a whole into more parts, the parts get smaller — a genuinely true and important fraction fact. These students have good place value and a correct fraction idea; they are failing only to coordinate the size of the pieces with how many there are.
Student wording, verbatim from the research interviews
"4.7 > 4.08, since this has 8 hundredths and this has 7 tenths." · "7.457 > 4.4502, because hundredths are bigger than ten-thousandths."
Surface it early with
0.75 vs 0.5 · 7.942 vs 7.63 · 2.8325 vs 2.516 · 0.426 vs 0.3. A longer-is-larger student gets all of these right.
When it shows up
Do not reteach place value — they have it. Make them count as well as size: "You're right that a tenth is bigger than a hundredth. How many hundredths are in 0.83? How many in 0.4?" The reasoning is nearly correct, which makes it worth celebrating out loud.
Reciprocal / negative thinking0.3 > 0.4  ·  0.20 > 0.35
Why it makes sense to them
1/12 > 1/3456 is completely correct fraction reasoning applied to the wrong notation. −12 > −18 is completely correct integer reasoning applied to the wrong notation. Decimals, fractions and negatives all arrive as "the opposite of the big-number operations."
Why it matters most
Highest persistence of any non-expert pattern (33% on retest) and it rises with grade. One Year 6 class ran 27%.
Surface it early with
Equal-length pairs — 0.3 vs 0.4. The original researchers never included these "because it is hard to imagine any student who could answer this incorrectly," which is exactly why this stayed invisible for decades. Plus 0.6 vs 0, the only item that separates negative from reciprocal thinking.
When it shows up
Physical placement on a line with zero marked. And when you teach negatives later this year, use decimal examples deliberately — the Years 7–8 spike in this error is attributed to negative-number instruction that never mentions decimals.
Right-edge alignment6 + 0.32 = 0.38
Why it makes sense to them
Right-align, then add is correct for every whole-number addition problem and every equal-decimal-place problem — which is nearly the entire diet they have been fed.
How common
42% correct on 6 + .32, with the single answer 0.38 accounting for 45–50% of all errors on that item. 4 + .3 = 0.7 is 48–51% of errors on that one.
Surface it early with
A matched pair on the same page: 4.6 + 2.3 immediately followed by 6 + 0.32. The gap between the two is the diagnosis, and the student sees it too.
When it shows up
"Estimate first — is 6 + 0.32 closer to 6 or closer to 0?" Then: the decimal point marks the ones column, so line up the ones. That motto appears independently in the Melbourne materials and in the CCSS progression document — good sign it belongs on your wall.
Multiplication makes bigger"0.77 × 0.63 > 0.77 — TRUE"
Why it makes sense to them
For every whole number at or above 1, multiplying does make it bigger. Nine years of consistent evidence.
Why it blocks self-correction
Because "multiplication makes bigger," a wrong answer like 5.5 × 0.6 = 33 looks right and the correct answer looks wrong. Students reject valid procedures on the strength of it. 49% missed all four below-one items while getting all 12 other items right.
Surface it early with
True/false plausibility items with numbers too awkward to compute, paired with "explain why." The explanation is the real diagnostic.
When it shows up
Do not correct the answer — get the sentence. Students who succeed name both the operation and the size of the numbers: "Multiplying by a number less than one makes it smaller, because you're taking part of it." Unconditional statements went with under 10% accuracy; operation-plus-magnitude statements with 93%.
Discreteness (density)"Nothing is between 3.14 and 3.15"
Why it makes sense to them
Every natural number has a successor and there is nothing between 63 and 64. Also shows as "after 4.9 comes 4.10."
Why it deserves real time
Across 1,343 students grades 4–12, natural-number bias was weakest on size tasks, stronger on operations, and by far strongest on density — and it did not disappear with grade except on size. If you only assess comparison, you will overestimate understanding.
Surface it early with
"How many numbers are between 3.14 and 3.15?" Score it three ways, not two: zero (successor principle), exactly nine (bounded by one extra place), infinitely many (correct). The middle category is progress and needs a different next move.
When it shows up
Run "Number Between" — two students hold the endpoints and stand at the ends of an imaginary line; a third writes a number in between and stands where it goes. Start with integers, not 0-to-1, so students see decimals live between all integers. Avoid the procedural fix ("write 0.30 and 0.40, now put 0.31 in") — it is documented as meaningless and quickly forgotten without grounding.

Prerequisite diagnostics — use these as warm-up review items

PrerequisiteDiagnostic itemWhat each wrong answer tells you
4.NF.C.7
compare decimals to hundredths		 "Which is larger, 0.4 or 0.83? Explain." "0.83, because 83 > 4" → passes by luck on a broken rule; will fail 0.5 vs 0.36. "0.4, because tenths are bigger than hundredths" → shorter-is-larger; place value is intact, do not reteach it. "0.83, because 0.4 is 40 hundredths and 83 > 40" → secure.
5.NBT.A.1
each place is 1/10 the place to its left		 "3 tenths is how many hundredths?" Blank or "3" → no re-unitizing between adjacent columns. Only 39% of 6th graders answer this correctly. This is the item that most often explains an alignment failure.
5.NBT.A.3
compare decimals to thousandths		 "Order: 0.3, 0.4, 0.38, 0.312" Any ordering that tracks digit count. Ragged by design — an equal-length set diagnoses nothing.
4.NF.C.6
decimal notation for fractions		 "Write 1/5 as a decimal." "0.5" → the classic. "1.5" → documented in over 25% of 7th graders in a US national survey.
5.NBT.B.5 / B.6
whole-number × and ÷ fluency		 "48 × 7" and "852 ÷ 4" Slow-but-correct is a fluency bottleneck, not a conceptual gap. Different response — see below.

Three gap types, three different responses

Conceptual
the model was never built		Longer-is-larger and density errors live here. Cannot be scaffolded around; requires the length model. Budget days, not minutes.
Procedural
model exists, routine is slow or error-prone		Alignment errors in a student who can answer "3 tenths is how many hundredths." Responds to worked examples, correct/incorrect example pairs, practice structure. Budget minutes.
Fluency bottleneck
can do it, but working memory is consumed		A student who can reason about 2.4 × 0.6 but stalls on 24 × 6 is not having a decimal problem. Give them the multiplication chart so the load lands on the decimal placement. That is a cognitive-load argument, not lowered expectations — worth framing that way for anyone reviewing the plan.

Just-in-time, not a two-week re-teach

Do not run a place-value unit before touching 6.NS. Re-anchor "the decimal point marks the ones column" for five minutes immediately before the alignment lesson, and let the length model carry the students who need it through grade-level problems.

Differentiation that is not three lessons. The number choices do the differentiating — within one problem set, 4.6 + 2.3 and 6 + 0.32 are the same task at very different difficulties. Correct/incorrect example pairs give a model to students who need one before attempting, and the evidence says low-prior-knowledge students benefit as much as high-prior-knowledge students.

Above grade level, deepen rather than accelerate. Reversibility: "What would the second number have to be for the answer to be exactly 1?" Generalization: describe the whole solution set for a digit-box inequality. Error-finding: hand them the wrong work and ask which rule the fictional student was using — the extension that most directly serves the culture, because it makes a student think about how someone else was thinking.

The dose is small, which is the most encouraging finding here. The Melbourne group ran a one-hour teacher PD plus targeted lesson plans. Classes that did no decimal work showed no change; classes that used the length model on one to four occasions saw "about one half of the children who had a misconception move to expertise," still holding four months later. Small sample, no randomization, teacher self-selection — but the effective dose in this literature is four well-targeted lessons on a diagnosed misconception, not a rebuilt unit.
