Behavior Redirection

6th grade Title 1 grade level to ~2 years behind  ·  ~22 Tier 1 · ~20 Tier 2 · ~15 Tier 3/4  ·  Compiled 2026-07-29
Read this first. The defensible core is small and boring: walk over, say it quietly, one or two sentences, name the replacement behavior, praise the second they comply. Every part of that has evidence. The famous technique layered on top of it mostly doesn't. One honest caveat before you use a single line below: nobody has experimentally tested the wording of a redirection. No retrieved study manipulates private vs. public, question vs. command, or naming-the-behavior vs. naming-the-student and measures compliance or escalation. The ladder is evidenced. The exact sentences are craft.

The ladder — what to do, in order

  1. Precorrect — before the moment you already know is coming Name the expectation ahead of the structure, not after the break. Active supervision plus precorrection: 7 of 7 studies met the CEC quality criterion, and one demonstration was literally in a 6th grade general education classroom. It costs about eight seconds. T2
    Say“Next four minutes are silent — just you and the paper. Then you check with your partner.”
  2. Proximity and scan — the correction is the walk Keep moving. The degree of active supervision — not the supervisor-to-student ratio — accounted for the most variance in problem behavior. Most of the time this step is the whole intervention and you never open your mouth. T2
    SayNothing. Stand there. Tap the problem number on their paper and keep walking.
  3. Quiet, brief, specific — then leave Get close. One or two sentences. Name the behavior and the replacement. O'Leary et al. (1970) ran a reversal across five classes over four months: switching from loud to soft reprimands reduced disruption; switching back to loud produced a consequent increase; switching to soft again reduced it. That is a demonstrated functional relationship, not a correlation. In this literature an “explicit reprimand” is not harsh — it means names the behavior, states exactly what to do instead, concisely. The praise-on-compliance step is the part most teachers skip, and it is the part that amplified the effect. T2

The threshold matters more than the philosophy: schools that fully complied with Philadelphia's suspension ban saw no peer harm; partial compliers saw peer math fall 0.06 SD. Same kid, second time todayThe bias checkpoint
Why this exact moment
In two randomized experiments with practicing teachers responding to real office-referral records, race manipulated by name only, there was no race difference after the first infraction — p = .999 on feeling troubled, p = .686 on discipline severity. The difference appeared at the second, and the mediator was pattern perception: whether the teacher perceived “a pattern.” The national data match the lab — Black students were 16.7% of enrollment, 33.3% of students suspended once, and 44.3% of students suspended multiple times. The disparity widens exactly at the repeat margin the lab result predicts. T2
The one question
Am I responding to this incident, or to a story I've started telling about this kid?
Read the effect sizes honestly
Study 1 (n = 53) gave d ≈ 0.9. The better-powered Study 2 (n = 191) gave d ≈ 0.3–0.5 — the classic small-sample inflation pattern. Trust Study 2. And these are judgments about paper records, not live referrals.
You're reaching for a referral and the reason is “disrespect”Highest-risk category there is
What's actually going on
Racial disparity in discipline originates at the classroom referral, and it lives almost entirely in the subjective categories. Across 1,154,686 students in 1,824 schools, disproportionality in subjective office referrals explained the vast majority of variance in total disproportionality. The discriminant analysis found no evidence Black students were referred for more serious or a greater variety of offenses — a different pattern: White students referred more for smoking, leaving without permission, vandalism, obscene language (documentable); Black students for disrespect, excessive noise, threat, loitering (interpretive). T2
Why it matters more in your building than in an elementary
African American students were 2.19× as likely to be referred in elementary and 3.78× in middle school. Middle school is where the multiplier peaks. Disproportionality is also elevated in classrooms rather than other settings, for incidents rated more severe, and it varies substantially by time of day.
The move
Step 4 instead: the private conversation. And log the reason plus the period, sorted subjective vs. objective. Honest caveat: the “vulnerable decision point / neutralizing routine” idea — a pre-committed if–then delay before acting on a subjective call — is well motivated by this data, but no experimental test showing it reduces referrals or disproportionality was retrieved. Treat it as theory-motivated, not proven.

What's actually going on
Individual redirects do not scale. A whole-room problem is a structure problem — a transition you didn't precorrect, a task nobody can enter, or an independent block you're supervising from your desk.
The move

You want to offer a choice to calm them downWrong end of the task
What the evidence says
A 2026 meta-analysis of 34 studies, 89 students, 170 cases, K–12 found instructional choice significantly increased desired classroom behaviors (LRR = .186, 95% CI [.042, .330]) but did not significantly decrease challenging behaviors (LRR = −.468, 95% CI [−1.1, .163] — the interval crosses zero). The same review group that rated praise, precorrection and active supervision as potentially evidence-based classified instructional choice as “insufficient evidence” under CEC criteria. T1
The move
Keep choice — move it earlier. Which problem set, which representation, which order, offered at the front of a task. Effects were larger for secondary students, so it is a real lever for your room. Just not after a kid is already off.
What this costs you. The ladder only works if steps 0 and 1 are actually happening, which means you are not sitting at your desk during independent work and you are spending eight seconds before the warm-up naming the expectation. That is the price, and there is no version where you skip prevention and buy a better script instead. The one observational study of middle school classrooms found teachers demonstrated low rates of appropriate responses to disruptive behavior, and that those responses had the strongest association with total rates of disruptive and on-task behavior — stronger than praise rate, stronger than OTR rate. Correlational, but it points squarely at the thing most of us are worst at.

The short version

The evidence is strong on prevention architecture and on the costs of exclusion. It is surprisingly thin on the exact micro-moves teachers get trained in — most of the famous numbers are weaker than their reputations. These are the figures that should actually move your defaults.

The mediator is the whole point

In that coaching RCT, Black students had a low probability of referral specifically with teachers who increased their skill at engaging students in high-level analysis and inquiry. Not with teachers who got better at redirection. That should reorder your priorities: your OTR rate, your instructional match, and your seating chart do more for behavior than anything you say after the fact.

Corroborated from an unexpected direction. 570 Belgian children were followed from 2nd to 3rd grade through a Good Behavior Game intervention with observational mediation analysis. The active ingredient was the reduction in teachers' negative remarks — that predicted increased on-task behavior and decreased talking-out, and those classroom changes in turn mediated the intervention's effect on hyperactive and oppositional behavior development. The game apparatus wasn't the mechanism. The teacher talking less negatively was.

First, the micro-move literature is almost entirely single-case — PND, Tau-U, LRR and between-case SMD are not Cohen's d and should never be lined up next to it. Second, effect sizes shrink on replication in this literature, repeatedly. The empathic-discipline suspension halving became a non-significant 2.4 percentage points. The referral-bias effect went from d ≈ 0.9 to d ≈ 0.3–0.5. Discount headline numbers accordingly, including the ones above.

The frame

The strongest classroom-level result in this entire brief is not a behavior technique. It is a two-year coaching RCT in 86 secondary classrooms that eliminated the racial referral gap inside intervention classrooms — and the mediator was teachers getting better at engaging students in high-level analysis and inquiry. Not a script. The work itself.

Everything below is the version of that finding one teacher can run without a coach, a co-teacher, or an administrator's budget.

Six routines that make the redirect unnecessary

  1. Precorrection in the warm-up window Before the warm-up, name the one expectation that matters for today's structure — “this is silent, then partner check” — and name it before anyone breaks it. This is the CEC-rated move, and it was demonstrated in a 6th grade general education classroom, where active supervision plus precorrection produced a classroom-wide decrease in minor behavioral incidents. Eight seconds. T2
  2. Circulate during every independent block. Do not sit. Active supervision went 7-for-7 on CEC quality indicators across 15 cases and 1,686 participants. And the study closest to your room complicates the usual difficulty story in a useful way: a multi-element structural analysis with three middle school boys (two 6th grade, one 8th) in honors math, randomizing easy/hard × high/low teacher attention daily, found attention, not difficulty, drove off-task behavior — 10.00% vs 35.67% off-task intervals for one student, 3.06% vs 14.86% for another, 6.33% vs 26.83% for the third. Difficulty showed no consistent relationship. The authors flag that “high attention” also meant proximity and possible direct assistance — which arguably strengthens the practical read. T2/T3
  3. Response cards, not hands Under formal CEC criteria, response cards specifically — not “increasing opportunities to respond” in general — earned a rating as a potentially evidence-based practice. Mixed choral-and-individual responding beat either pure format: lowest off-task percentage, lowest disruptive rate, highest active student responding. T2
  4. Assigned seats, rows for independent work Disruption during independent seatwork ran more than three times higher when students chose their own seats. The narrative synthesis of 8 studies: students display higher levels of appropriate behaviour during individual tasks when seated in rows, with disruptive students benefiting the most. Clusters only when the task is genuinely collaborative. Let the task dictate the arrangement. T2/T3
  5. A praise tally — a counter, not a target The most genuinely useful finding in the praise cluster is that coaching educators to increase behavior-specific praise meets CEC guidelines to be considered an evidence-based practice. Self-monitoring your own praise rate has better warrant than most scripts. A silent interval timer and a click counter is the entire intervention. There is no threshold to hit; only a direction. T2
  6. If you want a points system, make it a group contingency Individual token economies do not clear the bar. Group contingencies do — “particularly for addressing challenging behavior in general education classrooms.” See the table below. T1

Points systems: the one distinction that matters

SystemVerdict
Individual token economy A systematic evaluation applying WWC single-subject standards plus meta-analysis across four effect-size types to 24 studies concluded the research “does not provide sufficient evidence to be deemed best-practice based on the WWC criteria.” T1
Group contingency The same lead author's review found the opposite — the literature “demonstrated sufficient rigor, evidence, and replication to label the intervention as evidence-based,” with the honest caveat that reporting was too thin to say for whom and under what conditions. The 2017 update corroborated it and found group contingencies effective “particularly for addressing challenging behavior in general education classrooms.” T1
Praise and specific positive feedback Safe on both sides of the intrinsic-motivation fight. The two famous meta-analyses agree more than the debate suggests. One (128 studies) found engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28) while positive feedback enhanced free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31), with tangible rewards more detrimental for children than college students. The other (96 studies) found “verbal praise produces an increase” and that the only negative effect appears when expected tangible rewards are given simply for doing a task — and that effect is “minimal.” The practice safe on both readings: praise and specific positive feedback, freely; expected tangible rewards for mere participation, sparingly. T1

Prevention pieces with thinner warrant than you'd expect

Use them if you like them. Just don't defend them with the evidence, because it isn't there.

Transitions — the Timely Transitions GameMechanism portable, evidence weak
What it is
Explicit timing, publicly posted feedback, and an interdependent group contingency with randomly selected transitions and criteria, so students can't predict which one is being scored.
The catch
It was developed and evaluated in a sixth-grade classroom — exactly your band — but on an A-B design only, no reversal. A later study added experimental control in grade 2. The mechanism is directly portable; the evidence is weak. T3
“Brisk pacing”n = 2, grade 1, reading, 1976
What the study did
An ABABAB comparing slow-rate presentation (delay between student response and next task) vs. fast-rate, holding praise constant via a fixed 90-second prompt to prevent confounding. Fast-rate was accompanied by lower off-task behavior for both subjects, plus more correct answering and participation. Genuinely clean design. T3
The catch
It is two low-achieving first-graders in beginning reading. “Brisk pacing” for 6th grade math is an extrapolation, not a finding.
Instructional matchContested, in your exact grade band
The classic claim
Off-task behavior spikes when work is at frustration level. Supported: time on task highest at the instructional level (93–97% known words), lower at frustration and lower at independent; and a math-level mismatch increased inappropriate behavior both with and without a reinforcement contingency on accuracy — you cannot incentivize your way out of a bad match. T3
What complicates it
The structural analysis with three middle school boys in honors math found attention, not difficulty, drove off-task behavior, with no consistent difficulty relationship. And a separate single case found disruption occurring after a student finished, fixed by assigning harder work.
The defensible synthesis
Mismatch in either direction generates behavior, with a strong independent effect of teacher proximity and attention during seatwork sitting on top of it. In a mixed-readiness room, the move that serves both is circulating constantly during independent work.
The most useful null result in the brief. The definitive modern Good Behavior Game trial: 77 English primary schools, 3,084 Year 3 pupils, two years, EEF security rating “high,” 0% school-level attrition. Reading g = 0.03, 95% CI −0.08 to 0.16, p = 0.30 — zero months' progress. No significant impact on concentration problems, disruptive behaviour, or pro-social behaviour; none on teacher stress, self-efficacy, or retention. One quarter of intervention schools stopped before the trial ended. The report's leading explanation for the null: teachers in both arms already reported frequent use of several core GBG components at baseline, so “programme differentiation may have been too limited.” Translation: a manualized game bolted onto a classroom that already has rules, monitoring, teams and rewards adds nothing. Improve the baseline instead. T1

Restorative practices — what the flagship RCT actually found

44 Pittsburgh public schools, 22/22 matched pairs, randomized in front of district staff, whole-school model, two years. About 8,940 students for suspensions, 4,702 for achievement. T1

ResultDetail
What improved Days of instruction lost to suspension fell 0.103 days against a 0.63 baseline — a 16% reduction, ES −0.060, p < .05. Teacher-rated classroom-conduct management rose ES 0.308, p < .05. Reductions concentrated among African American students, low-income students, and female students, narrowing suspension gaps.
What did not No suspension reduction in middle grades. None for male students, none for students with IEPs, none in arrests. Suspensions for violence or weapons: not significant.
What got worse Students rated their teachers' classroom management significantly lower — 4.1 points off a 51.9 NCE baseline, ES 0.209, p < .01 — along with four of six other Tripod subscales and In-Class Peer Support. RAND notes the pre-existing downward trend but concludes the relative downward trend became more severe rather than less severe during the initiative.
Achievement, precisely Combined math + reading, grades 3–8: ES −0.065, p < .10 only — the report itself calls this not significant at conventional levels. Math alone, grades 3–8: ES −0.068, p < .05. Subgroups on the combined measure: African American −0.150 (p<.01); grade 7–8 students −0.105 (p<.05); students with IEPs −0.088 (p<.10); grades 1–5 +0.020 (ns).
Two corrections Both are usually garbled. First: the negative African American estimate is not a student-race effect — RAND's supplementary analysis found the effect happened at the school level, negatively affecting both African American and white students at schools predominantly attended by African American students, and attributes it to implementation rather than student attributes. Second: RAND flags a puzzle that breaks the simple causal story — the suspension gain was in elementary and the achievement loss in middle grades, “suggesting that reduced suspensions are not the reason that PERC reduced achievement.”

The largest positive study is observational2 million students, no randomization
What it found
Practice data from 485 middle schools combined with outcomes for roughly 2 million middle school students, tracking student exposure rather than program adoption. Increasing exposure across the 5th-to-6th transition improved ELA and math and reduced suspension probability, with stronger effects for Black and Latino/a students. It also found exposure was inequitably distributed — lower in schools serving more Black and low-income students, and lower for those students within schools. T3
The design limit
The report's own argument is that prior RCTs evaluated programs that failed to shift practice. That is a coherent argument. It is also the case that exposure is not randomly assigned, and the institute is advocacy-adjacent.
Where the disagreement actually hingesNothing adjudicates it
Programs vs. practices
Every study that randomizes adoption returns null or negative in middle grades. Every study that measures exposure returns benefits. Advocates read the gap as implementation failure; skeptics read it as selection. No retrieved study randomizes exposure, so nothing adjudicates it — though the Maine trial's within-trial dose finding leans toward the advocates on mechanism while leaving the selection problem intact.
Whose report you believe
Pittsburgh is the sharpest case: teachers rated climate significantly better (ES +0.31) while students rated classroom management significantly worse (ES −0.21) — same schools, same years, same randomization. Any summary citing one and not the other isn't summarizing the study. Worth noting: the IES/REL Northwest (2025) practitioner handout — the only quasi-official federal product located — cites that RCT for its positive findings and mentions none of the negative ones.
And the equity result doesn't replicate in one direction
RAND found Black–white suspension gaps narrowed. A difference-in-differences study across 2008–2017 found a profound decline in suspension rates during the first 5 years while disciplinary outcomes for Black students were largely unchanged and racial disproportionality widened. LAUSD's interrupted time series found large suspension declines while Black/non-Black and SPED/non-SPED gaps still persist. Reducing referrals is not the same as reducing bias. T2
The call, and what would change itConversations yes, framework no
The call
Use restorative conversations — the one-on-one, ask-why, repair-the-thing move — as your step-4 response. That is the component the empathic-discipline work and the Maine dose analysis both point at, and it is the part a single teacher controls. Do not treat “restorative” as a substitute for an explicit, enforced behavioral floor in a 6th grade math class: the middle-grades RCT evidence is null-to-negative and the math estimate in the best trial was significantly negative.
What would flip it
A middle-grades RCT that randomizes teacher-level restorative conversation practice — not whole-school adoption — and reports math achievement. That study does not exist in what was retrievable.

Exclusion — the genuine tradeoff

Present both halves or the argument doesn't hold.

SideEvidence
The costs A suspension decreases the suspended student's math and reading achievement (~0.05 SD), robust to an instrumental-variables specification; peer spillover from suspensions for serious misconduct was “very small” and did not change peer absences. Attending a middle school one SD stricter raises dropout 1.7 pp (+15%), lowers 4-year college 2.4 pp (−11%), raises ever-arrested 3.2 pp (+17%), ever incarcerated 2.5 pp (+20%), adult arrests +25%, incarceration spells +29% — largest for male and minority students, identified off a sudden boundary change comparing neighbors split by a new line. T1
The bystander cost of heavy exclusion 16,897 students in 17 schools, grades 6–10, 38,062 observations, student- and school-fixed-effects growth curves with placebo regressions: below-mean school suspension levels had almost no effect on non-suspended students; above the mean, and especially more than 1 SD above, non-suspended students' achievement declined. In low-violence schools, predicted reading fell from about the 54th percentile at mean suspension to the 28th at +2 SD. The bystander damage is worst in the schools that are otherwise calm. T1
The other half Principals more likely to remove students genuinely do reduce reported rates of minor student misconduct — and generate more juvenile justice complaints, lower graduation rates for all students in their schools, and additional attendance and test-score declines for students who committed minor infractions. Separately, one additional disruptive classmate in a class of 25 throughout elementary school lowers grade 9–10 math and reading by 0.02 SD and earnings at 24–28 by 3 to 4 percent, explaining 5–6% of the rich-poor earnings gap. Read that carefully: it identifies peer composition, not disciplinary response — it establishes that disruption is costly, not that removal fixes it. Absorbing disruption is not a neutral choice either. T1/T2
Structure is not the enemy Using admissions lotteries in Massachusetts charters: “Schools that emphasize discipline and comportment raise achievement in math and ELA by 0.29σ and 0.11σ more than schools that do not.” Same paper, same design, on discipline outcomes: urban charter attendance increases suspensions by 0.7 days in middle school and more than a full day in high school. The authors caution these practices “are typically implemented as a package rather than in isolation,” and a review argues the opposite reading — little evidence connecting no-excuses disciplinary methods to test performance. T2
The finding that resolves it Philadelphia banned out-of-school suspension for two classroom-disorder infractions. Partitioning schools into full compliers and partial compliers: in full-compliance schools, peer achievement and attendance were unaffected post-reform. In partial-compliance schools, peer math achievement fell about 0.06 SD — large enough to reduce the likelihood those students would achieve proficiency in math — and peer absences rose 0.45 days (3%), driven by a 0.78-day (8%) rise in unexcused absences. Baseline confound the authors are careful about: full compliers were already 0.17 SD above district peers in math, partial compliers 0.03 SD below. T2
Pointing the same way In Chicago, where the share of high schoolers suspended fell from 24% to 16% over four years, reducing suspensions for severe infractions was associated with slightly higher test scores and better attendance; students at the highest estimated risk of suspension felt safer; students at the lowest estimated risk saw the largest test score gains; and there was no evidence overall achievement was harmed. T2/T3
The synthesis, and it is the most actionable line in the brief. The variable that determines whether reduced exclusion helps or hurts bystanders is consistency of implementation, not the philosophy. Half-following a discipline policy is worse for bystanders than either following it or not having it. Write down the two or three things that go to the office, and hold that line every single time.
Bias — the intervention and its honest attenuation“Cuts suspension in half” did not replicate
The original
Placebo-controlled randomized field experiment: 31 middle school math teachers across 5 California schools, 1,682 students. One 45-minute empathic-mindset module plus a 25-minute booster. Year-long official suspension records: 4.6% treatment vs 9.8% control, OR = 0.42, p = .001. Previously suspended students: 51.2% → 29.4%. (The paper's own abstract prints 9.6%/4.8% while its Results print 9.8%/4.6% — a small internal inconsistency.) T1
The preregistered replication
66 randomized middle school math teachers, 5,822 students, a far more diverse sample. The preregistered main effect did not reach significance in the clean randomized sample — 2.4 pp, 95% CI (−0.007 to 0.055), P = 0.130 two-tailed. The effect was entirely on in-school suspension (9.4% vs 14.2%, P = 0.001); out-of-school suspension showed nothing (P = 0.553). A teacher-level analysis the following year found nothing (P = 0.754). T1
What did replicate, and was preregistered
For Black and Hispanic students, suspension fell 20.8% vs 26.5% (P = 0.005), with no effect for other students — narrowing the disparity from 10.6 pp to 5.9 pp, a 45% reduction (interaction P = 0.011).
The defensible claim
Narrower than the headline and still valuable: an empathic framing changes whether you escalate a lower-stakes exclusion for a student of color. Any use of this literature should say the attenuation out loud.
Function-based thinkingBest-evidenced “understand the cause”
The numbers
Meta-analysis of 83 studies, 145 participants in school settings: overall median PND 88% — reducing problem behavior 80%, increasing appropriate skills 90%, maintenance 100%. By setting: general education classrooms 93%, with no significant differences across settings. By grade: elementary 88%, middle school 76%, high school 99%, no significant differences. Students with no diagnosed disability: 90%. A separate HLM meta-analysis of 69 studies found FBA-based interventions reduced problem behavior by an average of 70.5%. T1
The one process feature that mattered
Team decision-making during intervention planning was the only process feature predicting significantly larger effects.
The honest ceiling
WWC rates FBA-based interventions “potentially positive effects” — not “positive” — on school engagement (15 studies, 32 cases, 74% of single-case experiments positive, 0% negative) and on problem behavior (8 studies, 21 cases, 68% positive, 0% negative). The reason is that the base is entirely single-case. That is the ceiling of this literature, not a knock on the practice. T1
Relationship repair after conflictPilot promising, scale-up not
The pilot
20 teachers and 190 middle school students randomized to Establish–Maintain–Restore: relationships g = .61 [0.21, 1.02]; academically engaged time g = .81 [0.01, 1.63]; disruptive behavior g = 1.07 [0.01, 2.16]. Both behavior confidence intervals bottom out essentially at zero; the authors' own wording is “potential promise.” T2
The scale-up
Cluster RCT, 94 teachers, 417 students, 6 high schools: non-significant main effects, targeted benefits for students starting low and for several minoritized groups, and unexpected negative effects for high-performing and advantaged students. That is the honest current state. T2
Spillover — how you correct one kid teaches the roomOrigin claim is unverified
The famous version
Kounin & Gump's 1958 “ripple effect.” The citation is verified but the article text was not retrieved; the familiar claims about task-focused vs. harsh desists come from secondary summaries and are unverified. T3/T4
The modern, quantified version
Heavy exclusionary discipline damages non-suspended students' achievement, and damages it most in otherwise-calm schools. That is the spillover claim you can actually defend.

“Hit five praises for every correction”No threshold exists
What the evidence says
Verbatim from the review that examined it: “Although this rule has good face validity, there is a lack of empirical support for any specific ratio,” and the authors “argue against the use of any specific ratio.” Two large observational studies inside efficacy trials — 28 middle school classrooms, and 151 elementary classrooms across 19 schools and three states — found a straight line and no threshold at which behaviour dramatically improved. The study most often cited for 5:1 had N = 6 teachers. T2/T3
What to do instead
Raise the ratio continuously and measure the direction, not the level. Steal the mechanism from the N=6 study: a prompt on a variable-interval 5-minute schedule plus a self-monitoring chart. That is the whole intervention.
“Give them a choice to de-escalate”Front-end move only
What the evidence says
Meta-analysis of 34 studies, 89 students, 170 cases, K–12: choice significantly increased desired classroom behaviors (LRR = .186 [.042, .330]) but did not significantly decrease challenging behaviors (LRR = −.468 [−1.1, .163]). A CEC-criteria review of 26 experimental studies classified instructional choice as “insufficient evidence” — from the same group that rated praise, precorrection and active supervision as potentially evidence-based. T1/T2
What to do instead
Offer choice at the front of the task — which problem set, which representation, which order. Effects were larger for secondary students, so it earns its place. Just not as a de-escalation move.
“Just ignore it”Never validated standalone
What the evidence says
The founding study concluded: “Rules alone exerted little effect” on classroom behavior; ignoring inappropriate behavior and showing approval for appropriate behavior in combination were very effective; and “showing Approval for Appropriate Behaviors is probably the key.” The standard synthesis lists the practice under the name “planned ignoring plus contingent praise and/or instruction of classroom rules” — the qualifier is in the name of the practice — and notes its effectiveness “is directly related to the degree to which teacher attention is a positive reinforcer maintaining undesired behavior.” T2/T3
What to do instead
Check the function first. For peer-attention-maintained or escape-maintained behavior — which is most of 6th grade — ignoring does nothing or makes it worse.
Individual token economiesDoes not clear the WWC bar
What the evidence says
WWC single-subject standards plus meta-analysis across four effect-size types applied to 24 studies: “the extant research on token economies does not provide sufficient evidence to be deemed best-practice based on the WWC criteria.” T1
What to do instead
Group contingencies do clear that bar — and specifically “for addressing challenging behavior in general education classrooms.” If you want a points system, make it a group contingency.
High-probability request sequences“Simon Says,” with a token economy inside it
What the evidence says
A CEC review of 22 studies rated HPRS potentially evidence-based (2 met all quality indicators, 16 met the 80% criterion). Then a critical review took it apart. It tabulates the actual high-p requests used across that literature — “Touch your hair,” “Clap your hands,” “Stomp your feet,” “Give me a hug,” “Move your eyebrows” — and comments that “a cursory examination of these high-p requests could lead one to believe the game of ‘Simon Says’ was being used.” The one classroom-relevant version is “the exception rather than the rule.” And the recommended implementation embeds a token economy, so “a purportedly low-intensity technique has embedded in it an extremely high-intensity reinforcement delivery system.” T2/T3
What to do instead
Keep the usable half. High-probability task sequences — three or four problems the student can already do at the top of the set, same instruction — are trivially implementable and are the classroom-valid form of behavioral momentum. Do that; skip the request sequences.
Positive greetings at the doorWWC: Does Not Meet Evidence Standards
What the evidence says
There is an RCT with middle school teachers reporting improvements in academic engaged time and reduced disruption — and WWC reviewed that study and rated it “Does Not Meet Evidence Standards.” The circulating “20 percentage points” and “12 minutes per hour” figures do not appear in the retrieved abstract. T3
What to do instead
Keep doing it. It is free and humane. Just don't cite a number for it.
Trauma-informed practiceZero studies met inclusion
What the evidence says
A full Campbell systematic review — multi-database search, conference proceedings, reference harvesting, author emails, dual independent screening. The finding, verbatim: “Although we conducted a comprehensive search to find studies testing trauma-informed approaches in schools, no studies met the inclusion criteria.” And: “the evidence in this area is completely lacking.” T1
What to do instead
Treat it as what it is: a values framework and a staff-training market. Say so plainly if it comes up. The humane instincts underneath it are fine; the claim that it is an evidence-based practice is not.
Collaborative & Proactive Solutions in classroomsClinical, parent-delivered, and equivalent
What the evidence says
Two genuine RCTs exist, both clinical and parent-delivered. The 2004 trial (50 clinic-referred children with ODD, CPS vs. parent training): on the primary symptom measure Time × Group interactions were non-significant — CPS did not beat parent training; the between-group wins were on a global clinical impression rating. The largest trial (160 youth with ODD in Australian community clinics, independent diagnostic interviews, equivalence testing): both treatments equivalent, no between-group differences. The school evidence is two uncontrolled studies from one consulting group — a self-described pilot whose headline outcome was teacher self-reported stress, and a pre-post with no control. T2 / T3
What to do instead
CPS is a defensible clinical alternative to parent training. It is not a demonstrated classroom-management method. The ask-why conversation it is built on is already your step 4.
De-escalation training and the escalation cycleNo rigorous evaluation, in any field
What the evidence says
The definitive systematic review, from healthcare: “All National Health Service staff are trained in de-escalation but there is little to no evidence supporting training's effectiveness,” and the earlier review concluded “no model of training has demonstrated effectiveness in a sufficiently rigorous evaluation.” No controlled trial of de-escalation training for teachers was found at all. T1 for the review
The seven-phase cycle
Calm, trigger, agitation, acceleration, peak, de-escalation, recovery. It is a practitioner heuristic with no validation study that could be located; the only school evaluation found is a dissertation with 30 volunteer teachers, no student outcomes, no control group. Useful mental map; not evidence. T4
What to do instead
The premise — that lower-intensity precursor behaviors reliably precede severe behavior and share its function — does have support in the applied-behavior-analysis precursor literature. Use the map for noticing. Don't call it a trained skill.
Bolting on a behavior gameg = 0.03, p = 0.30
What the evidence says
77 English primary schools, 3,084 pupils, two years, EEF security “high,” 0% school-level attrition. Reading g = 0.03 [−0.08, 0.16], p = 0.30. No significant impact on concentration problems, disruptive behaviour, pro-social behaviour, teacher stress, self-efficacy, or retention. One quarter of intervention schools discontinued. Teachers' stated reasons: setup time wasn't worth it, the game didn't fit all content, and the rule forbidding teacher interaction during the game blocked them from helping students with additional needs. T1
What to do instead
The report's leading explanation for the null is that control teachers already used core components at baseline — “programme differentiation may have been too limited.” Improve your baseline. A manualized game added to a room that already has rules, monitoring, teams and rewards adds nothing.
What does not belong in the classroom OS. An individual token economy, a bolt-on behavior game, a de-escalation flowchart, a trauma-informed poster, or a choice menu offered to a kid who's already off-task. Each of those is either unsupported or has failed in a trial larger than anything you would ever run.

Open questions and weak spots — say these out loud if pressed

Nobody has tested the wording of a redirection It was searched for directly. No retrieved study manipulates private vs. public, question vs. command, or naming-the-behavior vs. naming-the-student and measures student compliance or escalation. O'Leary (1970) tests volume; Matheson & Shriver tests command clarity plus praise. Everything else in the “what to say” space is craft.
The micro-move literature is almost entirely single-case PND, Tau-U and LRR are not comparable to Cohen's d. An ES of 3.41 for group contingencies does not mean what an ES of 0.34 would mean in a group design.

1. Progressive name-on-the-board

Unsupported. Not proven harmful — the direct evidence is close to nonexistent, which is itself the finding. But both defining features work against it: it is public, and it accumulates.

Where it comes from Assertive Discipline (Canter). Render, Padilla & Krank audited it twice in 1989. The Educational Leadership piece summarized 12 studies and found “no indication that the program is any more effective than any other approach to reducing discipline problems in schools.” The Teachers College Record review called it “philosophically questionable and unsupported by well-designed research.” T3, summaries only
The reply is the tell Canter's published response is titled “But Practitioners Say It Works!” and argues implementation should rest on factors beyond research. Fair position. Not a research defense.
The recurring pattern Emmer & Aussiker (1990) reviewed four discipline packages: teachers liked Assertive Discipline and their own behavior was influenced by it, but “effects on student behavior are unclear.” Same shape as behavior-specific praise, where over 96% of studies measured whether teachers praised more rather than whether students changed. T3, summary only
Clip charts and color systems No peer-reviewed efficacy literature exists. Searched directly; what returns is teacher-marketplace products, blogs, and theses. The one empirical item locatable is a master's manuscript on student perceptions, reporting most students disliked the chart whether public or hidden — an acceptability signal, not an effect. T4, unpublished thesis
Public delivery is the part with evidence against it O'Leary et al. (1970), four-month reversal design, five classes. At baseline nearly all reprimands were audible to the class. Soft private reprimands reduced disruption; returning to loud produced “a consequent increase in disruptive behavior”; soft again reduced it. Name-on-the-board is the maximally public version, and it stays visible. T2
And 11–13 is the worst audience for it Somerville et al. (2013), Psychological Science: participants saw cues indicating a camera was off, warming up, or projecting to a peer. Merely believing a peer was watching induced self-conscious emotion that rose from childhood to adolescence and partially subsided into adulthood, with autonomic arousal uniquely heightened in adolescents. Lab study, not classroom. T2, summaries only
The “progressive” part specifically No evidence found either way. Nobody appears to have tested whether check-one-check-two-check-three beats a single consistent response. Absent, not negative.
One inference, labeled as inference. A running public tally is a device for making incident count salient — to you and to the room. Bias did not appear at incident one; it appeared at incident two, mediated by whether the teacher perceived “a pattern.” A board of accumulating marks is an instrument for manufacturing that perception and broadcasting it. That connects two separate findings; nobody has tested it.

What to do insteadKeep the tracking, drop the display. A tally on your clipboard or in the control panel preserves the consistency benefit and removes the public audience and the visible pattern-cue. If a student needs to know they are on their second redirect, that is a sentence delivered quietly at their desk — which is the version with the reversal-design evidence behind it.

2. Positive family contact

Family contact works, with real RCT evidence that includes 6th graders. But “positive” is not the active ingredient, and one trial found improvement-focused framing outperformed positive framing.

42%

Higher odds of homework completion

Kraft & Dougherty (2013), randomized field experiment, 6th and 9th graders, daily phone call home plus a written or text message. Same study: teacher redirections down 25%, class participation up roughly 15%. T1, abstract-level

41%

Reduction in failing to earn credit

Kraft & Rogers (2015), weekly one-sentence individualized messages to parents. Failure to earn credit fell 15.8% to 9.3%, driven primarily by preventing dropout rather than reducing failure or dismissal. T1, abstract-level

1 sentence

The runnable design

Daily calls worked in a summer program and will not survive five sections across a school year. One sentence per kid per week produced the 41% result. That is the transferable version.

The twist, and it cuts against the intuition. Kraft & Rogers ran three arms — messages with positive information, messages about how the student could improve, and a no-message control. The messages emphasizing what students could improve produced the largest effects, larger than the positive-information messages. So if you get one sentence a week, the evidence says spend it on the one thing to work on, not on the compliment.
Do not overcorrect Both trials ran in summer or credit-recovery settings with short horizons and high-stakes outcomes — not your February. And the positive arm was not harmful; it was just not strongest.
The relationship case for positive contact Stronger teacher-student relationships was one of Kraft & Dougherty's three named mechanisms, and relationship quality correlates .39 with engagement with the association larger in low-SES and secondary samples. A purely positive first contact is defensible as relationship investment even though the improvement-framed message is what moved work completion.
The honest gap No controlled study isolates positive contact from contact generally. Anyone citing research for “make positive phone calls home” as its own practice is probably citing Kraft & Dougherty, which was daily calls covering academics and behavior — not a positivity intervention.

3. Your send-out pattern

The pattern you described is the best-documented disparity in the field, and it is documented specifically for Black girls — the less-covered half of this literature.

GAO-24-106787
Sept 2024, federal civil rights data, SY 2017–18. Verified verbatim.		 Black girls were 15% of all girls in public schools and received almost half of all suspensions and expulsions. They “received harsher punishments than White girls even when the infractions that prompted disciplinary action were similar — for example… defiance, disrespect, and disruption.” In every state, Black girls are disciplined at higher rates; the gap grew larger with a disability. GAO's review named adultification and colorism as contributing mechanisms, and noted many teachers encourage girls “to uphold a quiet, docile form of femininity and ‘to act like ladies.’” Student-side: Black girls more frequently disagreed that teachers treat students with respect and more frequently felt school rules were unfair. T1
Which categories Defiance, disrespect, disruption — the subjective ones. Same finding as the bias-checkpoint tab, with gender added.
Girlhood Interrupted
Epstein, Blake & González (2017), Georgetown		 Adults view Black girls as less innocent and more adult-like than white peers, especially ages 5–14 — your students sit in the middle of that band. Adults judged Black girls as young as 5 to need less protection and nurturing. T2
The one to read first
Morris (2007), “Ladies” or “Loudies”?, Youth & Society		 Classroom ethnography in a middle school that was roughly 45% African-American — essentially your building. The Black girls performed well academically but educators persistently questioned their manners: disciplined for assertive behavior read as loud and overbearing, while the same descriptor was not applied to other groups of girls in the same school. Teachers encouraged a “docile form of femininity… the prescription to act like ‘ladies’” while viewing these girls' femininity as “coarse and overly assertive.” T3
The variance sits in the adults
Blake et al. (2022), JEBD		 Experimental design with 515 practicing educators: teacher's racial/ethnic background and discipline philosophy were the most salient predictors of discipline severity. Not the described student behavior. T2
Built for your exact question
Annamma et al. (2019), Urban Education		 Denver district administrative data, mixed methods, examining racial differences in office referral reasons and applying discourse analysis specifically to whether Black girls' referrals were for subjective or objective behaviors. T2/T3

So your read is well supportedThe literature's account is not “these students behave worse.” It is that adults read assertiveness in Black girls as defiance, and that the reading happens in the categories where judgment has the most room — which is exactly where a well-intentioned teacher would be affected without noticing.

What recollection can and can't tell you

Two things, and they cut both directions. Recollection is a biased sample of itself — the send-outs you remember are the emotionally charged, conflictual, or recent ones, and there is no reason that sample is representative. It could be overstating the pattern. It could be understating it, because the routine ones don't stick.

And a single teacher's annual send-out count is a small number. At fifteen send-outs a year, the difference between disproportionate and chance is a handful of cases. I looked for methodological guidance on interpreting individual-teacher referral counts and could not find any — a genuine gap, which means nobody can hand you a threshold.

The conclusion is not “don't worry about it.” It is count it instead of recalling it. The count tells you magnitude and location; the recollection only told you where to look.

Read this before you build anything. McIntosh, Smolkowski, Gion, Witherspoon, Bastable & Girvan (2020), Educational Researcher, “Awareness Is Not Enough” — double-blind RCT, 35 schools, half randomly assigned to monthly disciplinary equity reports and half to monthly general discipline reports. Verbatim result: “Schools receiving the equity reports had significantly increased rates of viewing equity reports but no meaningful change in disciplinary equity or equity goal setting.”

They looked at the data. Nothing changed. A referral log is necessary and it is not sufficient. If you build the log and stop there, the best available trial predicts a well-maintained spreadsheet and the same distribution. What moved it in the same group's later work was an actual intervention rather than data alone — and that citation is the least verified thing in this addendum. T1, abstract verbatim

The audit — six fields, ten seconds

Log every send-out and every near-miss. The near-misses are where the decision actually lives; a referral you almost wrote and didn't is the most informative row in the table, and it will never appear in official data.

FieldWhy
Date and class periodDisproportionality varied substantially by time of day in the 1,666-school analysis. Your vulnerable window is probably specific.
StudentSo you can see the repeat margin, which is where the national gap widens most.
Race and gender, togetherThe interaction is the entire point. Either one alone hides it.

Compare against your roster per section — not against the school. Fourth period is a different population from first, and a school-level comparison will hide or invent a pattern. Read the subjective column separately: if your objective referrals are proportionate and your subjective ones aren't, that is the specific actionable version, and it is what every large dataset predicts.

And pair it with a rule from day oneThe written send-out threshold, plus the incident-two question: am I responding to this incident, or to a story I've started telling about this kid? The log tells you whether the rule is working. The rule is what does the work.

Timeline: don't conclude from three weeks, don't wait for a year. A quarter of near-miss-inclusive data across five sections is enough to see whether there's a pattern worth acting on, and short enough that you can still act on it this year.
