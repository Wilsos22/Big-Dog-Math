Big Dog Math · Research Brief

Classroom Mission, Culture & Mindset

~24 Tier 1 ~12 Tier 2 ~10 Tier 3/4  ·  6th grade, Title 1, grade level to ~2 years behind  ·  Compiled 2026-07-29

The short version

Nobody has ever shown that a mission statement changes a student. The two empirical papers on school mission statements are a coding-methodology study with no student-outcome analysis at all, and a correlational extreme-groups comparison whose own authors write that "cause and effect conclusions are not warranted." Write the mission anyway — but write it as a constraint on your own decisions, not as a claim about children. Its job is to make you choose the same way on a Tuesday in February as you would in August.

What the mission should contain is very well evidenced, and your instincts are closer to right than most classroom philosophies. But "how to think, not what to think" is directionally correct and, as usually implemented, wrong — because the evidence says thinking is taught explicitly, inside math content, by a teacher modeling it aloud. Not discovered. Not taught as a separate strand. You are already holding the right thing; the upgrade is to model it out loud on your own confusion rather than post it on a wall.

Question 1  ·  Is there a defensible evidence base for a mission statement or values framework?
Not for mission statements. Yes for Self-Determination Theory.
Missions: no causal evidence, and probably never will be SDT: 51 interventions, 38 RCTs

Slate et al. compared 100 Texas elementary schools drawn from the extremes of the state rating scale. Academic Success appeared in 36 Exemplary vs. 18 Unacceptable missions (χ² = 13.04, p < .0001, ES .36); a Challenging environment in 19 vs. 4. A discriminant function classified 74% of schools correctly. In the low-performing schools, academic success appeared in 18% of missions and challenge in 4%. The authors also note that "experimental manipulation of the mission statements of schools is neither practical nor ethical" T3.

The defensible claim, stated precisely so it survives a skeptical department chair: high-performing schools name academic success and challenge in their missions far more often than low-performing schools, and nobody has shown the statement causes it.

Build the content on Self-Determination Theory — autonomy and competence support has by far the strongest evidence of any framework here. CASEL and character frameworks are taxonomies, not interventions, and the largest federal RCT of schoolwide character/SEL programming found nothing T1.

Question 2  ·  Is "teach how to think" more effective than explicit instruction?
False dichotomy — and the resolution is specific: explicitly teach domain-specific thinking.
Richest evidence in the brief

Unassisted discovery loses to explicit instruction at every age — 580 comparisons, N = 25,986, d = −0.38 [−.44, −.31] — and adolescents show the largest gap, d = −0.53 T1. But enhanced discovery (generate, explain, get feedback) wins: d = +0.30. The real variable is the amount and specificity of guidance, not inquiry-or-not: guidance within inquiry produced d = 0.50 on learning outcomes T1.

Sixth grade is the flip point for problem-first. Across 53 productive-failure studies: overall g = 0.36, but 2nd–5th grade g = −0.09, 6th–10th grade g = 0.50, math g = 0.48 — and domain-general skills g = −0.17 T1. The counterweight is real: two randomized Year 5 experiments found instruction-first won, and won on both similar and transfer problems once element interactivity was high.

Generic thinking skills do not transfer. In the classic experiment, 19% of subjects spontaneously transferred a solution to a structurally identical problem; 35% did when told the two were similar T3. Content-specific critical thinking runs g = 0.57 against disposition at g = 0.23.

Question 3  ·  Is growth mindset a proven instructional framework?
No. And the version teachers actually implement is the version that has repeatedly failed.
Contested at best; teacher-delivered version failed three times

The best case for it: 12,490 ninth-graders individually randomized within school, pre-registered, two online sessions under an hour, teachers blind to condition. The lower-achieving subgroup gained +0.10 grade points [0.04, 0.16], SMD = 0.11; poor-performance rate fell 46% → 41% T1.

The case against: 63 studies, N = 97,672, d̄ = 0.05 — "nonsignificant after correcting for potential publication bias." Highest-quality subset only: d̄ = 0.02, n.s. No theoretically meaningful moderator was significant — not challenge, not SES, not dosage, not classroom integration T1. The fairest planning number, from the friendliest meta-analysis under its best conditions, is d = 0.14 [.06, .22] — with a 95% prediction interval of −0.08 to 0.35.

The finding that decides it for a classroom teacher. Every trial that worked used a short, standardized, self-administered module delivered around the teacher. The teacher-delivered version — teacher PD plus teachers running weekly lessons, which is the version schools buy — got ES = −0.01 in maths across 101 schools and 5,018 pupils, EEF security rating high T1. Yeager and Dweck concede it in print: "So far, teacher-focused growth mindset interventions have not worked."

Question 4  ·  What culture and norms produce the best outcomes?
Errors safe, relationships strong, expectations high and acted on, real choices, stated reasons.
Strong on the practices; almost all the culture measures are correlational

Errors safe. 1,641 students in 69 mathematics classrooms, five measurement points across fifth and sixth grade. Students' reactions to errors developed negatively over the two years — willingness to engage adaptively with mistakes decays by default across grades 5–6 — and that decline varied substantially between classrooms and systematically tracked perceived error climate T3.

Relationships. Positive relationship × engagement r = .39; × achievement r = .16. Effects are larger in higher grades and larger in samples with more low-SES students T3. Method artifact worth knowing: positive relationships correlate r = .24 with grades but only r = .07 with test scores — much of the "relationships raise achievement" story lives in teacher-assigned grades. Convert rapport into participation; rapport alone isn't the mechanism.

Expectations. 19 teacher-expectation interventions: g = 0.38 on expectations, g = 0.30 on achievement. Intervention type did not affect effectiveness; teacher support for the intervention did T1. Cite this meta-analysis, never Pygmalion.

Choices and reasons. Autonomy support across 51 interventions including 38 RCTs; in experimental designs autonomy g = 1.14, competence g = 0.48 T1. Utility-value effects concentrate in students with low success expectations — exactly your kids.

What is deliberately not on that list. Belonging as a stated value. It's a good value and worth keeping — teacher support is the single strongest predictor of school belonging, r = .46 [.37, .54], ahead of personal characteristics (.44), parent support (.33), and peer support (.32). But relatedness was the one SDT leg that came back non-significant in the 2024 meta-analysis (g = 0.44, p > .05), so don't build the defensible argument on it. Also absent by design: growth mindset language and grit.
What to actually do with this. Write the mission. Justify it instrumentally, not empirically: it is a decision constraint that keeps your February choices consistent with your August ones, and consistency of enactment is the variable that keeps showing up as the moderator — teacher support rather than intervention type; implementation fidelity rather than assignment; productive-failure design fidelity rather than the label. Do not tell anyone the research says a mission statement raises achievement. It doesn't say that.
Read this first. Several of the highest-status ideas in classroom culture are much weaker than their reputation, and two of them are on your wall. The pattern is consistent: the psychological lever often has small, conditional evidence, and the classroom program built on it has none, or has failed outright. They are not the same intervention — and the version schools adopt is almost always the failed one.
"We Celebrate Effort"This is the exact misconception Dweck names, and it has an empirical sting
The popular version
Praise effort. Put it on the wall. Effort is the thing we honor.
What Dweck herself wrote
"Perhaps the most common misconception is simply equating the growth mindset with effort," and praise is "too often" given to students "who are putting forth effort, but not learning." She writes that she fears the concepts "will be used to perpetuate" the failed self-esteem movement T4.
The finding that should stop you
She cites Kathy Liu Sun: math teachers who endorsed growth mindset and said the words in class but didn't follow through in practice had students who endorsed more of a fixed mindset about math ability. Saying it without enacting it was not neutral — it ran backwards.
The precise fix
Celebrate the effort that produced a visible change in the work — a revision, a second attempt, a strategy switch. That is a claim about evidence, which is already your vocabulary: "learning must leave evidence," "revise and try again." Effort praise detached from a change in the work is the failure mode with data behind it.
Growth mindset as something you teachES = −0.01 in maths across 101 schools and 5,018 pupils
Three independent failures of the teacher-delivered version
Foliano et al. (2019), EEF, security rating high — 101 schools, 5,018 Year 6 pupils; teacher PD then 8 weekly teacher-delivered sessions plus embedded growth-mindset feedback. KS2 maths ES = −0.01 [−0.04, 0.01]. Reading −0.00. GPS −0.00. FSM subgroup also zero. All four non-cognitive outcomes n.s. Process evaluation found it was delivered as intended and well received; teachers perceived increased enthusiasm and perseverance T1.

Rienzo et al. (2015), EEF — teacher-PD arm, 30 schools, 1,505 pupils: no additional progress in maths, less progress in English than control T1.

Ganimian (2020) — 202 Argentine public secondary schools randomized, fidelity verified in 90% of treatment schools: "no evidence" of effects on any outcome, and "I rule out small effects." Hints of negative effects for female students, low-income students, and grade-repeaters T1.
The obvious rebuttal, killed
"The null trials just didn't do enough of it" is not supported. Number of sessions was non-significant in the meta-analysis. The 8-week embedded version got zero; the two-session, under-an-hour version got 0.11. Dosage runs the wrong way.
What survives
Your growth mindset matters as context. Across 9,167 student records matched to 223 math teachers, students "could not independently implement growth mindsets in any classroom culture" — effects required teachers who themselves held them T2. Hold it and enact it in task design and feedback. Do not run it as curriculum, PD, or wall art.
"How to think, not what to think," taken literallyThe two things the shorthand licenses are the two things the literature rejects hardest
What the slogan usually licenses
Unguided discovery, and generic thinking instruction. Unassisted discovery: d = −0.38 against explicit instruction, worst for adolescents at −0.53 (worked examples −0.63, feedback −0.46, direct teaching −0.29). Domain-general skills in the productive-failure moderator table: g = −0.17, favoring instruction-first T1.
Transfer barely happens
"Critical thinking is not a set of skills that can be deployed at any time, in any context. It is a type of thought… very much dependent on domain knowledge and practice." 19% spontaneous transfer to a structurally identical problem, 35% with an explicit hint. When transfer has been tested in curricular thinking-skills programs, "positive results have been absent or modest and quick to fade" T3.
EEF is explicit about the format
Metacognitive strategies "should be taught and applied to the usual curriculum content, rather than taught discretely through 'thinking skills' lessons" T1. And Kirschner et al.'s line worth stealing: "it may be a fundamental error to assume that the pedagogic content of the learning experience is identical to the methods and processes (i.e., the epistemology) of the discipline being studied" T3. Doing math like a mathematician is not the same thing as learning math efficiently.
The precise version of your slogan
Think out loud in front of them, on real math, and make them practice the same questions you asked yourself. Content-specific critical thinking g = 0.57 vs. disposition g = 0.23 — the domain-embedded half is worth more than twice the generic half.
Grit — and "We Don't Give Up"Meta-analytically deflated across 66,807 people
What the meta-analysis found
584 effect sizes, 88 independent samples, 66,807 individuals: "the higher order structure of grit is not confirmed"; it is "only moderately correlated with performance and retention" and "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness"; "interventions designed to enhance grit may only have weak effects" T2.
The one facet that survived
Perseverance of effort. Consistency of interest did not.
What this means for the wall
"We Don't Give Up" is fine as a norm, and it is not the same claim as grit-the-construct. But if anyone asks for the research, the defensible target is perseverance of effort on a hard task — not grit as a brand, and never as a tracked trait.
"SEL works" / character education packages84 schools, 3 years, seven programs: no differences on any student outcome
The big null nobody cites
The Social and Character Development Research Consortium (IES/NCER with Mathematica and CDC): seven universal schoolwide social/character programs, each "coherent… based on a theory of action," schools randomized at 7 sites, one cohort followed 3rd through 5th grade, 84 schools. Finding: "there were no differences in students' social and emotional competence, behaviors, academic performance, or perceptions of school climate" between program and control schools T1.
And the WWC screen
93 studies of 41 character programs went to WWC; 18 studies of 13 programs met standards, 7 without reservations. Most programs had only one or two small qualifying studies T1.
The counterweight, which is also real
Durlak et al.: 213 programs, 270,034 students, an 11-percentile-point gain in achievement T2. Taylor et al.: 82 interventions, 97,406 students, mean age 11.09, mean 41.1% low-SES, follow-ups from 6 months to 18 years; benefits "similar regardless of students' race, socioeconomic background, or school location" T2. Cipriano et al.: 424 studies, 575,361 students — improvements across the board, but with significant heterogeneity moderating outcomes T2.
How both are true
The meta-analyses evaluate specific curricula run well. SACD evaluated schoolwide values programming as a bundle against a counterfactual already doing some of it. The value is in specific, well-implemented practices — not in adopting a framework. Any brief that says "SEL works" without citing SACD is not a defensible brief.
The math-discourse frameworks everyone endorsesThe most popular ones are the least tested
Untested as named packages
NCTM's eight Mathematics Teaching Practices; Stein/Smith's Five Practices (explicitly offered to make discussion manageable, not as an efficacy claim); Michaels/O'Connor/Resnick's Accountable Talk (the foundational paper is philosophical). None has been tested as a named package in any trial retrieved T4.
The only RCT-grade evidence in this space
Dialogic teaching: cluster RCT, 76 schools, 4,985 Year 5 pupils, ~35% EverFSM, 20-week intervention. +2 months English and science, +1 month maths; FSM pupils +2 months in all three. And a 2019 addendum found no impact on KS2 results a year later T1. Cost £52/pupil/year; most teachers said two terms wasn't long enough to embed it. Culture is not a thing you install; it's a thing you run.
Popular moves with no number behind them
Positive Greetings at the Door: an RCT exists and WWC rated it "Does Not Meet Evidence Standards"; the circulated "20 percentage points" and "12 minutes per hour" figures do not appear in the retrieved abstract. 2×10: no peer-reviewed controlled trial found at all; the "85% reduction in negative behaviors" figure traces to a practitioner book and an unpublished master's thesis with four students. Railside magnitudes are contested in both directions — don't cite them either way. The Chapin/O'Connor "Project Challenge" figures that circulate widely could not be traced to any primary source.
How to hold these
Use them. Don't cite them as evidence. That distinction is the whole point — a practice can be worth doing and still have no trial behind it, and you should know which is which before you defend it to anyone.
PBIS and behavior systemsReduces exclusions; doesn't move much else
What it does
Significant large effect for school suspension, g = −.86, and no treatment effect for office discipline referrals T1/T2. A separate 37-school RCT found significant reductions in both suspensions and ODRs T1, effect sizes not retrieved.
What it doesn't do
Korpershoek's classroom-management meta-analysis put SWPBS at g = 0.03, not significant, and lowest of any named program on social-emotional outcomes.
The long tail, honestly caveated
The Good Behavior Game Baltimore RCT (19 schools, 42 first-grade classrooms, n = 1,084 and 1,117) showed no main effects at the 6-year follow-up but significant impacts at 14 years on lifetime alcohol abuse/dependence and antisocial behavior. Blueprints states plainly that only one of ten reviewed studies meets their standards and it was developer-conducted, and that benefits concentrate in males on the high-aggression trajectory T1.
Co-constructed norms and "classroom organization" as validated leversFidelity predicts. Adoption doesn't.
Responsive Classroom
24 schools randomized, 2,904 children, end of 2nd through 5th grade. Assignment to the program produced no achievement advantage. But use of the practices predicted achievement (ES 0.26 math, 0.30 reading; 0.89 for math among students with low initial math achievement). WWC rated this study "Does Not Meet Evidence Standards" T2. That is a fidelity finding, not a causal one.
CLASS
The one meta-analysis retrieved (35 studies, preschool/ECEC) found only two significant associations out of fourteen — Classroom Organization × Pencil Tapping pooled r = .06 — with most studies at moderate-to-high risk of bias T3.
What classroom management actually buys
54 controlled studies, overall g = 0.22 (academic .17, behavior .24, social-emotional .21, motivation .08, n.s.); trim-and-fill adjusted to 0.17. The 2025 update, 76 studies: g = .23, no publication bias detected. Three things to act on: the social-emotional-development component was the only one that meaningfully raised effects (.24 with vs. .15 without; on SEL outcomes .25 vs. .04); low-SES g = .20 vs. mid/high .21 — no significant difference, so this works the same in Title 1; and duration made no difference. Also worth swallowing: students report less improvement than adults do (student-rated .16 vs. observer .30) T1/T2.

What to optimize for, if you care about growth beyond your class

Teacher effects on test scores and teacher effects on behavior correlate only 0.22. They are nearly different skills — "less than 5 percent of the variation in teacher effects on the behavior index is associated with teacher effects" on test scores. From 573,963 North Carolina 9th graders, 872 schools, 5,195 English and 6,854 math teachers T2.

The behavior dimension is worth roughly 12:11 SD of teacher test-score value-added raises graduation +0.118 pp; 1 SD of behavior value-added raises it +1.46 pp. Adding behavior VA raises explained variance of teacher effects by 305% for graduation, 326% for dropout, 228% for SAT-taking, 182% for four-year-college intentions, 607% for high-school GPA. The lifetime-earnings translation grafts a correlational earnings gap onto a causal graduation effect, and the author says so — don't use that number in a meeting.
It replicates in a district that looks like yoursChicago Public Schools, 133 high schools, 157,630 students, 86% economically disadvantaged, with value-added computed on a short student self-report survey. Per 1 SD of school VA on four-year college-going: test-score 1.83 pp, social 2.67 pp, hard-work 3.13 pp. Socio-emotional VA raises explained variance by over 180% relative to test-score VA alone; adding test-score VA on top of SED VA adds only 18%. T2
In a randomized experiment, the test-score part is the part that fadesProject STAR: 11,571 Tennessee students randomly assigned to classrooms, linked to tax records into adulthood. "The effects of class quality fade out on test scores in later grades, but gains in noncognitive measures persist." T1
The levers you personally controlUpper-elementary teachers have large effects on students' self-efficacy in math and happiness and behavior in class, predicted by the teaching practices most proximal to them — emotional support and classroom organization. Same paper: "teachers who are effective at improving test scores often are not equally effective at improving students' attitudes and behaviors." Teachers also have persistent, "arguably causal" effects on absences, and the teachers who improve test scores are not necessarily the ones who improve attendance. T2/T3
The fadeout testThe "trifecta": a skill worth investing in must be malleable, fundamental, and would not have developed eventually without the intervention. If your students would have picked it up by 8th grade anyway, expect the gain to fade. The other durable channel is foot-in-the-door — for you, getting a kid into the next math course rather than the remediation track. Note that the growth-mindset study's advanced-math-enrollment result (33% → 36%) is exactly this channel. T4
So the metrics the mission should commit you to: attendance, work completion, on-time progression to the next course, math self-efficacy, and whether kids feel connected. They are (a) causally within your reach through emotional support and classroom organization, (b) nearly uncorrelated with your test-score effect, so nobody else is covering them, and (c) the part of your effect that still exists in ten years.
Discount every headline number above. Effects shrink on outcome measures the researchers didn't write: the overall strategy-instruction effect drops from g = .78 on self-developed measures to .45 on intervention-independent ones. Math is the exception — it rose, .61 → .84, which is genuinely reassuring but doesn't excuse the rest. Effects also came out larger when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training. And the famous $250,000 per classroom figure is under live peer-reviewed dispute: a North Carolina replication found "the quasi-experiment is invalid, as teacher switching is correlated with changes in student preparedness," with bias "perhaps 10–35 percent as large, in variance terms, as teachers' causal effects." If you use the number, use the caveat.

Your wall language, audited against the evidence

On the wall nowVerdictWhat the evidence says, and the change
"Being confused is step one" Keep This is the best-supported line you have. 1,641 students in 69 mathematics classrooms, five measurement points across fifth and sixth grade: reactions to errors developed negatively over the two years, and the decline varied substantially between classrooms and systematically tracked perceived error climate T3. Math-specific, exactly your grade band, and it establishes that willingness to share a wrong answer is not stable — it erodes unless the room actively counteracts it. It's correlational, so state it as "classrooms differ and the difference tracks error climate," not "positive error climate causes retention of error tolerance."
"Step two: what do you know. Step three: try something." Move it This is a metacognitive self-questioning protocol, and teacher-modeled self-questioning is one of only two Strong-rated recommendations in the federal grades 4–8 math problem-solving guide — five studies found performance improved when teachers modeled the process and students then practiced it T1. Model it; don't post it. The upgrade is narrating your own confusion aloud on a real problem, at least weekly.
"We Think" / how to think, not what to think Sharpen Directionally right, dangerous in shorthand. Generic thinking instruction does not transfer (19% → 35% with a hint); EEF says metacognition must be taught inside curriculum content, "rather than taught discretely through 'thinking skills' lessons"; content-specific critical thinking g = 0.57 vs. disposition g = 0.23 T1/T3. Never run a thinking-skills strand or unit.
"We Try" / "try one move" Keep, with a condition Sixth grade is exactly where problem-solving-before-instruction flips positive: 6th–10th grade g = 0.50, math g = 0.48. But domain-general skills g = −0.17, 2nd–5th g = −0.09, and unassisted discovery loses at d = −0.38 T1. "Try something" has to mean try inside a scaffolded task with explicit consolidating instruction after — not try in the dark.
"We Don't Give Up" Sharpen Fine as a norm. Do not brand it as grit or track it: 584 effect sizes, 66,807 individuals, "the higher order structure of grit is not confirmed," "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness," "interventions designed to enhance grit may only have weak effects" T2. The defensible target is perseverance of effort on hard tasks — the one facet that survived.
"We Help Each Other" / "help a thinker" Keep, add a move Cooperative goal structures beat competitive and individualistic ones across 148 studies of early adolescents T1/T2. But group work does not distribute participation on its own — the deliberate status treatment (assigning competence, multiple-ability orientation) is what raised low-status participation, with no drop for high-status students T3. Name a specific competence out loud before the group starts.
"We Celebrate Effort" Rewrite This is the one line the evidence pushes back on directly. Dweck: "perhaps the most common misconception is simply equating the growth mindset with effort," and praise is "too often" given to students "who are putting forth effort, but not learning." She cites Kathy Liu Sun's finding that math teachers who endorsed growth mindset and said the words but didn't follow through in practice had students who endorsed more of a fixed mindset about math ability T4. Tie the celebration to a visible change in the work — which is already your vocabulary.
"Learning must leave evidence" / "make thinking visible" / "check the evidence" Keep Straight metacognition applied to curriculum content, which is the +8-months strand — and the sentence to tape to your desk: "Some studies suggest disadvantaged pupils are less likely to use metacognitive and self-regulation strategies unless explicitly taught to do so" T1. In a Title 1 room this is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost thing available.
"Use a resource" / "ask for help" Keep Autonomy support is not less structure; it is structure with rationale and voice. 51 interventions, 38 RCTs; autonomy g = 1.14, competence g = 0.48 T1. Guidance within inquiry runs d = 0.50 on learning outcomes, with a least→most-specific taxonomy — process constraints → status overviews → prompts → heuristics → scaffolds → explanations — that is a better planning tool than the word "inquiry."
"Revise and try again" Keep, upgrade Attach the wise-feedback sentence and it becomes the best-evidenced move you can make for the students least likely to trust the comment: 87% vs. 62% revised among low-trust students, and it halted a two-year decline in trust T2.
On Carnegie. Carnegie Learning frames productive struggle. It does not frame confusion as valuable — the confusion-is-step-one framing is yours, and it should never be attributed to Carnegie. The nearest research analogue is the productive-failure literature, and that carries two conditions the label alone doesn't: fidelity to the design features was a significant predictor overall (β = 0.0065, p < .001) and specifically for 6th–10th graders (β = 0.0190, p < .0001), and interventions spanning a few days had far higher fidelity than a-few-hours ones (mean 74.45 vs. 42.01). The authors warn that "cramming too many design features within a short amount of time may not be optimal."

The single best-evidenced move available for your exact population. A 2025 direct replication exists and was not read — read it before leaning hard on this.
  2. Add a weekly think-aloud slot to the warm-upOnce a week you work the third current question aloud before students do — narrating being stuck, naming what you know, choosing something to try, and being wrong at least sometimes. That is the Strong-rated move, and it is the only thing in this brief that directly counteracts the documented grade-5-to-6 decline in error tolerance.
  3. Stamp the wise-feedback sentence on every returned piece of graded workVerbatim, every time: "I'm giving you these comments because I have high standards and I know you can meet them." One sentence, not a program, and the effect was largest for exactly the students who trust school least.
  4. Open every unit with a use — and once per unit make students write the connection themselvesBeing told why it matters is weaker than generating it. Utility-value effects concentrate in students with low success expectations. Render the "why this matters" line on the projector surface with the current task, not as static wall text.
  5. Give real choices inside tasks you designedChoice of problem-set difficulty, choice of representation, choice of who to work with — inside a task you built and a standard you set.
  6. Decide problem-first vs. teach-first per lesson, on element interactivityGive the problem first only when the content is low-to-moderate element interactivity, only with real scaffolds inside the attempt, and always with explicit consolidating instruction after. For anything high-element-interactivity, or for your students furthest behind, teach first — WWC 2021's six Strong recommendations are unambiguous about that population.
  7. Use the two transfer techniques you can run tomorrowProblem comparison: present two structurally identical, superficially different solved problems side by side. Subgoal labeling: label each substep with the goal it serves, so a small variation doesn't strand the student.
  8. Assign competence out loud before group work startsNaming a specific student's specific strength raised low-status participation without lowering anyone else's. Group work alone does not do this.
  9. Build the two control-panel additionsA per-class work-completion and attendance strip, and a once-a-quarter three-item student self-report (connectedness, math self-efficacy, effort) modeled on the Chicago socio-emotional survey. These are the outcomes carrying the long-run signal, and no state test reports them back to you.
What to stop doing, or never start. A "thinking skills" strand or unit — EEF is explicit against it. Growth mindset posters, praise-the-effort, and mindset language without changed practice — that is the version that ran backwards. Grit as a brand or a tracked trait. Buying a character-education or SEL package on the strength of "SEL works." And in the classroom OS specifically: no mindset module, no grit tracker, no character-strengths rubric. All of these are either unsupported or have failed in trials larger than anything you would ever run.
And the instrumental case for writing any of this down. Consistency of enactment is the moderator that keeps recurring — teacher support for the intervention rather than intervention type; implementation fidelity rather than assignment; productive-failure design fidelity rather than the label. A mission statement's defensible function is to make you consistent. Say that out loud when someone asks for the research.
