Area & Volume — Research Dashboard

6th grade · Title 1 · grade level to ~2 years behind · compiled 2026‑07‑29

6.G.A.1 area of polygons 6.G.A.2 volume, fractional edges 6.G.A.4 nets & surface area Supporting cluster — not major work
The formulas are not the content.

The content is whether a kid can see structure — rows and columns in a rectangle, layers of cubes in a prism. A formula is just a compressed record of that structure. Battista and Clements documented across two decades that most students never impose it on their own, and Huang & Wu caught fifth graders using V = l×w×h correctly with no idea what it meant. A right answer is not evidence of understanding here.

The one thing most likely to go wrong You hand out cubes, students count happily, and counting replaces structuring instead of leading to it. Outhred & Mitchelmore found materials can actively obscure the row-and-column structure and reinforce one-by-one counting over multiplicative thinking. The manipulative is not the intervention. The narration and the fade are.

Where to spend the time

Spatial structuring

Rows × columns in 2‑D, layers of cubes in 3‑D. Highest misconception density and the widest prerequisite fan-out — it's what makes every formula in the unit mean anything. The extra days go here.

Base × perpendicular height

Built by cutting and rearranging, not stated. 91% of 11–12 year olds in one study only ever drew horizontal bases; 39% always marked height as a vertical line. Every one of them grabs the slant side.

Fraction multiplication

The silent gate on 6.G.A.2. A kid can fully understand packing a prism with ½‑inch cubes and still fail on the arithmetic. This double-dips into 6.NS — major work — so time here pays twice.

What gets compressed: nets and surface area

Surface area is genuinely just "add up the areas of faces you already know how to find." The mental folding skill is separate — Wright & Smith found it doesn't transfer between solids without explicit prompting, and Knight & Wright found success rates swinging from 100% to 26% depending on which net you pick. Physically unfold real cereal boxes, teach surface area as an application of 6.G.A.1, and move on. Defensible partly because 6.G.A is a supporting cluster — confirmed in three independent standards documents.

Watch out for

Your quiz may hide the difference In the one quasi-experiment that isolated it, structure-first beat formula-first on conceptual understanding (F(1,50)=6.90, p<.01, η²=.12) but showed no significant advantage on numerical calculation (p=.08). If you only measure computation, you will conclude the structure work didn't matter. Put "explain / draw / find the missing dimension" items on the assessment or you're flying blind.
CRA has never been tested on this content The most current CRA meta-analysis (Ebner et al. 2025, 30 studies) contains zero geometry studies — every coded operation is arithmetic or algebra. The IES practice guide's own evidence table marks "no qualifying studies" in the Geometry column for all six of its recommendations. The progression on the Sequence & fade tab keeps the shape but rebuilds the justification on the spatial-structuring literature, which is about this topic.
The cheapest diagnostic you have Show a rectangle partly covered by unit squares and ask students to finish the drawing. A student who draws individual squares one at a time has not structured the array — even when the final count is right. A student who draws two perpendicular sets of lines has. Outhred & Mitchelmore found correct counts almost never occur without the lines representation. Five seconds, no prep, and it's also your fade trigger.

Each one gets four parts: the wrong answer you'll actually see, the rule that used to be true, the task that makes the wrong rule produce a visibly absurd answer, and what to do when it shows up. Confusion is step one — don't rescue it.

Height is the slanted sidePlan for it

Looks like
Triangle, base 7, perpendicular height 6, slanted third side 8 → student writes 7 × 8 ÷ 2 = 28. True area is 21. Or: given an obtuse triangle, they draw a "height" that isn't perpendicular — or refuse to mark one at all.
Used to be true
Two rules collide. "Use all the numbers you're given" is a reliable elementary heuristic. And every triangle they've ever seen sat on a horizontal base with a vertical height inside it — so "vertical" and "height" fused before anyone said otherwise.
Evidence
Cutugno & Spagnolo, n=77, ages 11–12: 91% drew triangles with a horizontal base "as if it were a heavy body," 39% always marked height as a strictly vertical line, 56% always marked it inside the figure T2 · Barton diagnostic data, 471 responses: 8% chose the slant-side answer T4
Surface it
An obtuse triangle drawn on a slanted base, with the height's foot falling outside the base segment. There's no vertical-and-inside line to grab, so the broken rule announces itself instead of quietly producing a plausible wrong number. Include the slant length as given data so "use every number" also gets tested.
When it shows
Don't re-explain the formula — the formula was never the problem. What do you know? (area came from a rectangle). Try something (hand them the parallelogram cut-out and ask them to make the rectangle). The height is whichever length made the rectangle work.

Volume = the cubes you can seePlan for it

Looks like
A 4×5×2 prism drawn in isometric view → student counts the three visible faces: 8 + 20 + 10 = 38. True volume is 40 — and the near-miss is what makes it dangerous.
Used to be true
A drawing shows only what it shows. Without a 3‑D model built from actual layer structure, a picture of a prism is a 2‑D arrangement of squares, and counting the squares you can see is a rational response to that object. Students also don't recognize that edge cubes belong to two faces at once.
Evidence
Ben-Chaim, Lappan & Houang (1985) categorized four error types — visible faces, visible faces doubled, visible cubes, visible cubes doubled — still observed in classrooms in 2018 T3 · Battista & Clements (1996) attribute it to inadequate spatial structuring and failure to recognize shared cubes T2
Surface it
A drawn 5×5×5 cube. Visible-face counting gives 75; the answer is 125. Fifty missing cubes is not a rounding error and cannot be talked around. Then build it: predict, fill, count.
When it shows
Don't correct the count — have them build it and ask what's inside. Extend with the scaled-box task: build a box, scale all three dimensions ×3, predict how many small boxes fit inside. Most students predict 3 or 9. It's 27.

Volume computed as surface areaPlan for it

Looks like
Asked for the volume of a prism with 3×3 and 3×4 faces, student writes 2×9 + 4×12 = 66 and reports 66 as the volume. Running the other way: asked for surface area, computes l×w×h.
Used to be true
Both are taught close together, both "use all the face dimensions." If the mental model of a prism is still an uncoordinated set of faces rather than a filled region, the two operations aren't distinguishable — they're both "do something with the numbers on the faces."
Evidence
Chiphambo & Mtsi (2021), n=18 Grade 8: 44% used the volume formula when asked for surface area; 22% just added the edge dimensions; 83% couldn't state how many dimensions a 3‑D solid has T2 · Tan Sisman & Aksu (2016), n=445 sixth graders, documents "using the volume formula for surface area" and "believing a box has more than one surface area" T2
Surface it
A 1×1×20 cm prism, asking for both quantities side by side. Volume is 20 cm³; surface area is 82 cm². Students expect volume to be the big number and it isn't. The magnitude reversal makes the swap impossible to hide.
When it shows
Physical objects, immediately. Fill it (volume) vs. wrap it (surface area). Keep the two colour-coded all unit — students conflate them by default, not by exception.

The arc

Each step answers: what does this make possible that wasn't possible before it?

  1. Structure the rectangleMakes area multiplicative rather than a count. Nothing after this works without it.
  2. Conserve area under rearrangementMakes cut-and-rearrange arguments legitimate in students' eyes. Skip it and step 3 is a magic trick.
  3. Parallelogram by decompose-and-rearrangeMakes the perpendicular height necessary. Cut the triangle off one end, slide it to the other — the height you used is visibly the one that made the rectangle work. The slant side does nothing.
  4. Triangle as half a parallelogramMakes the ½ a consequence of a picture rather than a symbol. That's what keeps it from being dropped under load.
  5. Composite polygons with no direct formulaMakes decomposition necessary. Ekawati et al. found students used cut-rotate-paste reasoning only when no formula existed — assign only triangles and parallelograms and they never practice the actual standard.
  6. Volume by layers, before V = lwhMakes the formula a compression of a structure students already see. "How many in a layer, how many layers."
  7. Fractional edge lengthsSubdivide the unit cube. Breaks whole-number bias where you can see it, and forces the 5.NF.B.4 arithmetic into the open instead of letting it hide.
  8. Nets and surface areaAn application of steps 1–5. Not a new topic.

The progression to abstract

Not classic C→R→A — and that's deliberate The stages below come from the spatial-structuring progression, which is what the evidence for this topic actually describes. The shape is similar; the justification and the trigger are different. Each stage has a different job, and the middle one is load-bearing.
StageIts jobStudents doMove on when
Structure with material Not "be concrete" — to make layer and row structure available for narration Tile a rectangle, stack cubes into a prism, cut and rearrange a parallelogram. Always narrate: "how many in a row, how many rows" / "how many in a layer, how many layers" The student predicts the count before manipulating and is right. Predicting, not counting, is the signal
Structure in a drawing To strip the structure out of the material. This is where abstraction actually happens — and the stage most likely to get skipped as decorative Draw the covered rectangle with lines, not individual squares. Draw one layer of the prism and label how many layers. Mark the perpendicular height with a right-angle symbol The drawing shows two perpendicular sets of lines rather than individually drawn units
Formula as compression To make the structure portable Write A = bh, A = ½bh, V = Bh — and say what each letter is doing in the picture The student can go backwards: given the area, find the missing height
Return signal — fading is not one-way Student writes a formula and can't say which number is the height, or produces an answer whose magnitude is absurd (surface area smaller than one face; volume smaller than the base area). Drop back to the drawing, not the material — the drawing catches most of it, and "let's see your picture" is a much lower-status move for a 6th grader than "go get the cubes."

Where the research disagrees

Structure-first vs. explicit-formula-first

On one side, Huang & Wu and Huang both found structure- and geometry-integrated instruction beating calculation-focused instruction on conceptual outcomes T2. On the other, Gersten et al.'s meta-analysis found explicit instruction and heuristics to be the two most robust levers for students with LD T1 — and a third of your room is the population those findings are about.

What it hinges on: the outcome measured. Huang & Wu's structure advantage appeared on conceptual understanding and disappeared on numerical calculation. The explicit-instruction meta-analyses are dominated by computation outcomes. They aren't contradicting each other; they're measuring different things.

Recommendation: structure-first, taught explicitly. That's not a dodge — it means the content of the explicit instruction is the structure ("here's how you see rows and columns; watch me; now you") rather than the procedure. Explicit teaching of a conceptual move is not discovery learning.

What would flip me: if your six-week data shows kids can explain and can't compute, invert the emphasis without guilt. Huang & Wu gives you permission — formula-first was not worse on calculation.

How strong is the case for fading, honestly? Weaker than usually claimed. Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas (2015) is the flagship study isolating a faded condition against both concrete-only and abstract-only, and fading won — but the topic was math equivalence with balance scales in grades 2–3, not geometry. Carbonneau et al.'s meta-analysis measures manipulatives against symbols-only; it never isolates fading as a variable. Net: the evidence supports using concrete representations (strong, general, not geometry-specific) and gating their withdrawal on mastery (weak, indirect). It does not establish that fading is the active ingredient for area and volume. Say that plainly if anyone asks.
Can one teacher run this in 55 minutes with a mixed-readiness room? Yes, and it's not a compromise. One task for the whole class with the representation varying by student — the below-grade-level kid is still cutting the same parallelogram, just spending longer at the material stage while a classmate is already drawing. What it costs is that the material stage takes more class time than a formula-first unit would. Buy the structure by spending less on nets, not by spending more total days.

Three gap types, three different responses

Conceptual gap

Spatial structuring was never built. Requires the material stage; cannot be scaffolded around. Most common gap in this unit, and exactly why formula-first fails these students: they execute V = lwh and can't tell you what they computed.

Procedural gap

Structure is there but identifying the perpendicular height is slow and error-prone. Responds to worked-example pairs and to marking the right-angle symbol on every figure for a week.

Fluency bottleneck

Fraction multiplication eats working memory, so the volume idea can't land on top of it. Give the tool — a reference card, or the area model for the sub-step. This is a cognitive-load argument, not lowered expectations: it's refusing to assess 5.NF.B.4 while pretending to assess geometry.

Just-in-time, not re-teach-first Re-anchor the specific prerequisite for five minutes immediately before the lesson that needs it, rather than running a two-week 5th grade review before anyone touches grade-level content. TNTP's data: acceleration classrooms completed 27% more grade-level lessons than remediation classrooms — 28% more in Title 1 schools, 49% more in majority-students-of-color schools — and remediation classrooms showed more struggle, not less. Label this honestly: it's a non-randomized natural experiment on one platform's usage data with teacher self-selection. Tier 3. Useful, not dispositive.

Differentiation without three lessons

Support — task stays the same

  • Number choice within one set. Same L‑shape task, whole-number dimensions for some, fractional for others. Same structure, different arithmetic load.
  • The grid is the floor. A student who can't decompose counts squares and gets there. One who can does it in two moves. Same task, same answer, no separate worksheet.
  • Worked-example pairs beside a blank parallel problem — the cheapest version of Gersten's strongest finding.
  • Strategic partnering at the material stage, so "how many in a layer, how many layers" gets said out loud by a student rather than only by you.

Extension — deepen, don't accelerate

  • Reversibility. "Area is 24 and the base is 6 — what could the height be? Is there more than one answer?" Then: "Triangle area is 24. Give me three different base-height pairs."
  • Generalization. "You found that trapezoid by decomposing. Will your method work on every trapezoid? Show me one where it doesn't."
  • Error-finding. The flawed-net critique task, plus an area version — a worked solution using the slant side as the height, to be found and diagnosed. Explaining someone else's error is harder than avoiding it.
  • Do not send them into 7th grade circles. Same idea, more depth.

Where this evidence base is strong, and where it isn't Strong on how understanding develops and what errors students make — the spatial-structuring and misconception literature converges across four decades and multiple countries and maps cleanly onto 6.G. Weak on which instructional approach works best for this specific content: no WWC-qualifying intervention study in geometry, no RCT on area/volume instruction in grades 5–8, and zero geometry studies in the CRA evidence base. Everything here that reads as an instructional recommendation rests on (a) strong developmental evidence about what students do, plus (b) general instructional principles never tested on this content. That's the honest answer if someone asks for an effect size.

Design: RCT, 8-year-olds, mental-rotation vs. spatial-scaling training. Search-summary retrieval. · Found: Near transfer in both groups; far transfer specifically to geometry-shape problems from both training types. · Used for: the one retrieved study showing spatial transfer into geometry.
T2 Battista, M. T., Clements, D. H., Arnoff, J., Battista, K., & Van Auken Borrow, C. (1998). Students' spatial structuring of 2D arrays of squares. JRME, 29(5), 503–532.
Design: Grade 2, classroom teaching-experiment interviews. · Found: Many students do not see row-by-column structure even when covering a rectangle with unit squares; documents a hierarchy from disorganized counting → row-based counting with uncertain row count → immediate rows × columns. · Used for: the core claim that structuring is not automatic and must be taught.

Design: Grades 4–5. Abstract-level retrieval. · Found: Defines spatial structuring as "establishing units, establishing relationships between units… and recognizing that a subset of the units, if repeated properly, can generate the whole set"; without it students cannot build a coherent mental model of a 3‑D array and miscount hidden and shared cubes. · Used for: the 3‑D structuring claim and the visible-cube misconception.

Design: Grade 5, three pairs of students, inquiry-based classroom, task-based. · Found: Traces how students' cognitive constructions for enumerating 3‑D cube arrays change over time using predict-the-count-in-a-drawn-box tasks. · Used for: the predict-before-counting task design.
T2 Battista, M. T. (2004). Applying cognition-based assessment to elementary school students' development of understanding of area and volume measurement. Mathematical Thinking and Learning, 6(2), 185–204.
Design: Elementary students, cognition-based assessment framework. Abstract-level retrieval. · Found: An integrated set of levels spanning area and volume from informal pre-instructional reasoning to formal targeted concepts, marking cognitive plateaus. · Used for: framing diagnosis as level-placement rather than right/wrong.
T2 Outhred, L., & Mitchelmore, M. (2004). Students' structuring of rectangular arrays. Proceedings of PME28, Vol. 3, 465–472.
Design: n = 115, grades 1–4, individual interviews, array-drawing tasks. Full text retrieved. · Found: Five-level progression in students' drawings: unorganized units → primitive covering → individually drawn aligned units → some lines → two perpendicular sets of lines. Numerically correct counts rarely occurred without the lines representation. Materials can obscure structure and encourage counting over multiplicative thinking. · Used for: the fade trigger and the manipulatives caution.
T2 Huang, H.-M. E., & Wu, H.-Y. (2019). Supporting children's understanding of volume measurement and ability to solve volume problems. EURASIA J. of Mathematics, Science and Technology Education, 15(12), em1789.
Design: n = 53 fifth graders, Taipei, two-phase quasi-experiment, pre/post. Full text retrieved. · Found: Layer-structure curriculum beat formula-only on post-test total, F(1,50) = 6.45, p < .01, η² = .11, and on conceptual understanding, F(1,50) = 6.90, p < .01, η² = .12; not significant on numerical calculation (p = .08). Design: n = 131 fourth graders, northern Taiwan, one instructor, three curriculum conditions. · Found: Curriculum integrating 2‑D geometry knowledge outperformed geometry-only and calculation-only on problems requiring judgment and explanation, and improved perimeter/area discrimination. · Used for: the parallel area-side evidence against formula-first.
T2 Barrett, J. E., Cullen, C., Sarama, J., Clements, D. H., Klanderman, D., Miller, A. L., & Rumsey, C. (2011). Children's unit concepts in measurement: A teaching experiment spanning grades 2 through 5. ZDM Mathematics Education, 43(5), 637–650.
Design: Six-semester longitudinal teaching experiment, grades 2–5. · Found: Task sequences integrating representations of unit, grouping units into higher-order units, and recognizing the arbitrary nature of unit produced more developed unit concepts; reflection on multiplicative relations was the productive move. · Used for: treating length → area → volume as one unitizing thread.

Abstract-level verification only (paywalled). · Found: Documented misconceptions including confusing the area formula with the perimeter formula, believing a box has more than one surface area, using the volume formula for surface area, and writing "surface area equals length plus width plus height." · Used for: volume/surface-area conflation and unit errors. Design: Diagnostic test, 18 Grade 8 students, South Africa. Full text retrieved. · Found: 44% used the volume formula when asked for surface area; 22% added edge dimensions; 17% combined both shapes into one sum; 11% applied a plain 2‑D area formula; 61% couldn't identify a cube or rectangular prism from a drawing; 83% couldn't state the number of dimensions of a 3‑D solid. · Used for: volume/surface-area and unit-error misconceptions. n = 18 — illustrative, not a population estimate.

Design: 82 students, Years 4–6, three schools in Victoria, Australia. · Found: Given "a rectangle with a larger perimeter will always have a larger area," students readily produced confirming examples but struggled to construct counterexamples that refuted it. · Used for: the covariation misconception, and the recommendation that students construct the counterexample themselves.
T2 Cutugno, P., & Spagnolo, F. Misconceptions about triangle in elementary school. G.R.I.M., University of Palermo.
Design: Open-question questionnaire, 77 students aged 11–12, Palermo, Italy. Research-group technical report, not a refereed journal. · Found: 91% drew triangles with a horizontal base "as if it were a heavy body"; 39% always marked the height as a strictly vertical line; 56% always marked it inside the triangle; 18% got the height right only when the base was horizontal. T2 Ekawati, R., Kohar, A. W., Imah, E. M., Amin, S. M., & Fiangga, S. (2019). Students' cognitive processes in solving problem related to the concept of area conservation. Journal on Mathematics Education, 10(1), 21–36.

T2 Funny, R. A. (2014). Students' initial understanding of the concept of conservation of area. IndoMS Journal on Mathematics Education, 5(1), 57–65.

T2 Wright, V., & Smith, K. (2017). Children's schemes for anticipating the validity of nets for solids. Mathematics Education Research Journal, 29, 369–394.

Open questions and weak spots

  • No strong instructional-effectiveness evidence exists for this content. No RCT on area/volume instruction in grades 5–8. No WWC-qualifying study in geometry — the practice guide's own table says so. The two quasi-experiments carrying the recommendation (n = 53, n = 131) are small, non-US, and one has a null on computation.
  • Fractional edge lengths (6.G.A.2) is the least-studied piece. No peer-reviewed empirical study of 6th graders learning volume by subdividing unit cubes turned up. The nearest verified work is on prospective teachers. The whole-number-bias framing is my inference, marked unverified.
  • No NAEP or TIMSS percentages appear anywhere in this brief. Genuine released items were retrieved (irregular area on a grid; unit-of-volume recognition; the linear-vs-square-vs-cubic classroom-floor item; identify-the-net); the percent-correct data lives in the NAEP Questions Tool, which wouldn't render. The items are still usable as diagnostics.
  • Several prevalence figures come from very small samples. Chiphambo & Mtsi's striking numbers are n = 18. Treat them as evidence the error exists, not as estimates of how many of your students will make it.
  • What I'd want and couldn't find: a controlled comparison of structure-first vs. formula-first area/volume instruction in a US middle school with a similar population, measuring both conceptual and computational outcomes at delay. That study would resolve most of the disagreement above. It does not appear to exist.
