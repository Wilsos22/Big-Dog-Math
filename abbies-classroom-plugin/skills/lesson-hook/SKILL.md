# The hook

The hook question is the single highest-leverage subjective decision in a Big Dog Math lesson. Everything downstream - the CRA spine, the manipulative, the numbered routine, the exit ticket - is machinery that works only if students already want the answer. A weak hook does not make a lesson slightly worse. It makes the next 45 minutes compliance instead of curiosity.

So: **no lesson ships without a hook that clears all four gates.** If a lesson is being built and the hook is missing, thin, or corny, stop and fix the hook first. Do not author `concrete` around a hook you would not personally want answered.

---

## 1. The hook is an arc, not a line

It occupies four states in the 50-minute spine. Author all four or the payoff never lands.

| State | Min | The hook's job |
|---|---|---|
| `warmup` | 0-5 | The question sits on the projector the entire time students do the Google Form. They read it. They cannot solve it. That gap is the whole point - confusion is step one. |
| `launch` | 5-8 | Not answers. **Approaches.** "What would you even try first?" Harvest two real approaches and one honest dead end. |
| `exit` | 46-49 | The same question comes back, word for word, as independent written evidence. Same wording matters - a reworded return reads as a different problem and kills the callback. |
| `closeout` | 49-50 | The payoff. One line: *you could not do this 50 minutes ago and now you can.* Reveal what the answer turned out to be. |

If the hook cannot survive being asked identically at minute 0 and minute 46, it is not a hook. It is a word problem.

---

## 2. The four gates

Every hook is scored 0 or 1 on each. **4/4 ships. Anything less gets rewritten, not softened.**

**Gate 1 - Curiosity.** A student who dislikes math still wants to know the answer.
Test: strip the math out entirely. Is the question still interesting? If the interest was doing arithmetic, it fails.

**Gate 2 - Stakes.** Something is riding on it. Money lost, time stolen, an argument settled, an adult proven wrong, a claim busted, a status win over older kids. "Interesting" is not enough. Somebody has to be affected.
Test: name who loses what if the answer goes the wrong way. If you cannot, it fails.

**Gate 3 - Locked, then opened.** Genuinely unsolvable at minute 0 with what they walked in with. Fully solvable at minute 46 with what the lesson gave them.
Test: if a strong student could answer it during the warm-up, it is a warm-up question, not a hook. If a student who nailed the exit ticket still could not answer it, the lesson taught the wrong thing.

**Gate 4 - Plausibility.** It survives *"wait, did that actually happen?"*
Test: read it as a suspicious 12-year-old. Anything convenient is a tell. Round numbers are a tell. A story where the teacher looks competent is a tell.

## 3. Five families

Pick the family that fits the mathematics, not the one that is easiest to write.

**A. Steele has a real problem.** Something outside school with actual consequences. Draws on what students already know about his life - Abbie, his car, his apartment, rec league, a purchase he regrets.
> Abbie's vet says she needs to drop 4 pounds. The bag says one cup per 20 pounds of dog. She is 58 pounds and I have been giving her 3 cups a day. I need to know if I am the problem here.

**B. The school is doing something.** Bell schedule, vending prices, field trip cost, cafeteria portions, the gym floor, the parking lot, the mile run, book fair. Students have standing and an authority to check.
> Next year passing period drops from 5 minutes to 4. The email said that is "a negligible change." I want to know how much class time that actually is over a year before I decide whether to be annoyed.

**C. The invented study, or the older-kid challenge.** A stat that sets up a status win. Sixth graders will burn down a building to beat 8th graders.
> A study found 47% of 12th graders could not do this. I do not believe it needs to take that long to learn. I think this class does it today.

Variants that work the same way: the 8th grade teacher next door said her kids struggled with it; this was a question on a hiring test at a real job; this showed up on the ACT.

**D. The disputed claim.** Two people disagree and exactly one is wrong, and it is costing somebody.
> My buddy and I split the check every Friday. He says we each owe $23. I say $19. One of us has been wrong since March and I want to know who.

**E. The hidden number.** Something they look at daily that nobody has ever measured.
> Nobody in this building knows how many gallons of water this school flushes in one day. I think we can get close enough to be dangerous.

**Family chooser:** proportion / rate / unit rate → A or E · percent change, time, money → B or D · fluency and procedure they think is beneath them → C · estimation, ratio reasoning, large numbers → E · order of operations, expressions, equality → D.

## 4. Hard fails - the corny list

Any one of these and the hook is dead. Rewrite, do not patch.

- Opens with "Imagine you are...", "Have you ever wondered...", "Picture this...", "What if I told you..."
- A named fictional student. *Marcus has 3 apples* is not a hook, and every child knows Marcus is not real.
- Pizza. Fractions of pizza, sharing pizza, any pizza.
- The answer is visible in the question.
- Adult-life framing a 12-year-old does not care about: mortgages, taxes, retirement, 401k, grocery budgeting, compound interest on savings.
- Exclamation marks doing the work enthusiasm should do.
- **Round convenient numbers.** 50, 100, exactly 25%, exactly $20. Real life produces 58 pounds and $23.40. Ugly numbers are the cheapest plausibility you can buy.
- **Steele is the hero.** He is never the expert in a hook. He is stuck, out of money, losing an argument, suspicious, or already wrong. Students help people who need help; they do not help people who are fine.
- It requires a student to pretend. Pretending is a tax on the ones who already feel dumb.
- It is a scenario rather than a question. It must end in a question mark somebody wants answered.
- Emojis. Anywhere. Ever.

## 5. Voice and surfaces

**Main Display (projector, 0-5).** Read at 25 feet by thirty people. **25 words maximum**, ending in the question. No command verb - no "SOLVE:", no "THINK ABOUT:". The state marker already tells them what to do. Put supporting numbers underneath as bare facts, not sentences.

**What Steele says out loud.** 2-4 sentences, spoken, his register - dry, self-deprecating, a little put out. This is where the story lives; the projector only carries the question.

**Pace Directions.** Nothing about the hook. It carries current directions only.

**Abbie line (optional).** One deadpan sentence, calls him "dad", roasts him and never a student. Use it when the hook is family A and Abbie is in the story, or when the payoff at closeout wants a button on it.

Invented statistics are used as written, no reveal required. Keep the percentage ugly - 47%, 63%, not "half."

## 6. Output format

Deliver exactly this. It is the transcription source for the Notion Lesson Steps.

```
HOOK - [lesson code or topic]
Family: [A-E]   Storyline: [new | continuing "<thread name>" from <lesson>]

WARMUP 0-5 | Main Display  (<=25 words)
  <the question>
  <bare supporting numbers, one per line>

WARMUP | spoken
  <2-4 sentences in Steele's voice>

LAUNCH 5-8 | discussion
  Prompt: <what would you try first, phrased for this hook>
  Fish for: <approach 1> / <approach 2>
  Let live: <the honest dead end worth 30 seconds>

EXIT 46-49 | the return
  <the identical question, now answerable, as the written item>

CLOSEOUT 49-50 | payoff
  Answer: <what it turns out to be>
  Line: <one sentence Steele says>
  Abbie: <optional, one deadpan sentence>

GATES  Curiosity [x] Stakes [x] Locked [x] Plausible [x]
  Stakes: <who loses what>
  Locked because: <what they lack at min 0>
  Opened by: <the specific thing concrete/representational gives them>
```

## 7. Audit mode

Triggered by "this hook is weak", "they will not care", "audit my hooks", or any existing lesson whose hook is being reviewed.

1. Score the existing hook against the four gates. Report the score before anything else.
2. **Quote the exact failing words.** "Imagine you are a store manager" is a specific, fixable phrase. "It lacks engagement" is not feedback.
3. Rewrite in full, same family if the family was right, different family if it was not.
4. Show the old and the new side by side with one line on what changed.
5. If the hook fails Gate 3 because the lesson does not actually give students what the hook needs, say so plainly - that is a lesson problem, not a hook problem, and rewriting the hook will hide it.

A 3/4 hook is not "pretty good." Name which gate failed and fix that gate.

## 8. The storyline bank

Recurring threads are what turn hooks into culture. When students start asking *"wait, did Abbie ever lose the weight?"* unprompted, buy-in stops being something you manufacture per lesson.

Maintain `lesson-hooks/HOOK-BANK.md` in the Big Dog Math repo (`/Users/steelewilson/Big Dog Math Site`), so Codex and cloud sessions can read it too. One row per thread:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Thread | Short name - "Abbie's diet", "the passing period fight", "Friday check split" |
| Established | Facts already stated to students that later hooks must not contradict |
| Used in | Lesson codes, in order |
| Status | open / resolved / retired |
| Next beat | Where this thread could plausibly go |

**Rules of continuity**

- Read the bank before writing a new hook. A continuing thread beats a fresh one roughly two times out of three - it inherits the buy-in you already paid for.
- Never contradict an established fact. Abbie is 58 pounds in October, she cannot be 40 in January without the intervening story.
- Run **three to five threads at a time**, not one. A single thread every day becomes a bit, and a bit gets old.
- Resolve threads out loud. An open loop that never closes teaches students the stories are fake.
- Retire a thread the moment a student can predict it.

Add the row in the same turn you write the hook. A thread that lives only in a Notion lesson is a thread the next session cannot see.

---

## 9. Working rules

- Write the hook **before** the CRA states, not after. The hook decides what `concrete` has to give them.
- One hook per teaching day. Not one per state.
- Practice days and review days still open with a hook. Shorter, lower stakes, but present - the arc is warm-up and closeout only.
- If you cannot clear all four gates, say so and offer two alternates from different families rather than shipping a 3/4.
- Never attribute the confusion-is-step-one framing to Carnegie. It is Steele's.
