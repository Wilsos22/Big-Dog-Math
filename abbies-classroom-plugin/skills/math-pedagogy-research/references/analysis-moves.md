# Analysis Moves

Read this before writing. Collecting sources is the easy half; these five moves are what turn a pile of citations into something Steele can teach from Monday.

Each move ends with a **what "done" looks like** check.

---

## 1. Emphasis determination

Steele asked for this by name, and it's the least common thing in a research summary. The question is not "what's in the standard" — it's **where does an hour of instruction buy the most?**

Four ways to establish leverage, in descending order of defensibility:

**a. Downstream predictive evidence.** Some knowledge predicts later achievement out of proportion to its size in the standards. Fraction magnitude understanding is the canonical example — there is longitudinal work tying elementary fraction knowledge to later algebra performance. When you can cite this kind of evidence, it is the strongest possible argument for spending extra days on something.

**b. Prerequisite fan-out.** Map what this topic feeds. A sub-skill that three later units depend on outranks one that dead-ends. Achieve the Core's coherence map is a fast source for this and is defensible in a coaching conversation because it's built on the standards themselves.

**c. Misconception density.** Sub-skills where the documented error rate is high and the errors are *durable* deserve more time — not because they're hard, but because a wrong model formed here survives into 7th and 8th grade. If the literature shows an error persisting across grades, that's an emphasis argument.

**d. Standards weighting.** Major work of the grade vs. supporting/additional. Real, but weakest — it tells you what's assessed, not what's cognitively load-bearing.

Then say the uncomfortable part out loud: **what gets less time.** A 6th grade unit is finite. An emphasis recommendation that only adds is not a recommendation. Name the sub-skills that can be compressed, folded into warm-up spiral review, or handled procedurally-first, and say why the research makes that acceptable.

> **Done when:** the brief names 2–3 highest-leverage sub-skills with a stated reason and evidence tier for each, *and* names what gets compressed to pay for them.

## 2. Sequencing

Give a defensible order and the reasoning behind it — not just a list of days.

For each step in the sequence, answer: **what does this step make possible that wasn't possible before it?** If you can't answer that, the step is in the wrong place or doesn't belong.

Things that reliably matter in middle-grades sequencing:

- **Meaning before procedure, but not meaning *forever* before procedure.** The procedural/conceptual literature increasingly describes these as iterative and mutually reinforcing rather than strictly ordered. A sequence that withholds procedure for two weeks is as poorly supported as one that opens with it. Look for evidence on the *interleaving* of the two.
- **Which meaning comes first.** Many topics have multiple valid interpretations — division as measurement ("how many halves fit in 3?") versus partitive ("3 shared into half a group?"). Which one to lead with is often an empirical question with a real answer in the literature. Find it and say it.
- **Representational progression.** Which model first, which second, and what each one makes visible that the previous one hid. Note where a model *breaks* — area models handle fraction ÷ fraction badly; number lines make measurement division obvious but partitive awkward. Naming a model's expiration date is more useful than endorsing it.
- **Where to place the generalization.** The moment students are asked to state the rule is a design decision. Research on invented strategies and on productive failure bears on this directly.

Where sources conflict, present both orders and what the disagreement hinges on. Then recommend one and give the condition that would flip you.

> **Done when:** each step has a stated purpose, at least one sequencing choice is justified against a named alternative, and the recommendation is stated plainly rather than hedged into mush.

## 3. Misconception pre-emption

The request was specifically "how to prepare for them" — not just list them. So each misconception gets four parts:

1. **What students actually do** — the observable error, written as a wrong answer you'd see on paper. `2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 2/18` beats "students confuse operations."
2. **The reasoning underneath** — why an intelligent student produces this. Most durable middle-school errors are over-generalizations of rules that *were* true earlier: whole-number bias, "division makes smaller," "multiplication makes bigger," treating a fraction as two independent whole numbers. Naming the previously-valid rule is what makes the error fixable, because it tells you what has to be renegotiated rather than just corrected.
3. **The design move that surfaces it early** — a task, question, or number choice that makes the misconception *produce a visibly wrong answer* in week one instead of hiding until the test. This is the highest-value part of the whole section. Estimation prompts ("will the answer be bigger or smaller than 3?"), number choices where the wrong rule gives an absurd result, and same-numbers-different-operations comparisons all do this work.
4. **The response when it appears** — what to actually say and do. This is where Steele's culture lives: confusion is step one, *what do you know* is step two, *try something* is step three. A response that just re-explains the correct procedure wastes the moment.

Connect this to his warm-up format when it fits: 2 review + 3 current, with the third current question engineered to surface a named misconception. That's already how he works, and misconception research is exactly what should be choosing that third question.

> **Done when:** every misconception has all four parts, the errors are written as actual wrong answers, and at least one is traced back to a rule that used to be true.

## 4. The progression to abstract

**Pick the progression the evidence supports for this topic, then design the fade.** Do not assume the answer is C→R→A.

Steele runs CRA as a default because one task with a built-in progression serves a room with three years of spread — a scheduling and management argument, and a good one. But he wants what works above all else, so CRA enters this section as a candidate, not a premise. Before writing, ask what the literature on *this specific topic* actually recommends. Real alternatives worth checking every time:

- **Number-line-first / magnitude-first**, where the number line is the primary representation rather than a middle stage
- **Worked-example pairs and faded worked examples**, which come out of cognitive load theory and often outperform discovery for procedural targets
- **Invented strategies before the standard algorithm**, where students build methods first and the algorithm arrives as a compression
- **Compare-and-contrast**, where two solution methods are presented side by side rather than sequenced
- **Concreteness fading proper**, which is CRA-adjacent but makes different claims about timing and about what the "R" stage is doing

If one of these has better evidence for the topic, lead with it and say where CRA sits relative to it. If CRA *is* the best-supported choice, say that too — with the evidence, not by default. Either way, close with the practical check: can one teacher run this in one period with a mixed-readiness room? An approach that wins on evidence but needs parallel lessons or a co-teacher still gets reported, with the cost named.

**The nuance that has to be in the brief regardless of which progression you land on:** the empirical support in this area is strongest for *fading* — starting with support and systematically withdrawing it — rather than for concrete representations on their own. Studies in the concreteness-fading line generally find faded conditions outperform both concrete-only and abstract-only. The implication is that **the fade is the intervention.** A manipulative that never leaves is a documented failure mode, not a neutral choice: students can get very good at the manipulative and not transfer at all. Verify the specific findings before writing this, but do not omit the point — it's the most actionable thing the CRA literature has to say.

For the topic at hand, work out:

- **What the concrete stage represents.** The manipulative must embody the mathematical structure, not just be a hands-on activity adjacent to it. Fraction tiles that can't be subdivided can't show fraction ÷ fraction. Ask what the material makes *impossible* to do wrong, and what it lets students do wrong.
- **Materials that are actually gettable.** Title 1 budget. Prefer paper strips, folded paper, grid paper, string, measuring cups, and printables over class sets of specialty plastic. When you recommend something purchased, say what the free substitute is.
- **The representational bridge.** Drawings are not a decorative middle step — the research treats representational work as where structure gets abstracted from the material. Say what students draw, and what the drawing must preserve from the concrete stage.
- **The fade trigger.** Under what observable condition does a student move to the next stage? "After two days" is a schedule, not a trigger. "When the student can predict the result before manipulating" is a trigger. Give Steele a trigger.
- **The return path.** Fading is not one-way. Name the signal that a student should drop back a stage, so that returning to materials reads as a strategy rather than a demotion — which matters a lot for 6th graders' willingness to use it.

> **Done when:** the brief names the material, what it embodies, what students draw, an observable fade trigger, a return signal, and a cheap substitute for anything purchased.

## 5. The two-years-behind band

This is the difference between a generic brief and one for Steele's room. A 6th grade Title 1 class does not have a shared starting point, and a sequence that assumes one will lose a third of the room in the first ten minutes.

**Prerequisite chain.** Trace back two to three grade levels. For each prerequisite, give a *diagnostic item* — one specific problem whose answer tells you whether the student has it. Not "check understanding of equivalent fractions," but the actual problem and what each likely wrong answer tells you. These should be usable as the 2 review problems in his warm-up format, which is the cheapest possible diagnostic delivery.

**Acceleration over remediation.** The practitioner and policy consensus — Tier 3, label it as such — favors teaching grade-level content while addressing prerequisite gaps *just in time*, over pulling students back to re-teach prior grades before they're allowed at grade-level work. Say concretely what just-in-time support looks like for this topic: which prerequisite gets a 5-minute re-anchor immediately before the lesson that needs it, and which gaps can be worked around with a scaffold instead of closed first.

**Distinguish gap types.** They need different responses:

- **Conceptual gap** — the model was never built. Requires the concrete stage; can't be scaffolded around.
- **Procedural gap** — the model exists but the routine is slow or error-prone. Responds to worked examples, practice structure, and fluency work.
- **Fluency bottleneck** — the student can do it but so slowly that working memory is consumed and the new learning can't happen. Often solvable with a tool (multiplication chart, calculator for the sub-step) so the cognitive load lands on the new idea. Say when a tool is the right call — this is a load argument, not a lowering of expectations, and it's worth framing that way for anyone reviewing the plan.

**Differentiation that isn't three different lessons.** Steele is one person with 55 minutes. Favor structures where the *task stays the same* and the support varies: low-floor/high-ceiling task design, varied number choices within a single problem set, strategic partnering, worked-example pairs for students who need a model before attempting, extension prompts rather than more problems. Flag anything that requires a separate parallel lesson as high-cost and say whether the evidence justifies the cost.

**Above grade level too.** The band goes both directions. Extension should deepen the same idea — reversibility ("what would have to be true for the answer to be 4?"), generalization, error-finding — not race ahead into 7th grade content.

> **Done when:** each prerequisite has a real diagnostic item with interpretation, gap types are distinguished with different responses, and the differentiation plan is something one teacher can actually run in one period.
