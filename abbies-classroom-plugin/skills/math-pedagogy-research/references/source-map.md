# Source Map — where the real evidence lives

Read this before searching. It exists because a naive web search for "how to teach dividing fractions" returns worksheet farms and blog posts, and Steele needs something that survives scrutiny.

---

## Evidence tiers

Tag every claim in the brief with one of these. The tier is about **how much weight the claim can bear**, not how useful it is — a Tier 3 activity can be excellent and still be craft wisdom.

**Tier 1 — Strong.** Meta-analyses, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, IES/WWC Practice Guides where the recommendation itself is rated *Strong* or *Moderate*, and large well-controlled quasi-experiments. These can carry a "the evidence supports" claim.

**Tier 2 — Moderate.** Peer-reviewed studies with smaller samples or weaker designs, well-designed qualitative and design-based research, teaching experiments, longitudinal correlational work, and WWC recommendations rated *Minimal*. These support "research suggests" or "documented in classroom studies."

**Tier 3 — Practitioner / professional consensus.** NCTM position statements and MTLT articles, curriculum publisher research briefs, state and district guidance, respected practitioner books. Genuinely valuable for *what a task looks like on Tuesday*, but they describe practice rather than test it. Say "widely used and professionally endorsed, though not experimentally tested."

**Tier 4 — Anecdotal.** Blogs, teacher forums, TPT, undated PDFs with no author. Use only for activity inspiration, and only if labeled. Never let a Tier 4 source carry an effectiveness claim.

A short brief made of Tier 1 and 2 beats a long one padded with Tier 4.

## Where to search

**Search these first:**

- `ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc` — What Works Clearinghouse practice guides and intervention reports. The single highest-value source for this brief; recommendations come pre-rated by evidence strength.
- `eric.ed.gov` — the federal education research database. Best single place to find peer-reviewed work with free full text. Filter to peer-reviewed.
- `scholar.google.com` results surfaced through general web search — good for finding what a study actually found and how often it's been cited.
- `nctm.org` — *Mathematics Teacher: Learning & Teaching PK-12* (MTLT), *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education* (JRME). JRME is Tier 1–2; MTLT is usually Tier 3.
- `achievethecore.org` — coherence maps and focus documents. Useful for the prerequisite chain and for "major work of the grade," which feeds emphasis determination.

**Journals worth naming in a query** (adding a journal name to a search often flips results from blogs to scholarship):

Journal for Research in Mathematics Education · Educational Studies in Mathematics · Journal of Educational Psychology · Cognition and Instruction · Learning and Instruction · ZDM Mathematics Education · Journal of Learning Disabilities · Learning Disabilities Research & Practice · Exceptional Children · Mathematical Thinking and Learning · Journal of Mathematical Behavior · Elementary School Journal

**Domains to distrust** unless you're mining them for activity ideas and labeling Tier 4: teacherspayteachers, study.com, content-farm "math help" sites, undated printable collections.

## Query patterns that work

Bad: `how to teach dividing fractions`
Better: `fraction division misconceptions students research study`
Best: `"fraction division" conceptual understanding middle school JRME OR "Journal for Research in Mathematics Education"`

Patterns worth rotating through:

- `<topic> misconceptions students error analysis study`
- `<topic> instruction randomized trial effect`
- `<topic> "learning trajectory" OR "learning progression" research`
- `concrete representational abstract <topic> intervention students disabilities`
- `"concreteness fading" <topic>`
- `<topic> intervention "struggling students" OR "below grade level" middle school`
- `What Works Clearinghouse practice guide <domain>`
- `<researcher surname> <topic>` once a name keeps recurring — following a productive author is usually faster than another keyword search

When a source keeps getting cited by other sources you found, go get it directly. Citation gravity is a good signal.

## Anchor literature — leads, not citations

These are landmarks that recur across middle-grades math. **Treat them as search targets to retrieve and verify, not as citations to copy into the brief.** Details below may be imprecise; confirm author, year, and finding before anything appears in the brief.

**Practice guides (start here for almost any topic)**
- IES/WWC practice guide on developing effective *fractions* instruction, K–8 — the anchor document for any rational-number topic
- IES/WWC practice guide on assisting students struggling with mathematics (intervention/RTI)
- IES/WWC practice guide on improving mathematical problem solving, grades 4–8
- IES/WWC guide on organizing instruction and study to improve student learning (spacing, interleaving, worked examples)

**Why fractions carry outsized weight**
- Siegler and colleagues on fraction knowledge in elementary school predicting later algebra and overall math achievement — the strongest single argument available for emphasis decisions in 6th grade
- Siegler's integrated theory of numerical development (number line as the unifying representation across whole numbers and fractions)

**Rational number reasoning and representation**
- The Rational Number Project (Behr, Post, Lesh, Cramer) — curriculum and studies on fraction representations
- Empson & Levi on extending children's fraction thinking through equal sharing
- Steffe, Norton, Hackenberg, Tzur on fraction schemes and splitting/partitioning operations
- Lamon on rational number and proportional reasoning
- Ma (Liping Ma) on teachers' understanding of fraction division — the measurement vs. partitive distinction and why "invert and multiply" is so often taught without meaning

**Concrete → abstract**
- CRA research in special education: Witzel, Mercer & Miller; Flores; Bouck; Agrawal & Morin's review of CRA in mathematics
- **Concreteness fading**: Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone. Important nuance — this line of work generally finds that *fading* concrete supports outperforms staying concrete *and* outperforms starting abstract. The empirical support is for the fade, not for the manipulative. Read `analysis-moves.md` before writing this section.
- Cognitive load theory and worked examples: Sweller; Renkl; faded worked examples; the expertise-reversal effect

**Students below grade level / intervention**
- Gersten et al. meta-analysis of mathematics instruction for students with learning disabilities
- Fuchs and colleagues on fraction intervention for at-risk 4th–6th graders — one of the few RCT-grade fraction intervention lines
- Explicit and systematic instruction literature (Archer & Hughes; Doabler & Fien)
- Acceleration vs. remediation for unfinished learning — TNTP's *The Opportunity Myth*, Student Achievement Partners guidance. **Tier 3**: policy and practitioner analysis, not experimental. Label it.

**Instructional-approach debates worth surfacing**
- Explicit instruction vs. problem-solving-first / productive struggle (Hiebert & Grouws on struggle; Kapur on productive failure; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark against minimal guidance)
- Interleaved vs. blocked practice (Rohrer & Taylor)
- Retrieval practice and spacing in math
- Procedural and conceptual knowledge as iterative rather than sequential (Rittle-Johnson & Star; comparing solution methods)

## Recording what you find

For each source that earns a place in the brief, capture:

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| Author(s), year, title, venue | Basic defensibility |
| Tier (1–4) | So the claim's weight is visible |
| URL | So Steele can pull it up in a meeting |
| Population & design | "6th graders, RCT, n=259" is what makes a finding checkable |
| What it actually found | In the source's terms, not stretched toward your recommendation |
| Instructional implication | The translation into Steele's classroom — this is yours, and should read as yours |

Keep the last two separate. Blending the finding with your interpretation is how briefs quietly overclaim.
