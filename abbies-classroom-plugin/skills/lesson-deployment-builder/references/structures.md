## First: which kind of day is this

**New-learning day** → the CRA spine. Hook on the projector through the warm-up, LI and SC read from the spinner, concrete exploration in pairs, representational on a website tool, abstract on the assignment, Fist-to-Five, two learning checks, routes, exit.

**Practice day** → error analysis run extensively, gallery walks, the vertical classroom. The roster below.

**Review day before a test** → **Bruh** and **Grudge Ball**. These are self-contained games with nothing to set up — the format is already built and it does not need authoring. Do not design a Bruh or Grudge Ball day; schedule it, keep the fixed frame around it, and stay out of the way.

The split is a decision, not a spectrum. A practice day is not a CRA day with a game bolted on; a new-learning day does not open with Grudge Ball.

**What is universal across both**, and does not become optional because the day is a practice day:

- The hook, real-world, on the projector through the warm-up, unresolved.
- LI and SC read aloud by the spinner's pick, after the hook discussion.
- A Fist-to-Five, before any graded item.
- Transitions budgeted as real minutes, out of instruction, not added on top.
- All four surfaces authored on every step.
- `exit` 46–49, `closeout` 49–50, day sums to 50.

## The practice-day roster

Every one of these has to end in something written you could grade. That gate is the whole point — "use games, card sorts, relays, gallery walks, or design challenges only when they produce mathematical evidence."

| Activity | Use it when | The evidence it must produce |
|---|---|---|
| **Error analysis** (house protocol below) | Any practice day. This is the highest-value move in the system and belongs on nearly every one. | The five-part receipt, individually, on paper. |
| **Gallery walk** | Students have the concept and there is a product worth walking to. | A required revision plus an individual receipt at the end. |
| **Vertical classroom** | Work should be visible and revisable mid-thought — students at vertical non-permanent surfaces, in the small random groups the format depends on. | The board is the thinking; the receipt is what each student copies down and justifies before sitting. |

**Bruh and Grudge Ball are not on this roster.** They are **review-day-before-a-test** games, they are self-contained, and the setup is already done. Schedule the day, keep the fixed frame, author nothing inside it.

**The failure mode that applies to all three**: the activity generates energy and no artifact. If you cannot name what you would collect at the door, redesign it before you schedule it.

## Where discussion goes — three different moves, three different homes

"Discussion" names three things in this classroom and they are not interchangeable. Pick the one the day needs; do not default to a generic six-minute talk block.

### 1. Compare what you did — inside `concrete`, no state of its own

Students explored; now they check their build against a partner's. This is 60–90 seconds at the tail of the exploration and it costs **zero planned minutes**. It lives as one line on `Pace Directions` inside the `concrete` record:

```
Pace Directions:  Compare with your partner → What did you both get? → What is different?
```

Do not spend a state on this. A state buys nothing a sentence does not.

### 2. Insight, no work — a `discussion` state right after `concrete`

Steele's rule: "the discussion without work, just a chance to give insight works after concrete time to look into things."

This is the harvest between exploring and formalizing. They have messed with the thing; now they say what they noticed, **before** the tool in `representational` gives it a shape and a name. 4 minutes, `discussion` state, no writing required, no product collected.

It works there and nowhere else in the day. Run it before `concrete` and there is nothing to say; run it after `abstract` and you are debriefing a procedure they already wrote down.

```
Main Display:     What did you notice that surprised you?
Pace Directions:  Whole class → One noticing each → No answers yet
Remote Actions:   Capture 2 noticings verbatim. Reuse the exact wording in the R demo.
```

That last line is the move that makes it worth four minutes: name a student's phrasing back to them when the tool formalizes it, and the formalization lands as *their* idea getting a name.

### 3. The whiteboard protocol — this IS the release block, on Shape A days

Think → write → try → look at your neighbor and discuss → revise → share out via the spinner wheel. A **full work cycle with a product**, and it is not an addition to the day — it **is** the release, on whiteboards resting on the Chromebook keyboard decks, at students' own desks. Nobody moves.

On a whiteboard day the class works **the assignment's own problems** on the boards and the paper assignment goes home as homework, already rehearsed. There are no small groups that day; the teacher circulates instead of pulling. Run it 35–51, which is room for two full cycles plus a share-out, or three problems on a lighter cycle.

Routes still decide **which problem** each pair gets, so the privacy contract holds: every board looks identical, the problems differ, nobody can read a route off the room.

**Vertical surfaces are a different thing.** Boards at the walls are a *practice-day* structure and they do cost transition minutes — 1 out, 2 back. Do not confuse the everyday desk version with the vertical version.

One cycle, roughly 8 minutes:

| Beat | Min | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Think | 1 | Silent. Nothing written. Marker down. |
| Write | 1 | First move on the board, before anyone solves. |
| Try | 2 | Work it. Wrong work stays visible — do not erase. |
| Discuss | 2 | Compare with your partner. Compare, do not correct. |
| Revise | 1 | Back to your own board. Revision is the goal, not agreement. |
| Share | 1 | **Spinner picks who explains.** Two boards, not all. |

Non-negotiables: wrong work never erased (it is the artifact the discussion needs), and **an individual receipt** — the board is not evidence the system can see, so each student copies their final work and one justification line onto the paper assignment before it goes home.

**The day sums without cutting `review`, because nobody moves:**

```
0-5 warmup · 5-9 launch · 9-10 LI/SC · 10-14 review · 14-20 concrete
20-27 representational · 27-31 abstract (model one only) · 31-32 LI/SC + Fist-to-Five
32-35 learning check · 35-46 whiteboard on the assignment problems
46-49 exit · 49-50 closeout                                            = 50
```

`abstract` shrinks to 4 because the "we do one together" beat moves into the first board problem.

Do not run the whiteboard protocol *and* a `small-group` pull on the same day. Those are the two release shapes and they are alternatives — Shape A is boards with the assignment going home, Shape B is small groups with the assignment worked on paper in class.

**Vertical classroom specifics.** Visibly random grouping and one marker per group are what make the format work rather than three students watching one write; state both in the routine config. Budget the hustle out and back as `transition-hustle` states with real minutes. Board work is not evidence the system can see — the receipt is.

CRA is the default engine of a new-learning day. But three other structures earn their place on specific days, and picking the right one is a real decision, not decoration. Steele's rule, from the Practice Day template:

> "Use error analysis or a gallery walk when it produces better reasoning than repeating CRA."

And from M2.T1.L1-D1:

> "Prefer error analysis, method comparison, or a gallery walk when students already know the concept."

And the gate that applies to all of them:

> "Use games, card sorts, relays, gallery walks, or design challenges only when they produce mathematical evidence."

So the decision tree is short. **Do students still need a model-to-symbol bridge?** Run CRA. **Do they already have the concept and need better reasoning about it?** Error analysis or gallery walk. **Either way, does the structure end with something written you could grade?** If not, don't run it.

The lesson template also requires you to justify the pick in writing — "**Primary structure:** Name the one structure chosen for this mathematics. **Why it fits:** Explain how it improves access to the mathematical idea." Do that in the body's structure block.

## Error analysis — the house protocol

This is the most developed structure in the system and the one to reach for most often on a practice or second-day lesson. It has a named five-part receipt every student produces, from M1.T2-P3:

> - What is correct?
> - What is the first incorrect step?
> - Why is it tempting?
> - What is the precise repair?
> - What check would catch it earlier?

And the principle behind it:

> "A strong error analysis preserves correct work, identifies the first incorrect step, explains its effect, repairs only what follows, and names a check that would prevent the error. Diagram and dimension errors often occur before arithmetic."

**The diagnostic order** students are taught to run, in this sequence: diagram and cut lines → dimensions and endpoints → shape formula → partial areas → addition or subtraction → units → reasonableness. Generalize the first three to whatever the lesson's representation is; the point is that representation errors precede arithmetic errors, and students who only check arithmetic never find the real one.

**Two design features that make it work**, both visible in M1.T1.L2-D1's Jordan task:

1. **Credit the correct work first.** The prompt opens "What did Jordan do correctly" before it asks what's wrong. A student who learns to strip and rewrite everything hasn't learned to diagnose.
2. **Withhold the reveal until after revision.** `Remote Actions` on that step: "reveal the complete arch only after revision." If the answer appears before students commit, the analysis becomes copying.

**Standard stems:**
```
The first incorrect step is...
This error is believable because...
I would preserve ___ and revise...
The earliest reliable check is...
```

**Guards against the four predictable failures:**
```
only gives the correct answer :: Require the first incorrect step and its effect.
rewrites everything :: Mark and preserve each correct step before revising.
focuses only on arithmetic :: Use the diagnostic order from diagram through units.
names an error without evidence :: Require a dimension, formula, region, or unit that proves the diagnosis.
```

**Sizing.** Three ways it appears, pick by the day:
- **Embedded in `discussion`**, 6 minutes, as one wrong worked example — the new-learning-day version. This is M1.T1.L2-D1 step 10.
- **A 5-minute retrieval slot** on a practice day.
- **A full station day** — four stations × 5 minutes, each station a different error type, plus a 6-minute whole-class debrief. M1.T2-P3 is the model.

**A worked example of a good task**, verbatim from M1.T1.L2-D1:

> "Jordan tests 1 through 6 for 36, records only 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6, and says the factor list is finished. What is correct? Which partners are missing? How does a completed factor arch prove the real list?"

The error is one a real student makes, it's placed exactly where they make it, and the repair requires the day's representation. A fabricated error nobody makes teaches nothing.

**Also do this:** pair the flawed reasoning with one piece of *sound* reasoning to justify in the same period. That lesson runs a companion item — "Priya tests 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 for 36 and stops. Which statement explains why she can stop?" — so students practice recognizing valid reasoning, not just hunting for errors.

**Distractors are error analysis too.** Every multiple-choice item in the good lessons has each wrong option traceable to a named misconception, and the strongest ones attach the *wrong explanation* to the wrong answer. From M1.T1.L1-D1's exit ticket:

> "7(20 + 3) = 7 x 20 + 3; only the first part needs the factor 7.
> 7(20 + 3) = 27 x 23; add the side lengths before finding area.
> 7(20 + 3) = 140 + 3; the second region already shows its area."

`src/lib/sbacCheckpoints.ts` has `type: "error-analysis"` as a first-class item type with `misses: [{ answer, misconception }]` pairs — use those pairings where they exist.

## Gallery walk

Works when students already have the concept and need to compare reasoning across groups, and when there is a *product worth walking to*. It does not work as a substitute for a first exposure, and it does not work when everyone produced the same thing.

**Real configurations that ran:**

| Instance | Stations | Min/station | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1.T2-P2 area formula posters | 3 | 2 | 6 min |
| M1.T2-P4 design posters | 3 | 2 | 6 min |
| M1.T2-R1 moving error clinic | 4 | 4 | 16 min |
| Ratio-graph error clinic | 4 | 4–5 | ~18 min |

The shipped code default is 4 stations × 3 minutes (`defaultLessonRoutineConfig()`), which matches none of the authored lessons — treat the default as a starting point, not a recommendation. Bounds are `MAX_STATIONS 20`, `MAX_ROTATION_MINUTES 60`.

**The config you author** (`GalleryWalkRoutineConfig`, encoded as base64url in `AI Context` as `[BDM_ROUTINE_CONFIG:…]`):

```
stationCount, rotationMinutes, movementDirections,
observationPrompt, recordPrompt, sharePrompt, materials[]
```

`materials` and the whole teacher plan are stripped automatically before anything reaches students.

**Prompts that work** — specific and mathematical, from a live instance:

> move: "Move clockwise to the next posted design when the signal sounds. Leave markers at the poster and carry only your feedback strip."
> observe: "Verify one area calculation, one volume calculation, and the stated constraint before discussing style."
> record: "Record one calculation that is clearly defended and one precise question or possible revision."
> share: "Return to the home poster, read the evidence, and revise at least one label, calculation, unit, constraint check, or explanation."

Note the shape: **each round has a different job.** "Round 1: area evidence. Round 2: volume evidence. Round 3: constraints and communication." A gallery walk where every round asks the same question degenerates into three laps of the same glance.

**The rule that keeps it mathematical:** "Comment on the mathematics, not the artwork." Pair it with a revision requirement — "Teams choose one useful note and make one mathematical revision. If the poster is already correct, deepen it with a second example or sharper explanation" — so the walk produces a change, not just sticky notes.

**Failure modes with prepared guards:**
```
Decoration before proof :: Require every rubric element first.
One student owns the work :: Rotate roles and require individual explanation.
Feedback copied without reason :: Require the exact evidence behind the revision.
```
Plus the logistics that prevent chaos: materials staged by team before class, markers capped until the physical proof is approved, movement one-way and timed, posters stay at their stations, and an **individual receipt at the end** — team work without an individual product means you learn nothing about any one student.

**Roles:** Marker / Director / Checker, switch the marker at halfway, and every student must be able to explain the proof.

**Budget the movement.** A gallery walk needs a `transition-hustle` out and another back — 2 of your 50 minutes. Take those minutes from instruction, not from practice. And per the transition rule: only plan a `transition-hustle` when students physically change location; for a same-location change of prompt or materials use Settle 30s from the iPad Remote instead.

**One open conflict to flag, not resolve.** A Feature Tracker item ("New State for State bank-Gallery walk", status *Needs revision*) asks for a different design: 8 chunks of 30 seconds with a 10-second beeping countdown, four rotations, then a final 15 seconds to be seated. That contradicts every shipped default and every authored lesson (3–4 stations, 2–5 minutes). It's unbuilt. If a lesson wants the rapid-fire version, say so explicitly rather than silently splitting the difference.

## Discussion protocol

Six minutes, five phases, fixed. Don't improvise it — the rhythm is what gets 6th graders talking.

```
Think 1 · Write 1 · Discuss 2 · Revise 1 · Share 1
```

Writing before talking means the quiet students arrive with something to say. Revision, not agreement, is the goal. The share is 1–2 statements you overheard while circulating, elevated by name — so students learn that being listened to is the reward.

Sentence stems and vocabulary stay **visible on the support surface** for the whole state. A stem students can't see is a stem they don't use.

Good stems name the mathematical move, not just the social one. Compare the state-default floor —
```
I agree with ___ because...
I disagree because...
```
— with the lesson-specific version from M1.T1.L2-D1:
```
___ is a factor because ___ / ___ = ___.
Its partner is ___ because ___ x ___ = ___.
The factor list is complete because the next pair would...
Jordan's first error is... because...
The completed arch proves the full list is...
```
The second set makes a student say something mathematical or nothing at all. Write that kind.

A discussion can also critique a **correct but inefficient** method rather than an error — M1.T1.L2-D2 does this, and protects the first method explicitly: "Factor pairs are a strong meaning method. We are adding a second proof, not declaring this one bad." That framing matters; students who feel their method was insulted stop offering methods.

## The hook

Both ends of the day. The stated criteria, verbatim from the lesson template:

> - Use one student-readable situation connected to experiences students may recognize: school, sports, games, food, music, transportation, money, phones, pets, schedules, or community life.
> - The problem must be approachable enough for an estimate or prediction before instruction, but the day's learning must be needed to prove the answer.
> - Show the unresolved problem on the main projector from minute 0 while students complete the separate Google Form retrieval warm-up.
> - Tell students they are not expected to solve it yet. Keep the answer, method, learning intention, and success criterion hidden.
> - Default the independent exit to the same anchor problem when it can measure the selected success criterion cleanly. Otherwise use a closely matched transfer question, then return to and resolve the exact anchor during closeout.
> - The final payoff must name how the day's model, representation, or method proves the answer — not merely reveal the number.

**The two tests.** Approachable enough to *estimate* before instruction, impossible to *prove* without it. If a student can answer it with yesterday's math, it's the wrong question. If they can't even form a guess, it's not a hook, it's the lesson.

**Intriguing is a higher bar than valid.** A question can pass both tests and still be flat. What makes the good ones land:

- **A dare in the second sentence.** "You have 40 water balloons for a balloon fight — equal buckets, none left over. What are ALL the ways you could split them? *Could you prove you found every single one?*" The first sentence is the situation; the second is the challenge. Most flat hooks are missing the second sentence.
- **The answer is a thing you have to name before you can compute it.** The parking-lot hook works because 12 could mean sections or spaces, and the whole lesson is about which. "Do not hunt for an operation. First tell me what the answer counts." The hook attacks the day's actual misconception rather than merely motivating.
- **Stakes an 11-year-old recognizes.** Concert floors, water balloons, skate parks, parking lots, carnival lights. Two trains leaving a station is not a hook.
- **A stated deferral promise on the screen.** "You do not need to solve this yet. You will be able to answer it by the end of class." The screen makes a contract, and the payoff pays the contract.

**Wiring it — five touch points.** M1.T1.L2-D2 is the reference implementation:

1. **Warm-up, minute 0** — the puzzle sits on the main projector, unresolved, headed `PUZZLE OF THE DAY`, with the deferral promise. Teacher line: "The puzzle stays up while you warm up. Do not solve it yet — just notice what the answer will need to count."
2. **`launch`** — re-posed as its own state. READ → PREDICT → JUSTIFY, with scripted timing (1:30 silent think, 1:00 partner compare, 1:30 share) and seeded tempting wrong answers: "Would 6 sections work? Would 12?" "Why can the answer not be greater than 24?"
3. **When the concept is named** — interpreted back into the hook's context immediately. "Parking-lot meaning: 12 identical sections; 2 visitor spaces and 3 staff spaces in each. What does 12 count — not 2 and not 3?"
4. **The readiness gate** — one gate item *is* the hook, so the gate measures transfer rather than procedure.
5. **Exit plus a named payoff state** — the exit asks the anchor (or a close transfer), then `closeout` is titled "You Can Answer It Now" and names the proof: "At the start, this was a puzzle. Now you can prove the answer two ways." No new mathematics in the payoff state.

**Concealment is written as a rule, not a hope.** Every step before the reveal carries an explicit prohibition: "Do not reveal the learning intention, GCF, factor trees, 12, or the anchor answer."

**The failure mode to avoid.** M1.T1.L1-D1 has a genuinely good `Anchor Problem` — a concert venue floor, 6 rows by 28 squares, prove the two sections still sum to the whole — and it **never appears in any of the fifteen steps.** It sits in a property no student ever sees, the actual hook is an unrelated 10-second warm-up, and there is no payoff. An anchor that isn't wired into a `Main Display` and a payoff state does not exist. Writing the property is a third of the job.
