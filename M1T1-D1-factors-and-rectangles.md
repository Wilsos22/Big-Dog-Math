# Every Way to Make 24

**Unit opener, M1.T1. Replaces the old L1-D1.** 50 minutes.
Standard: 6.NS.B.4 (foundation). Tool: `/divisibility`.

**Learning intention.**
> I am learning how to find every factor pair of a number.

**Success criterion.**
> I can list all the factors of a number in sequential order.

The intention is the hunt. The criterion is the record. Pairs in one, factors in the other, is deliberate: the rectangles produce pairs, and GCF tomorrow needs the flat ordered list.

The word doing the work is EVERY. Completeness is the day's idea, and the stopping rule is what proves it.

---

## The two numbers, and why they are different

**24 is the STRUCTURE number.** Blocks, rectangles, the reveal, ordering, the rainbow. Kids know 24's pairs by recall and that is a FEATURE here — you do not want computation competing with the insight. They can hold the whole thing at once.

**48 is the METHOD number.** `3 x 16` and `6 x 8` are not facts a sixth grader has. Recall fails, so the divisibility rules become the only way in. That is the only condition under which a method is worth learning.

The original plan ran the tool on 24 and that was the flaw: it demonstrated a machine solving a problem they had already solved twenty minutes earlier. Nothing about that convinces anyone.

## Materials

- Whiteboards and markers, one per student

## Block by block

### 5-11 · All the ways to make 24

**Hook screen:**

```
                    24

     How many different ways can two numbers
              multiply to make 24?

              Is there a limit?
```

That last line is the hook. Not "find some" — *how many exist*. They cannot answer it at minute 5, cannot answer it at minute 12, and it stays open until the rainbow closes at minute 25. It also plants the learning intention without you reading it aloud.

**The hook cannot be a story, and that is deliberate.** The best moment in this lesson is minute 19, when two messy columns turn out to be the same information. Any hook that mentions rows, arrangements, or area tells them multiplication is rectangles BEFORE they discover it. The natural one you would reach for — "I have 24 desks, how many ways can I arrange them in equal rows" — is exactly the one that kills the reveal. Keep the hook numeric.

> "Three minutes. On your board — what **two numbers** multiply to make 24? Every pair you can think of. Go."

Say "two numbers" in the prompt. It costs one word and it keeps `2 x 3 x 4` off the board, which has no rectangle partner and would eat five minutes you do not have on day one.

Think, write, neighbor, revise, share. This is the routine's first outing — narrate the phases by name so they hear the structure.

At 10, take answers **and write them on the left in whatever order they give them to you.** Do not sort. Do not clean up. The mess is what makes minute 23 have a reason to exist.

### 11-19 · Build every rectangle

24 cubes to each pair.

> "Thirty seconds. Do not touch them. Think about what shapes you could make with exactly 24. ... Now build. Every rectangle you can, all different side lengths. Write the dimensions on your board for each one."

Three minutes build, one minute compare with a neighbor, two minutes more build. The compare is where the ones they missed show up.

At 17, collect dimensions **on the right, again in the order given.**

### 19-23 · The two columns

> "One minute. With your table — what do you notice?"

Walk and listen. Pick two students you overheard, by name, and call on them in the whole group.

**The connection you are steering toward:** multiplying is finding area. Do not say it. Wait for it. If nobody gets there by 22, point at one pair on the left and its partner on the right and ask what they have to do with each other.

**Then the commutativity moment.** Someone has 12 by 2 and someone has 2 by 12.

> "Same size?" — yes, easy
> "So is this one rectangle or two?"

Pick the build up and rotate it 90 degrees in front of them. Nothing added, nothing removed, just turned. That decides whether their list has four entries or eight, so make them say it.

### 23-26 · Organize, then arch

> "Think about the best way to organize all these ways to multiply. Then do it on your board."

You are hoping for numeric order. If you get something else that works, take it.

> "But wait — how do I know which two of these combine to 24?"

They will say draw a line. Draw the arch. Show the rainbow: outside in, 1 with 24, 2 with 12, 3 with 8, 4 with 6.

**NAME IT HERE.** You have not said the word yet and this is the moment:

> "These pairs are called **factor pairs**. Each number in them is a **factor** of 24."

Meaning first, vocabulary second. But the vocabulary has to actually land or they leave with a good experience and no name for it.

### 26-28 · 48 goes up

Wipe the SCREEN. Put **48** up. Nothing else.

The whiteboard still holds 24's list and rainbow here — leave it through the tool block, it is the reference for what the machine is about to do. **It comes down before the paper goes out.** See minute 44.

> "Same job. Every factor pair of 48. Go."

Let them flounder for sixty seconds. They will get `1 x 48`, `2 x 24`, maybe `4 x 12`. They will not get `3 x 16` or `6 x 8`, because those are not facts they have.

> "So what do we do when we cannot just know it? How do we attack this on purpose?"

Do not resolve it. This is the question the tool answers, and it now has teeth because 48 just beat them.

If you want the drama, name a monster rhetorically: *"What about 360? You going to guess your way through that?"* Ten seconds, creates the need, and the rules answer it at a size that works.

### 28-33 · The tool

Chromebooks up. `/divisibility`.

**Decide before you start: hands off until you say, or let them run and you catch up.** Say which out loud. Lockstep with no rule means your fast kids finish while your slow kids watch you instead of their screen.

**First, 24, in thirty seconds.** This is a calibration, not a lesson. "Watch — it gets the same four pairs we did." They see the machine agrees with what they already know, so they trust it. Then move on fast.

**Then 48, together.** This is the real run. Rule bank on the left, 48 travelling down it, factor family building on the right.

The run stops when d times d passes 48. **That stopping rule is the answer to minute 26** — it is how you know you are done. Say it out loud when it happens.

**Put the whole method on the board and leave it up.** It is three parts and right now they are scattered:

```
1. Test 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 with the rules
2. Stop when the number times itself passes N
3. Arch the pairs
```

### 33-37 · Reps

Four numbers left: 35, 36, 40, 42. Roughly a minute each.

**Watch 35 and 42.** Both hide a 7, and there is no rule for 7 on the rail — it turns up as the partner of a factor they did find. Somebody will ask where the rule for 7 is. That is either your best thirty seconds of the block or a dead end, depending on whether you saw it coming. The answer: you do not need a rule for every number, because factors arrive in pairs.

### 37-39 · Close

Back to the learning intention. Then the day's real question, which has been open since minute 5:

> "How do you know you found them all?"

Fist to five on the success criterion, not on the lesson.

**Do not add an application closer.** "Which arrangement would you pick" has no defensible answer, because a factor pair is only better than another when something constrains it — and nothing does yet. Tomorrow's constraint is "greatest, and shared with another number." That is GCF, and it is the first time in this unit one factor beats another for a mathematical reason. Let Day 2 open with why they needed them all.

### 39-44 · Readiness checks, digital

These gate the release to paper. Two items, both on the Chromebook.

**Check 1 — build every pair.** Number is **18**. Bank of 1 through 20. Click a number, it moves into the expression, click its partner, the row solidifies and a new one opens. Keep going until you cannot find any more, then submit.

Answer: 1 by 18, 2 by 9, 3 by 6.

18 is deliberate. The other numbers that fit under 20 — 16, 20 — can be solved by halving over and over without ever using the rule for 3. 18 forces it. Both 2 by 9 and 3 by 6 need them to see 18 is divisible by 3, and 9 is not a factor a sixth grader reaches for on reflex.

**Check 2 — find the impostor.** Number is **28**. Select the pair that is NOT correct.

- 1 x 28 — correct
- 2 x 14 — correct, and it tests whether they think 14 is too big to be a factor
- 4 x 7 — correct, and it brings the 7 back from the tool
- **3 x 9** — the answer. 27, off by one, so it catches the student who is close rather than lost

Make the stem unmissable. "Select the pair that is NOT correct" trips kids who have spent five years picking the right answer.

Check 1 lands on the criterion. Check 2 is diagnostic and does not. If your AP walks in during the checks, Check 1 is the one to be on.

### 44-50 · Paper assignment, collected at the door

It doubles as the exit ticket. You get digital data from the checks AND handwritten work you can actually read — and an unfinished sheet is information, not a hole.

**Three problems. Problems 1 and 2 are what you are grading. Problem 3 is a silent extension and you never say so.**

That gives a three-band sort off one sheet with no labels on it:

| What came back | What it means |
|---|---|
| 1 and 2 not finished | Needs help. Start here tomorrow |
| 1 and 2 done | Ready |
| Got into 3 | Kept going past the required work. **Motivation more than ability** — use it to seed tomorrow's share-out, not to group anybody |

**1. List all the factors of 30 in order. Draw the rainbow.**
1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30 — four pairs.
30 exercises the rules for 2, 3, 5 and 6. Not a teaching number (24, 48), not a rep number (35, 36, 40, 42). `5 x 6` is the pair they drop.

**2. List all the factors of 45 in order. Draw the rainbow.**
1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 45 — three pairs.
45 is odd, so the rules for 2 and 4 die on the first move. A student running the method handles that in two seconds; a student guessing stalls or invents `2 x 22.5`. Best discriminator on the sheet.

**3. List all the factors of 240 in order. Draw the rainbow.**
1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 16, 20, 24, 30, 40, 48, 60, 80, 120, 240. Ten pairs.

**Same wording, same size, same spacing as 1 and 2.** No badge, no star, no "challenge" label. The moment it looks special, a kid decides in advance whether it is for them, and the thing you are trying to see stops happening.

**Erase 24's list and rainbow before the paper goes out.** Not so they have to memorize it — so they attack 240 honestly, with the method, instead of copying an old answer off the wall.

**This is a method problem first.** They run the rules: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and their partners 240, 120, 80, 60, 48, 40. That is six of the ten pairs and it is real work.

**The noticing is supposed to happen DURING that, not before it.** Somewhere around testing 4 or 6, a student may realize the numbers they are testing are the same ones that worked for 24. That recognition emerging out of the work is the thing worth seeing — it is not a shortcut applied at the start, and a student who tried to shortcut it would skip the practice that makes it visible.

What you are looking for on the page is a margin note. "Same as 24." An arrow. "x10." Anything that says the connection surfaced.

**The method also runs out here, honestly.** 240 stops at 15 and the rail only reaches 6, so the rules deliver six pairs and then stall. That is not a design flaw, it is the Day 2 thread: the rules ran out, now what?

**Leave the three-part method up while they work.** Problem 3 is a method problem, so the method is the one thing that belongs on the wall. Erase the answer, keep the tool.

**Brute force genuinely cannot finish this**, which is what makes it an extension rather than just a longer problem. The stopping point for 240 is past 15 and the rail only goes to 6, so the method they learned today runs out. Only structure gets the rest. That is a good Day 2 thread on its own: the rules ran out, now what?

And the GCF seed survives. All of 24's list living inside 240 means the greatest factor they share is 24 itself — a clean opener tomorrow: *"Some of you noticed 24's whole list was hiding inside 240. What is the biggest thing those two numbers share?"*

**Calibration is what makes the unfinished sheet informative.** A ready student finishes 1 and 2 in about four minutes and starts 3. Four problems and nobody finishes, and "incomplete" only tells you the sheet was too long.

Say **"do what you can"** rather than "finish the sheet." It does not tip that 3 is extra, and it stops an anxious kid reading an unfinished page as failure.

**Leave the three-part method up while they work.** It is the artifact the research says to publish, and this is the block it exists for.

## The rule for 4 changed

**Old (in the tool right now):** the last two digits make a number divisible by 4.
**New:** half of the number is even. Cut it in half, and if the half is even, 4 works.

Three reasons:

1. **It composes.** 4 is 2 x 2, so the rule for 4 is the rule for 2 done twice. Same shape as the rule for 6, which is 2 and 3. Composite divisors decompose, and 4 and 6 stop being unrelated tricks.
2. **It generalizes free.** 8 is three halvings.
3. **The old rule is vacuous on every number in the bank.** 24, 35, 36, 40, 42, 48 are all two digits, so "check the last two digits" restates the question.

**Replace it, do not run both.** The rail is six rows read at a glance mid-task, and two rules on the hardest row invites "which one do I use" from the kid least able to afford the question. Keep last-two-digits in your pocket as the answer to *"what if the number is huge?"* — if a kid asks that, they earned it.

**The tool has not been changed yet.** Until it is, the left rail will contradict you.

## Three decisions, all made

**1. Do 4 x 6 and 6 x 4 count as one or two?**
One. Settled in the moment at minute 21 by rotating the 12 by 2. Make sure it holds at the learning check too.

**2. Does 1 x 24 count?**
Yes. Somebody will build a single row of 24 cubes; it is a rectangle and 1 is a factor. A few will feel it is cheating — be ready.

**3. What about 2 x 3 x 4?**
Ruled out in the prompt. Say "what two numbers multiply to make 24" at minute 5 and it never reaches the board. If someone writes one anyway, just reiterate: only two numbers. No discussion, no detour.

Worth knowing what you traded away — a three-factor product has no rectangle partner, it is a box, and "why does that one have no rectangle" is a real noticing. It is also a five-minute detour on a day that is already tight.

## Three things worth knowing

**The divisibility rules will not be mastered today.** They get about nine minutes for what is really its own lesson. That is survivable because GCF tomorrow factors TWO numbers instead of one — double the reps, arriving with a reason. Today they need to be *needed*, not fluent.

**The content is grade 4 until GCF arrives.** Listing factor pairs is 4.OA.B.4. What makes this a sixth grade day is the completeness argument, and none of it pays off until GCF gives it a purpose. **This day earns its place only if Day 2 reaches GCF fast.**

**Check 1 does not exist yet.** The click-a-number-into-a-pair interface is a new response kind end to end. For Monday, `Structured Numeric` holds the seat — six boxes, answers land in `poll_answers.values`, read them by hand for one day. Build the real one after you have watched it fail once.
