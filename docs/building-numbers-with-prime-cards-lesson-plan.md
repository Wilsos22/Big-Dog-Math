# Building Numbers with Prime Cards: See the GCF, Build the LCM

Status: Revised draft lesson plan. Not published to the live Math 6 database.

Recommended placement: A practice-and-connection lesson after students have learned GCF and LCM meanings separately in M1.T1.L2 and before the contextual strategy-choice work in M1.T1.L3. This lesson turns Carnegie's aligned prime-factor tables into physical prime-card builds. It is not the first introduction to both ideas.

Length: 50 minutes

Standard: 6.NS.B.4

Mathematical practices: MP1 and MP7

Lesson type: Practice and application with a concrete-to-representational-to-abstract bridge

## Instructional decision

Students build numbers by multiplying prime cards. Division is not the strategy.

- To find the GCF, students match identical prime cards one-to-one and read the greatest build shared by both numbers.
- To find the LCM, students start with one complete build and add only the unmatched prime cards needed to contain the other build.
- Students verify with multiplication: `8 = 4 x 2`, `12 = 4 x 3`, `24 = 8 x 3`, and `24 = 12 x 2`.
- The main examples keep both LCM input numbers at or below 12, as required by 6.NS.B.4.

The attached source contained one arithmetic error that is corrected here: `12 = 2 x 2 x 3`, not `2 x 2 x 2 x 3`.

## Template properties

### Lesson title

Building Numbers with Prime Cards: See the GCF, Build the LCM

### Learning intention

We are learning how prime-factor builds show what two numbers share and what a smallest combined build needs.

### Selected success criterion

I can build two numbers with prime cards and use the cards to justify their GCF and LCM.

### Essential ideas

- Every composite number can be built as a product of prime numbers.
- The GCF is the largest prime-card build contained in both number builds.
- The LCM is the smallest prime-card build that contains enough copies of every prime to make either original number.
- Matching prime cards count once in the LCM. Combining every card from both rows double-counts the shared primes.
- Prime-card construction makes the structure visible before symbolic exponent notation is introduced or used.

### Exact prerequisite

Students can:

- recognize common prime numbers;
- write or interpret a prime factorization;
- explain GCF as the greatest factor shared by two numbers; and
- explain LCM as the first positive multiple shared by two numbers.

If either meaning is new, split this into two days: shared builds for GCF first, then smallest containing builds for LCM.

### Carnegie anchor

Primary source: `M1.T1.L2 TIG`, Lesson 2, *Searching for Common Ground*.

- Activity 1, Prime Factors, teacher pages 14-15 (PDF pages 10-11): students decompose numbers into products of primes and check that the product rebuilds the number.
- Activity 2, Common Factors, teacher pages 16-17 (PDF pages 12-13): students align shared prime factors in the same columns and use those shared primes to determine the GCF.
- Activity 4, Using Prime Factors to Determine LCM, teacher pages 20-21 (PDF pages 16-17): students use the same aligned factor table to distinguish shared and non-shared prime copies, then multiply both sets to determine the LCM. Carnegie's first pair is 12 and 10.

Next-lesson connection: `M1.T1.L3 TIG`, Lesson 3, *Composing and Decomposing Numbers*, teacher pages 27-30 (PDF pages 7-10). Students move from calculating GCF and LCM to deciding whether a real-world situation involves equal sharing or coinciding cycles.

Adaptation: Replace Carnegie's static prime-factor table with physical prime cards placed in aligned rows. The mathematics remains the same: shared copies line up in the same columns; GCF uses the shared copies; LCM uses shared copies once plus every non-shared copy. The cards make prime multiplicity and double-counting visible before students record the table abstractly.

Number adaptation: Carnegie models the structure with 56 and 42, but this lesson keeps every LCM input number at or below 12 to honor the stated 6.NS.B.4 bound and keep the physical card load manageable. Carnegie's 12-and-10 pair is retained in the guided whiteboard cycle.

### Anchor problem

The Prime Card Factory can build 8 and 12.

What is the largest prime-card build that fits inside both numbers?

What is the smallest prime-card build that contains enough cards to make either number?

You are not expected to solve both questions yet. Make a prediction and look for something the two builds might share.

### Anchor answer

- `8 = 2 x 2 x 2`
- `12 = 2 x 2 x 3`
- The shared build is `2 x 2 = 4`, so the GCF is 4.
- Start with the complete build for 8 and add the unmatched 3 from the build for 12: `2 x 2 x 2 x 3 = 24`. The LCM is 24.
- Multiplication check: `8 = 4 x 2`, `12 = 4 x 3`, `24 = 8 x 3`, and `24 = 12 x 2`.

- One-to-one marks showing the shared prime copies
- A boxed GCF build and a boxed LCM build
- One written explanation using `shared` or `contains`
- Private Fist-to-Five, one diagnostic response, and one independent digital exit

### Discussion vocabulary

- prime factor: a prime number multiplied with other prime numbers to build a composite number
- shared: present in both builds
- match: pair one identical prime copy from each build
- contains: has enough copies of every needed prime card
- greatest common factor: the greatest factor contained in both numbers
- least common multiple: the least positive multiple that contains both number builds

### Discussion prompt

A student multiplied every card in the 8 row and every card in the 12 row and got 96 for the LCM. What did the student double-count, and how should the build be revised?

### Discussion stems

- I notice ___ appears in both builds.
- The shared build is ___ because ___.
- The smallest containing build needs ___ copies of ___ because ___.
- The student double-counted ___ when ___.
- I would revise the build by ___ because ___.
- I heard you say ___. I agree or disagree because ___.

### Mid-lesson check

Fist-to-Five statement: I can build two numbers with prime cards and use the cards to justify their GCF and LCM.

Diagnostic question: The prime-card builds are `6 = 2 x 3` and `10 = 2 x 5`. Which is the smallest build that contains both?

- A. `2`
- B. `2 x 2 x 3 x 5`
- C. `2 x 3 x 5`
- D. `3 x 5`

Correct answer: C, so the LCM is 30.

### Help path

1. Build each number as a multiplication of prime cards.
2. Line up identical prime cards one-to-one.
3. Record one copy from every matched pair in the Shared Build box.
4. Start the LCM row with one complete number build.
5. Add only unmatched cards from the other build.
6. Ask a partner one exact question or show the teacher the place where your build stops.

### Big Dog Challenge

Build 4 and 9. They share no prime cards. Explain why the empty shared-prime build represents a GCF of 1, and explain why the LCM is 36.

Keep this hidden until both required pairs are complete or the teacher assigns it.

### Exit ticket

The prime-card builds are for 8 and 10.

1. Write the prime factorization of each number.
2. State the GCF and name the shared prime-card build.
3. State the LCM and name the smallest containing prime-card build.
4. Complete one sentence: `The LCM is ___ because the build must contain ___.`

Answer:

- `8 = 2 x 2 x 2`
- `10 = 2 x 5`
- GCF: `2`
- LCM: `2 x 2 x 2 x 5 = 40`

### Retention questions for the next teaching day

1. `9 = 3 x 3` and `12 = 2 x 2 x 3`. What is the shared prime-card build and the GCF? Answer: `3`, so the GCF is 3.
2. `6 = 2 x 3` and `8 = 2 x 2 x 2`. What is the smallest containing build and the LCM? Answer: `2 x 2 x 2 x 3 = 24`.

### Misconception plans

- `wrong-factorization` :: Ask, `What multiplication sentence does your card row actually make?` Have the student multiply the cards before continuing.
- `matching-value-not-copy` :: Ask, `How many copies of 2 appear in both rows at the same time? Match them one-to-one.`
- `gcf-uses-every-card` :: Ask, `Which cards can stay if this build must fit inside both original rows?`
- `lcm-combines-all-cards` :: Ask, `Which cards were already supplied by the first row? Cross out the duplicate copies.`
- `gcf-lcm-swap` :: Ask, `Are we building something that fits inside both, or something large enough to contain both?`
- `gcf-one-confusion` :: Use 4 and 9 only after the core task. State that a product with no prime cards has value 1.

- Prepare a few optional labeled build mats and pre-built first rows that can be handed privately to individual students without moving seats or naming a support group.
- Open the Google Form warm-up, Big Dog lesson session, Main projector, Pace and Support projector, and exit ticket before students enter.
- Keep a large magnetic or projected card set ready for 8 and 12.
- Do not display the learning intention, success criterion, answers, GCF label, or LCM label during the warm-up.

## In-seat support plan

The park system is not used. Every student remains at the regular seat and sees the same required task. The two private readiness points create a teacher visit queue, not student groups or public route labels.

- Teacher first visits: privately hand over a labeled build mat or a pre-built first row. Ask the student to multiply the row, match shared copies, and name what is still missing. Fade the support for the second pair.
- Partner check: all students may use the same Builder and Checker routine with the assigned table partner. Partners verify the product of a row; they do not supply another student's build.
- Independent continuation: students who demonstrate the method continue the same two required pairs without extra repetitions. The hidden challenge appears only after the teacher checks both required pairs.
- No student changes location. No planned Hustle or transition state is needed.

## Screen design rules for this lesson

- Main projector: one large number pair or card row at a time. No full factor list, division problem, support information, or dense paragraph.
- Pace and Support: current steps, contextual timer, vocabulary, and one worked reference. Keep permanent thinking stems visible in the fixed support area.
- Student Chromebook: one action only. Use the Form, a calm holding screen, a private readiness control, or the exit ticket.
- Private teacher Remote: pacing, speaker notes, reveal rules, response data, the private visit queue, overrides, and transition controls for unplanned needs only.
- The prime cards and paper are the mathematical workspace. Screens direct and capture; they do not replace the build.
- Public scores, readiness results, support labels, and student names never appear on either projector.
