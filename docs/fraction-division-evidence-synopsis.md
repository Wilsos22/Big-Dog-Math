# Dividing Fractions and Converting Mixed Numbers: Evidence Synopsis for Math 6

Prepared for Steele Wilson, Big Dog Math. July 2026.
Scope: 6.NS.1 (divide fractions by fractions) plus 4.NF/5.NF mixed-number and improper-fraction
conversion, for a class range running from on grade level to roughly two years behind.

Purpose: a defensible menu of options. Every instructional claim below is tagged with the strength
of evidence behind it so that if a coach, admin, or MTSS team asks "why are you teaching it that
way," the answer is a citation and an evidence tier, not a preference.

---

## 0. How to read the evidence tiers

| Tag | Meaning | Source type |
|---|---|---|
| **[IES-Strong]** | What Works Clearinghouse rated the recommendation *Strong Evidence* | WWC practice guide |
| **[IES-Moderate]** | WWC rated the recommendation *Moderate Evidence* | WWC practice guide |
| **[IES-Minimal]** | WWC rated the recommendation *Minimal Evidence* (expert panel judgment) | WWC practice guide |
| **[RCT]** | Randomized controlled trial with reported effect sizes | Peer-reviewed journal |
| **[Meta]** | Meta-analysis or research synthesis | Peer-reviewed journal |
| **[Theory]** | Conceptual/design argument in a peer-reviewed venue, no causal test | Peer-reviewed journal |

Anything tagged **[Theory]** is defensible as a reasoned instructional choice but should not be
described as "research-proven." The honest limits section at the end names exactly where the
evidence runs out.

## 1. Why this unit deserves extra days

Siegler et al. (2012) analyzed nationally representative longitudinal datasets in the US and UK and
found that elementary students' **knowledge of fractions and of division uniquely predicted algebra
knowledge and overall math achievement in high school five to six years later** — after controlling
for whole-number arithmetic, general intellectual ability, working memory, and family income and
education. **[RCT-adjacent: longitudinal, not causal, but two independent national samples]**

Practical read: fraction division is not one topic among many. It is the topic that best forecasts
whether a student survives algebra. Spending 12–14 days instead of 7 is defensible on this citation
alone.

Second implication, and this one matters for the students who are behind: the predictor was
*fractions plus division together*. A student who is two years behind on fraction magnitude is not
behind on a sixth-grade skill; they are behind on the strongest single predictor of their algebra
outcome. Remediation inside this unit is higher-leverage than remediation anywhere else in the year.

## 2. The five decisions the research actually settles

These are the choice points where the evidence is clear enough to defend a specific option.

### Decision 1 — Make the number line the spine, not the pie

**Choose:** number line / linear (length) models as the primary representation, with area and set
models as secondary supports.

- IES *Developing Effective Fractions Instruction K–8*, Recommendation 2: help students recognize
  fractions are numbers that extend the number system, and **"use number lines as a central
  representational tool."** **[IES-Moderate]**
- IES *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics* (2021), Recommendation 4: use the number line
  to build understanding and prepare students for advanced mathematics. **[IES-Strong]**
- Barbieri, Rodrigues, Dyson, & Jordan (2020): 51 **sixth graders with mathematics difficulties**,
  randomly assigned, 27 lessons over 6 weeks, number-line-centered intervention. Effects at posttest:
  fraction concepts **g = 1.09**, number line estimation percent-absolute-error **g = −0.85**,
  magnitude comparison **g = 0.82**. Gains held at delayed posttest 7 weeks later. WWC rates this
  study *Meets Evidence Standards with Reservations*. **[RCT]**

This is the single best-supported decision in the whole unit, and the sixth-grade struggling-learner
study is almost exactly your population. If you change one thing, change this.

**What it does not mean:** area models are not banned. It means the number line is the representation
that never leaves the screen, and area models are brought in for specific jobs (see Decision 3).

### Decision 2 — Meaning and procedure iterate; do not finish one before starting the other

**Choose:** short cycles that alternate a sense-making move and a symbolic move, rather than a long
"conceptual unit" followed by an "algorithm unit."

- IES Fractions Recommendation 3: help students understand **why** procedures for computation with
  fractions make sense. **[IES-Moderate]**
- Rittle-Johnson, Schneider, & Star (2015), reviewing the empirical literature: conceptual and
  procedural knowledge develop **bidirectionally and iteratively** — gains in one produce gains in
  the other, in both directions. The iterative view is now the accepted position. Rittle-Johnson &
  Koedinger (2009) showed students in an *iterating* condition made fewer arithmetic errors and
  transferred better than students taught concepts-then-procedures. **[Meta / RCT]**

Practical read for your 55-minute spine: your existing C → R → A day already is the iteration, at the
scale of one class period. The mistake to avoid is stretching it across a week — "we will do models
Monday through Wednesday and the rule on Thursday" is the thing the research argues against.

### Decision 3 — Lead with measurement (quotative) division; teach partitive deliberately and later

**Choose:** open with *"how many groups of ___ fit inside ___?"* Introduce *"how much is in one whole
group?"* as a named second meaning about halfway through the unit.

Rationale: students arrive with a partitive ("fair share") schema from whole-number division, and
that schema **breaks** on fraction divisors — you cannot share cookies among 3/4 of a person. The
measurement interpretation survives the transfer intact, which is why it is the safe entry point.
Simultaneously, restricting instruction to measurement is a documented cause of later failure on
partitive fraction-division word problems, because students never build the second schema.
**[Theory, broadly agreed in the math-education literature]**

- The Common Core *6–8 Number System Progression* requires both: students must create story contexts
  for `(2/3) ÷ (3/4)` and use the multiplication/division relationship to explain the quotient.
- The partitive-fraction-division literature is explicit that haste toward invert-and-multiply
  starves the partitive meaning and produces intuitive errors.

**Model pairing that keeps this honest:**

| Meaning | Question form | Best model | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measurement / quotative | "How many 1/4-cups fit in 3 cups?" | Number line, fraction strips, ribbon | `3 ÷ 1/4 = 12` |
| Partitive | "3/4 of a pan weighs 6 lb. How much is a whole pan?" | Tape diagram / bar model | `6 ÷ 3/4 = 8` |

A large fraction of student confusion in this unit is **model–context mismatch**: an area model
dragged onto a measurement problem, or a "how many fit" count applied to a partitive stem. Name the
two question forms out loud and post them.

### Decision 4 — Route to invert-and-multiply through the common-denominator algorithm

**Choose:** `a/b ÷ c/d = (a/b ÷ c/d)` with like denominators first — `6/8 ÷ 3/8 = 6 ÷ 3 = 2` — then
generalize, then *derive* invert-and-multiply as the shortcut.

- The common-denominator algorithm rests on partitioning, unitizing, and counting, which students
  already have, and it requires only whole-number division once denominators match. It offers far
  more links to fraction meaning than invert-and-multiply, which offers almost none.
  (*An Alternative Route to Teaching Fraction Division: Abstraction of Common Denominator Algorithm*,
  International Electronic Journal of Elementary Education, 2015.) **[Theory]**
- IES Fractions Rec 3 supports "help students understand why procedures make sense," which is what
  this route delivers. **[IES-Moderate]**

**Be honest about the tier.** There is no RCT showing common-denominator-first beats
invert-and-multiply-first on sixth-grade outcomes. What you can defend is: (a) the route is
conceptually derivable by students, (b) it matches the standard's demand for explanation, and (c) it
gives students who are behind an on-ramp that only needs whole-number division. Do not claim more.

**Do still teach invert-and-multiply.** Students need the efficient algorithm for later work. Teach
it as the *thing you discover* on roughly day 10, not the thing you are given on day 1.

### Decision 5 — Concrete tools are a phase, not a home; fade them on stated criteria

**Choose:** manipulatives with explicit teacher guidance, then a deliberate fade through a
stripped-down drawing to symbols.

- Carbonneau, Marley, & Selig (2013), meta-analysis of 55 studies (N = 7,237, K–college): teaching
  with concrete manipulatives beat symbols-only instruction with **small-to-moderate** effects, but
  results were **moderated by instructional characteristics** — high instructional guidance and
  developmental fit mattered. Moderate-to-large effects on retention; small effects on problem
  solving, transfer, and justification. **[Meta]**
- Fyfe, McNeil, Son, & Goldstone (2014) systematic review, and McNeil & Fyfe (2012): **concreteness
  fading** — meaningful concrete form, then a faded intermediate representation that keeps the
  correspondence but strips perceptual detail, then symbols — outperformed staying concrete and
  outperformed starting abstract. **[Meta / RCT]**
- Ebner, MacDonald, Grekov, & Aspiranti (2025), meta-analysis of 30 single-case CRA studies:
  significant overall effect, and a **non-integrated CRA sequence** (distinct C, R, A phases) slightly
  outperformed integrated delivery. **[Meta — see caveat below]**

**Caveat you should state before anyone else does:** the CRA meta-analysis reports Tau-BC ≈ 0.997.
That is a nonoverlap index from single-case designs, not a Hedges' *g*, and values near 1.0 are
routine in that literature. It supports "CRA works for students with learning disabilities." It does
not support "CRA has an effect size of 1.0."

Net: your existing C → R → A spine is well-aligned, including the fact that your phases are distinct
rather than blended. The addition the research asks for is an **explicit fading criterion**, which is
in section 8.

## 4. Mixed numbers and improper fractions, specifically

This gets its own treatment because the standard classroom approach — "multiply the denominator by
the whole number and add the numerator" — is the clearest example in the sixth-grade curriculum of a
procedure that runs without meaning.

**What the research says goes wrong:**

- Many students hold fractions as *parts of a whole only*. That definition makes a number greater
  than one literally impossible to represent, so improper fractions read as errors rather than
  numbers. This is a direct consequence of an exclusively part-whole diet in grades 3–5.
- Teaching the conversion procedurally means only students who already have solid fraction,
  multiplication, and division understanding get anything beyond parroting. **[Theory]**
- Students **ignore the fractional part and operate on whole numbers only** when computing with mixed
  numbers — a documented, widespread error pattern.

**The evidence-aligned replacement, in order:**

1. **Iterate the unit fraction.** `5/4` means "1/4, five times." Build it physically (paper strips,
   Cuisenaire rods) before any symbol manipulation. This is IES Fractions Rec 2's "fractions are
   numbers" claim in its most concrete form. **[IES-Moderate]**
2. **One point, two names.** Put `7/4` and `1 3/4` on the same number line tick. The conversion is
   *renaming*, not *transforming*. This kills the "improper is wrong" belief directly.
3. **Re-covering with same-size pieces.** Cover `1 3/4` with one whole strip and three fourths, then
   re-cover the identical length using only fourths. Students count 7. The "multiply and add" rule is
   then *described* as the counting shortcut they just performed, in their own words, before it is
   written symbolically.
4. **Only then** the symbolic rule, introduced as a shortcut with a stated justification.

**Why this matters for division:** `2 1/2 ÷ 1/2` is trivially "how many halves in two and a half" if
mixed numbers are magnitudes on a line, and nearly impossible if they are two unrelated numbers
sitting next to each other. Mixed-number conversion is not a side topic in this unit; it is the
prerequisite that determines whether day 12 works.

**The remainder trap.** `13 ÷ 4 = 3 r 1` and `13/4 = 3 1/4` describe the same situation, and students
routinely write `3 r 1` as `3 1/4` in one context and `3 1/1` in another. The repair is to force the
unit statement every time: *"1 what?"* The leftover is one of the four pieces that make a group, so it
is `1/4` **of a group**, not `1/4` of the original whole. This same confusion reappears on day 8 with
fraction divisors and is worth pre-empting.

## 5. Misconception catalogue and pre-emption plan

These are the errors documented in the fraction literature and the ones that will actually appear on
your learning-checks. For each: what it sounds like, why it happens, what to do *before* it appears,
and the diagnostic item that exposes it.

| # | Misconception | Student voice | Root cause | Pre-emptive move | Diagnostic item |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Division makes smaller | "The answer can't be 12, I divided" | Whole-number bias; every prior division shrank the number | Day 5 estimation sort *before* any fraction-divisor computation | `3 ÷ 1/4` — is the answer bigger or smaller than 3? |
| 2 | Reversal of dividend and divisor | Computes `4 ÷ 1/2` when asked `1/2 ÷ 4` | Reads the phrase, not the structure; "divide the big one" | Always read aloud as "how many ___ fit in ___" with a gesture | Pair `1/2 ÷ 4` and `4 ÷ 1/2` back to back |
| 3 | Answering the leftover instead of the count | Says "1/2 left" when asked how many fit | Confuses the remainder with the quotient | Force the sentence "the answer is a COUNT of groups" | `7/2 ÷ 3/4` |
| 4 | Divide across without checking | `6/8 ÷ 3/4 = 2/2` | Overgeneralized from multiplication; sometimes accidentally right | Show it working, then failing, in the same lesson | `6/8 ÷ 3/4` then `5/8 ÷ 3/4` |
| 5 | Inverting the wrong fraction | `2/3 ÷ 3/4 = 3/2 × 3/4` | Rule memorized without the derivation | Derive on day 11 rather than give on day 1 | Any `÷` with both terms non-unit |
| 6 | Flipping during multiplication | Inverts inside a multiplication problem later in the year | Rule stored as "fractions and flipping" | Interleave `×` and `÷` items in warm-ups from day 11 forward | Mixed `×`/`÷` set |
| 7 | Whole-number bias on mixed numbers | `3 1/2 ÷ 1/2 = 3` then adds the half separately | Mixed number read as two numbers | Day 3 "one point, two names" | `2 1/2 ÷ 1/2` |
| 8 | Improper fractions are illegal | "You can't have 7/4" | Part-whole-only definition of fraction | Day 2 unit-fraction iteration past 1 | Place `9/4` on a number line to 3 |
| 9 | Remainder in the wrong unit | `13/4 = 3 1/13` or `3 1/1` | Referent unit lost | Always ask "1 what?" | `13 ÷ 4` in a context |
| 10 | Referent unit drift | "2/3 of what?" cannot be answered | The whole changes mid-problem and no one named it | Underline the whole in every stem, out loud | `2/3 ÷ 3/4` with a context |
| 11 | Model–context mismatch | Draws a pizza for a partitive stem | Only ever saw one model | Day 10 card sort | 4 stems, 2 of each type |
| 12 | Whole-number division unavailable | Stalls on `6 ÷ 3` inside `6/8 ÷ 3/8` | Fact fluency gap, unrelated to fractions | Warm-up Q1 fluency slot targeted at ÷ facts from day 5 | Any common-denominator item |

**How to prepare for these rather than react to them.** Two moves, both cheap:

1. **Pre-teach the estimate.** Before every computation set from day 5 onward, students commit to
   "more than 1 / less than 1 / more than the dividend." Misconceptions 1, 2, and 3 all become
   self-caught. This is the Rational Number Project finding in practical form: RNP students beat
   commercial-curriculum students specifically on **order and estimation** subscales (Cramer, Post, &
   delMas, 2002; 1,600+ students, 66 classrooms randomly assigned). **[RCT]**
2. **Plant the error on purpose.** On practice days, the error-analysis specimens are the exact wrong
   answers from yesterday's learning-check, de-identified. Your privacy rule already requires this;
   it is also the higher-quality instructional choice, because the errors are live rather than
   hypothetical.

**System note.** Misconception tags in your Supabase `misconceptions` table are a finite exact-match
vocabulary and adding one is a SQL migration. Before this unit ships, check the twelve above against
the existing 18 tags. Any that is missing will silently fail to cluster and will render a blank
prepared move in `/teacher/rightnow`.

## 6. Concrete activities (the C phase)

Every activity below is designed for 6–8 minutes, pairs or table groups, computers open, board flat
on the keyboard deck, manipulative in the space between partners — your existing room logistics with
no extra transition. Each ends in something written.

### 6.1 The ribbon cut (day 4) — measurement division, whole ÷ unit fraction

- **Materials:** 3-foot ribbon or adding-machine tape per pair, scissors, a 1/4-foot paper ruler.
- **Prompt:** "Cut this into pieces that are each 1/4 of a foot. How many pieces do you get? Do not
  measure with a ruler with numbers on it — use the 1/4 strip."
- **Listen for:** counting *pieces* versus counting *cuts* (off-by-one), and the moment someone says
  "wait, we got more pieces than we started with feet."
- **Written product:** "We got ___ pieces because ___ groups of 1/4 fit inside 3."
- **Why it works:** it is the measurement interpretation with a physical count, and it directly
  attacks misconception 1 through surprise rather than through being told.

### 6.2 Same-size pieces (day 7) — common denominator

- **Materials:** fraction strip sets (halves through twelfths).
- **Prompt:** "How many 3/8s fit in 6/8? Now how many 3/4s fit in 6/8? What made the second one
  harder?"
- **Listen for:** "we need the same size pieces." That sentence *is* the common-denominator algorithm.
- **Written product:** the student's own sentence for why matching denominators helps, verbatim,
  which you then reuse word-for-word in the R-phase demo. (Your "insight with no work" discussion
  move, used exactly as designed.)

### 6.3 The scoop task (day 8) — remainders with meaning

- **Materials:** dry rice or beans, a 1/3-cup scoop, a container holding 2 1/2 cups.
- **Prompt:** "How many full 1/3-cup scoops? What is left, and what fraction of a *scoop* is it?"
- **Listen for:** students naming the leftover as a fraction of the cup instead of a fraction of the
  scoop. This is misconception 9 in its native habitat.
- **Written product:** two sentences — the count, and the leftover with its unit named.

### 6.4 Paper folding (day 2) — unit fraction iteration

- **Materials:** identical paper strips, 4 per student.
- **Prompt:** "Fold to make fourths. Now build 5/4 using these strips. You will need more than one
  strip. What do you call the number you built?"
- **Listen for:** "that's more than one whole" — and whether anyone says it cannot be done.
- **Written product:** `5/4 = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4` and the mixed-number name.

### 6.5 Human number line (day 1) — magnitude

- **Materials:** rope or clothesline across the room, clothespins, index cards with fractions.
- **Prompt:** "Place your card where it belongs. You may move one other person's card if you can say
  why."
- **Listen for:** denominator-size reasoning, and whether anyone uses 1/2 as a benchmark unprompted.
- **Written product:** each student sketches the final line and writes one comparison with a because.
- **Note:** this one costs a `transition-hustle` out and a `transition-reset` back. Budget the two
  minutes from `review` on day 1, which you are skipping anyway.

### 6.6 Card sort (day 10) — measurement vs partitive

- **Materials:** 12 pre-cut word-problem stems, no numbers computed.
- **Prompt:** "Sort into 'how many groups fit' and 'how much is in one group.' You may not solve
  them."
- **Listen for:** the stems students argue about — those are the ones to use on the assessment.
- **Written product:** the sort, plus one sentence naming the tell they used.

### 6.7 Cuisenaire relabeling (optional, powerful for level C)

- **Materials:** Cuisenaire rods.
- **Prompt:** "If the dark green rod is 1, what is the light green rod? Now if the brown rod is 1,
  what is light green?"
- **Why:** it directly trains the referent-unit flexibility that misconception 10 depends on, and it
  requires no fraction notation at all — which makes it accessible to a student who is two years
  behind on notation but not on reasoning.

## 7. The representational layer (the R phase)

Per your spine, R is normally a website tool: teacher demos one problem, then students run a stated
number of reps.

**Existing routes that map cleanly:**

- `/number-line-plus` — days 1, 2, 4, 5, 6. This is the workhorse and it is the route the Barbieri

- `/area-model` (the `box method` alias) — useful on day 9 for partitive tape diagrams, less useful

**Rep counts.** The IES 2021 guide's systematic-instruction recommendation supports stating the
number of practice reps explicitly rather than "work until time." **[IES-Strong]** Six to eight reps
per R phase, with the first one demoed, is consistent with your existing spine.

## 8. Fading from scaffolded to abstract

The research supports fading; it does not support fading on a calendar. Fade on evidence.

**The three-touch rule.** A student moves from C to R when they can produce the correct count with
the manipulative on three consecutive items *and* state the count in the sentence frame without
prompting. From R to A when they can solve two consecutive items with the drawing and then one with
the drawing hidden.

**The fading ladder for this unit specifically:**

| Stage | What the student holds | What they say | Sixth-grade example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C — concrete** | Strips, ribbon, scoops | "Four of these fit in one of those" | Physically fits 1/4 strips into 3 |
| **R1 — faithful drawing** | Their own drawn number line with every tick | "I marked every fourth and counted 12" | Draws and counts |
| **R2 — faded drawing** | A stripped diagram: one bar, tick marks only where needed | "Twelve fourths in three" | Bar with labels, no shading |
| **A1 — symbols with a spoken justification** | Notation, plus the sentence out loud | "3 ÷ 1/4 = 12 because twelve fourths make three" | Written equation + oral because |
| **A2 — symbols alone** | Notation | — | `3 ÷ 1/4 = 12` |

R2 is the stage most classrooms skip and the one the concreteness-fading research says carries the
transfer (Fyfe et al., 2014; McNeil & Fyfe, 2012). **[Meta / RCT]** Budget a full day's R phase for
it around day 6.

**The verbal bridge is not optional.** IES 2021 Recommendation 2 — teach clear and concise
mathematical language and support students' use of it — is rated **Strong**. The 2026 synthesis of 39
middle-school fraction interventions found explicit instruction and multiple representations were the
most consistently embedded practices, **often paired with verbalization**. **[Meta]** Concretely: no
student moves to A2 without having said the because-sentence aloud to a partner at least once.

**Two things to withdraw and one to keep.**

- Withdraw: the manipulative, and the fully-ticked number line.
- Withdraw: the sentence frame printed on the page (move it to the wall).
- **Keep permanently:** the estimate-first move. It is not a scaffold, it is a habit, and the RNP
  results suggest estimation is a durable outcome worth protecting rather than a crutch to remove.

## 9. Differentiation plans

Four plans. The unifying constraint, which is also your standing rule: **support changes the path, not
the required work.** Every level produces the identical product. No student sees a tier, a route
meaning, or a group name that reveals ability.

### Level A — on grade level or above

- **Entry:** day 1, full pace.
- **Compress:** days 1–3 can run at half depth if the readiness gate is clean. Reclaim the time on
  days 9–13.
- **Extend, do not accelerate:** give harder *reasoning*, not the seventh-grade standard. Good
  extensions — (a) write a context for a given quotient, which is strictly harder than solving;
  (b) find the error in a plausible wrong solution; (c) "when does dividing across the top and bottom
  work, and why?" (misconception 4 turned into an investigation); (d) `a/b ÷ c/d` with letters.
- **Success criteria:** solves both meanings, chooses an efficient method, justifies the choice.
- **Watch for:** fast-and-fluent students who cannot produce a model. They will hit a wall in day 9
  and again in seventh-grade proportional reasoning. Require the model from them too.

### Level B — approximately one year behind

Profile: fraction magnitude is shaky, part-whole is solid, whole-number division is fluent.

- **Entry:** day 1, full sequence. Do not skip anything.
- **Prerequisite repair, embedded:** benchmark comparison to 0, 1/2, 1 — five minutes inside the
  `review` state on days 1, 2, and 4. Nothing pulled out.
- **Number choice:** keep denominators friendly and related — halves, fourths, eighths — through day
  8. Introduce unrelated denominators (thirds vs fourths) only on day 9 and after.
- **Method:** stay on the common-denominator algorithm as the primary method through day 12. This
  student can succeed on it because whole-number division is available. Invert-and-multiply is
  presented but is not the required method until day 13.
- **Scaffold:** the faded R2 diagram stays available one week longer than for level A.
- **Success criteria:** identical assessment, identical items. Path differs, product does not.

### Level C — approximately two years behind

Profile: fractions understood as part-whole only, weak magnitude, multiplication and division facts
not automatic, likely holds misconceptions 1, 8, and 12 simultaneously.

This is the level where the research is most specific and most encouraging. Barbieri et al. (2020)
ran a number-line-centered intervention with exactly this profile of sixth graders and got **g = 1.09**
on fraction concepts, holding seven weeks later. **[RCT]** The design features worth copying:

- **Number line as the only spine.** Not one representation among several — the organizing one.
- **Small group, high dose.** Their dose was 27 lessons over 6 weeks. In your structure that is the
  `small-group` block (38–51) every day of the unit, not occasionally.
- **Explicit strategy instruction folded in**, not just content.
- **Attention buffering:** their exploratory analysis found the intervention *reduced* the usual
  penalty for low attentive behavior. Relevant if this group overlaps with your attention concerns.

Plan specifics:

- **Entry:** day 1, but with days 1–3 taught to mastery even if that costs two extra days. The
  magnitude work is the intervention; skipping it to "get to the standard" removes the mechanism the
  effect came from.
- **Prerequisite repair:** division facts in the warm-up Q1 fluency slot, every day of the unit. IES
  2021 Recommendation 6 (regular timed activities to build fluency) is rated **Strong** —
  but keep timing on *facts*, never on fraction-division problem solving, where time pressure
  degrades reasoning.
- **Number choice:** unit fractions as divisors through day 8. Halves, fourths, thirds only.
- **Method:** common-denominator algorithm only. Do not require invert-and-multiply on the
  assessment; require correct answers with a defensible method. Introduce invert-and-multiply on day
  11 as "here is the shortcut you will see later," with no accountability attached.
- **Concrete access:** strips remain available through day 12. Fade the *drawing*, not the object,
  last.
- **Language support:** sentence frames stay on the desk, not just the wall. IES 2021 Rec 2,
  **Strong**.
- **Success criteria:** identical product. Reduce the number of items if needed, never the type.

### Level D — significant gap (whole-number division meaning absent)

Profile: cannot reliably explain what `12 ÷ 3` means, or cannot produce it. Rare but real, and it
makes every fraction-division representation illegible.

- **Entry:** day 1 with the class, plus a parallel three-session repair inside `small-group` on days
  1–3 covering measurement division of whole numbers only: "how many 3s in 12," physically, then on a
  number line, then symbolically. Same activities as day 4, whole numbers only.
- **Then merge at day 4.** The measurement structure they just built is precisely the structure day 4
  needs, so the merge is natural rather than remedial.
- **Method:** measurement counting on a number line, indefinitely. A student who can reliably answer
  "how many 1/4s in 3" by counting has met 6.NS.1's core demand.
- **Flag for MTSS** if the day-1–3 repair does not take. That is a Tier 3 conversation, and this doc
  is your documentation of the Tier 1 and Tier 2 supports already attempted.

### The routing mechanic

Your learning-check at 35–38 is two problems, and answers set the private routes. For this unit the
two items should be chosen to separate *magnitude* from *procedure*, because those failures need
different routes:

- **Item 1 (magnitude):** "Is `3 ÷ 2/3` more or less than 3? Explain in one sentence."
- **Item 2 (procedure):** one computation at the day's level.

| Item 1 | Item 2 | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Correct | Correct | Extension: write a context for the quotient |
| Correct | Wrong | Procedure repair — the algorithm, on the tool |
| Wrong | Correct | **Highest priority.** Magnitude repair — number line. This student is running a rule they cannot check. |
| Wrong | Wrong | Teacher table, back to the concrete task |

The wrong/correct row is the one most likely to be missed by a teacher watching answers alone, and it
is the profile most predictive of collapse in seventh grade.

## 10. Assessment and progress monitoring

- **Daily:** learning-check at 35–38, routing as above. Fist-to-Five at 34–35 before graded items,
  per your fixed spine.
- **Weekly progress monitor for levels C and D:** a 20-item fraction **number line estimation** task
  scored as percent absolute error. This is the measure Barbieri et al. moved (g = −0.85) and it
  detects growth earlier than computation accuracy does, because magnitude improves before procedure
  stabilizes.
- **Fluency:** warm-up Q1 only, timed, division facts. IES 2021 Rec 6, **Strong**.
- **Unit assessment:** must include (a) both meanings, (b) at least one item requiring a model, (c) at
  least one item requiring a *context to be written*, and (d) one estimation item. Item type (c) is
  the one that cannot be passed by a memorized rule.

**Retention slots.** Your warm-up format pulls Q4–Q5 from the previous taught day's retention fields
and only pulls backward. For this unit, set `Retention Q4` / `Retention Q5` deliberately so that
magnitude items keep recurring after the unit ends — fraction magnitude decays, and it is the thing
seventh grade will need.

## 11. Honest limits

State these before someone else finds them.

1. **No RCT tests the algorithm order.** Common-denominator-before-invert-and-multiply is a
   theoretically strong choice with no causal evidence behind that specific comparison at sixth
   grade. It is defensible as *aligned with* IES Rec 3, not as *proven by* it.
2. **The CRA meta-analytic effect size is not comparable to a Hedges' g.** Tau-BC ≈ 0.997 comes from
   single-case designs. Cite CRA as effective; do not cite that number as a magnitude.
3. **Manipulatives are not automatically good.** Carbonneau et al. found the effect is moderated by
   instructional guidance and by developmental fit, and effects on transfer and justification were
   *small*. Unguided manipulative time is a documented way to spend a period and change nothing.
4. **Fuchs's fraction-intervention RCTs were fourth grade.** The design principles transfer well and
   the sixth-grade Barbieri study corroborates them, but do not describe Fraction Face-Off as a
   sixth-grade-validated program.
5. **The IES fractions guide is from 2010** and its strongest relevant recommendations are rated
   *Moderate*, not *Strong*. The 2021 struggling-learners guide is newer and all six recommendations
   are *Strong*, but it covers K–6 intervention generally, not fraction division specifically.
   Together they are strong; individually each has a gap.
6. **Barbieri et al. (2020) had N = 51** and is rated by WWC as *Meets Evidence Standards with
   Reservations*. It is the best-matched study available, not a definitive one.
